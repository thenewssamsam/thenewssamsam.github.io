    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Expiring Agreement</h2>
    <p>The preliminary framework agreement signed back in June established a 60-day negotiating window aimed at reaching a permanent end to military action and outlining the future of Iran's nuclear program. However, as that deadline looms, the White House has remained silent on whether an extension will be granted.</p>
    
    <p>In the months since the deal was struck, both Washington and Tehran have accused each other of persistent violations. The primary flashpoint has become the Strait of Hormuz—the vital maritime artery through which a massive share of the world's oil is transported. While the waterway was completely open before the current war, vessels now routinely come under fire as both nations claim authority over the passage.</p>
    
    <p>Tensions reached a fever pitch recently when high-level rhetoric escalated. Following threats regarding the territory of the Strait, Iranian officials quickly fired back, signaling that neither warnings nor aircraft carriers would easily dictate terms in the region. Meanwhile, military repositioning continues: the U.S. Central Command released photos of the USS George Washington moving into the Middle East from the western Pacific, relieving the USS Abraham Lincoln amid ongoing domestic scrutiny over conditions aboard naval deployments.</p>

    <h2 id="historical-parallel">A Historical Parallel: Choke Points and Crises</h2>
    <p>To understand the high-stakes chess match playing out in the Strait of Hormuz today, it helps to look at past geopolitical flashpoints where control of narrow waterways became the epicenter of global anxiety.</p>
    
    <p>Consider the 1956 Suez Crisis, when the Egyptian government nationalized the Suez Canal, cutting off a crucial global trade and oil transit route. Much like the current standoff over Hormuz, the closure of a vital maritime artery instantly transformed a regional political dispute into an international economic emergency. Major powers scrambled to secure transit rights, demonstrating that nations will risk military escalation rather than allow strategic shipping lanes to fall under adversarial control.</p>
    
    <p>Another striking parallel is found in the tanker wars of the 1980s during the Iran-Iraq War, when commercial vessels navigating the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz were routinely targeted. Back then, as today, the weaponization of commercial shipping created a tense game of escort missions, naval posturing, and retaliatory strikes. History shows that whenever a critical maritime choke point is contested, diplomacy becomes extraordinarily fragile because neither side can afford to project weakness without risking economic and strategic isolation.</p>

Internationally, however, it hardens negotiating positions to the point where backing down feels politically impossible. This dynamic creates a dangerous feedback loop where every tweet, carrier deployment, and verbal counter-punch pushes the two nations further away from the negotiating table.</p>
