  <section class="ncg-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the Texas-Minnesota Extradition Clash?</h2>
    <p>A federal judge handed Minnesota state officials a notable setback in their ongoing effort to keep Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agent Christian Castro behind bars. U.S. District Judge Fernando Rodriguez Jr. denied an emergency request for a temporary restraining order that would have blocked Castro's release from the Cameron County Jail in Texas.</p>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      "The court was right to reject Governor Walz’s absurd claim... The Constitution requires deliberate consideration of extradition requests." — Andrew Mahaleris, Texas Governor's Office
    </div>

    <p>The dispute centers on criminal charges filed against Castro in Minnesota following a January enforcement operation in Minneapolis. Prosecutors allege Castro fired a shot through a closed front door, injuring a person in the leg, and subsequently charged him with multiple felony counts of second-degree assault. When Castro returned to Texas under federal orders, Minnesota officials sought his extradition. However, Texas Governor Greg Abbott's office launched an investigation to review the rendition warrant, leading to a standstill as Castro's 90-day detention under Texas law neared its expiration.</p>

    <h2>A Legal Rights Explainer: Understanding Extradition and Federal Authority</h2>
    <p>This high-profile interstate conflict provides a clear window into how the U.S. Constitution and statutory law handle criminal extradition between sovereign states. At its core, the legal battle tests the boundaries of the Extradition Clause and the Uniform Criminal Extradition Act, which govern how governors must respond when a sister state requests the return of a fugitive.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">The Role of the Executive Branch in Extradition</h3>
    <p>Under established constitutional principles, a state governor receiving an extradition demand has a duty to review the paperwork for basic legal sufficiency. However, federal courts have historically granted executives broad discretion regarding the timeline and thoroughness of that review. Judge Rodriguez noted that neither federal nor state extradition laws impose a hard deadline for an executive to complete an investigation into whether a requested individual qualifies as a fugitive under the law.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">The Supremacy Clause and Law Enforcement Immunity</h3>
    <p>Beyond standard interstate extradition, this case touches upon the delicate interplay between state criminal jurisdiction and federal law enforcement immunity. Federal agents operating within official duties often claim Supremacy Clause immunity, arguing that states cannot prosecute federal officers for actions taken pursuant to valid federal directives. Minnesota prosecutors contend that Castro's alleged actions fell well outside legitimate law enforcement duties, while Department of Homeland Security officials have characterized the state prosecutions as politically motivated stunts.</p>
