<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The 630-Missile Warning</h2>
<p>Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy delivered a stark warning regarding Russia's military capabilities, noting that Moscow currently maintains a stockpile of 600 to 630 high-speed ballistic missiles—some of its most potent and difficult-to-intercept weaponry. According to reports from state-owned media outlet Ukrinform, the Kremlin is actively scaling up production with the goal of manufacturing more than 100 of these weapons per month, potentially pushing annual capacity toward 1,300 units as colder weather approaches.</p>

<p>This surge in projected manufacturing arrives at a perilous juncture for Kyiv. While Ukraine has successfully adapted to intercepting incoming cruise missiles using fighter jets alongside advanced Western air defense systems like the IRIS-T and Hawk platforms, ballistic missiles represent an entirely different category of threat. Their immense speed and terminal trajectories leave remarkably narrow reaction windows.</p>

<h2>The Economic and Strategic Strain of Interceptors</h2>
<p>A crucial historical parallel lies in the asymmetrical economics of defense. In military history, defensive measures are historically more expensive and difficult to produce than offensive counterparts. A ballistic missile, while technologically sophisticated, is designed for serial destruction; an interceptor missile must possess guidance systems precise enough to collide with or detonate near a high-speed projectile in mid-air.</p>
