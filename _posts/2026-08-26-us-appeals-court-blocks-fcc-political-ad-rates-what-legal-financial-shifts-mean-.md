  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 2.5rem;">
    <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: 700; color: #2d3748; border-bottom: 1px solid #edf2f7; padding-bottom: 0.5rem; margin-bottom: 1rem;">What Happened: The Court Intervenes</h2>
    <p>With control of Congress hanging in the balance and billions of dollars poised to flood into media markets, the legal and regulatory rules governing campaign advertising are shifting in real-time. On Tuesday, a three-judge panel of the Richmond, Virginia-based 4th Circuit U.S. Court of Appeals issued a 2-1 decision blocking a Federal Communications Commission (FCC) move that would have given political party and joint fundraising committees access to the lowest unit charges for broadcast television advertising—rates traditionally reserved strictly for individual candidates.</p>
    
    <p>The Republican-controlled FCC had advanced the plan ahead of the November midterm elections. However, Democratic commissioners, alongside a coalition of Democratic congressional candidates who filed suit—including Senator Jon Ossoff of Georgia, Representative Kristen McDonald Rivet of Michigan, and Senate candidates Sherrod Brown of Ohio and Roy Cooper of North Carolina—argued that the rule change would trigger a massive influx of coordinated party money into local broadcast spaces.</p>
    
    <p>AdImpact, an advertising intelligence firm tracking the cycle, projects that total 2026 election spending will hit $11.6 billion, with a staggering $5.6 billion dedicated purely to broadcast television. With the lowest-cost advertising window for qualified political candidates scheduled to open on September 4, the court's intervention lands as a major tactical and financial moment for campaigns nationwide.</p>

    <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: 700; color: #2d3748; border-bottom: 1px solid #edf2f7; padding-bottom: 0.5rem; margin-bottom: 1rem;">A Legal Rights Perspective on Statutory Interpretation</h2>
    <p>To understand why this ruling matters, we have to look past the political headlines and examine the underlying mechanics of administrative law and statutory interpretation. The core of the 4th Circuit's 2-1 majority opinion rests on a strict reading of federal campaign finance statutes enacted by Congress. The court determined that the law is clear: statutory provisions granting the lowest unit charge—the cheapest advertising rate a broadcast station offers during a specific pre-election window—apply only to candidates themselves, not to party committees or joint fundraising vehicles that include non-candidate participants.</p>
