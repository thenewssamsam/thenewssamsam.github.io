  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened on I-65 South</h2>
    <p>On a Tuesday just before noon, the rhythm of a normal workday along Alabama's busy corridors shattered in an instant. Near the Shelby County Airport around exit 234, an ordinary commute transformed into a terrifying scene when a semi-truck transporting an excavator struck the County Road 87 overpass. The impact triggered a partial bridge collapse, sending massive chunks of debris and crumbled pavement cascading onto the interstate lanes below.</p>
    
    <div style="border-left: 4px solid #7c3aed; background: #f5f3ff; padding: 15px; margin: 20px 0; border-radius: 4px;">
      <strong>Quick Take:</strong> A routine transport error led to a catastrophic highway failure on I-65 South, highlighting how fragile our daily infrastructure and sense of safety truly are.
    </div>

    <p>According to local officials in Calera, the falling debris struck the windshield of an oncoming vehicle, leaving one person in critical condition. Two other occupants in a separate damaged vehicle sustained injuries as emergency crews rushed to secure the scene. The incident forced the complete closure of I-65 South for seven hours as Department of Transportation officials evaluated the structural integrity of the overpass, clearing heavy rubble, and managing massive traffic backups. While southbound lanes reopened later that evening, the damaged bridge section remains closed for upcoming repairs.</p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Sudden Crisis</h2>
    <p>When an unexpected catastrophe strikes—such as tons of concrete suddenly falling onto a highway—our minds undergo an immediate, violent shift. Human beings are creatures of habit who rely on a deeply ingrained sense of predictability. We drive the same stretches of highway, pass beneath the same overpasses, and operate under the comforting assumption that our physical environment is stable.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias in the Face of Danger</h3>
    <p>Psychologists call the initial mental hurdle in these situations <strong>normalcy bias</strong>. When the extraordinary happens, our brains instinctively try to normalize it. In the first fraction of a second after debris begins to fall, drivers do not immediately think, "A bridge is collapsing." Instead, the mind searches for familiar explanations: road debris kicked up by a truck, a loud tire blowout, or construction shifting. This cognitive delay, while natural, underscores how ill-equipped our everyday mental machinery is for instantaneous catastrophe.</p>

    <h3>Loss Aversion and the Illusion of Control</h3>
    <p>Events like the Shelby County incident also trigger profound anxiety regarding control and vulnerability. Modern drivers trade hyper-vigilance for convenience, trusting engineering standards, transport regulations, and fellow motorists. When that trust is breached by an oversized load striking an overpass, it triggers a spike in collective vulnerability. Drivers suddenly realize that safety is not guaranteed by their own skill behind the wheel, but by the collective compliance and attentiveness of thousands of strangers.</p>
