<h2 id="what-happened">What Is the "Drip-Drip" US Debt Crisis?</h2>
<p>While sudden fiscal cliffs or government shutdowns grab immediate television headlines, the more insidious threat to the US economy is the gradual, compounding accumulation of sovereign debt. According to economic reports, federal borrowing continues to climb due to structural deficits—meaning the government routinely spends significantly more money than it takes in through tax revenues.</p>

<p>Unlike a sudden market crash, a "drip-drip" crisis does not trigger a dramatic single-day collapse. Instead, it slowly drains fiscal flexibility. As total debt mounts, the federal government must allocate a larger percentage of its annual budget simply to pay interest on past borrowing. This interest burden crowds out funding for infrastructure, education, and discretionary investments, while steadily increasing the long-term risk of inflation and currency devaluation.</p>

<h3>Higher Interest Rates for Longer</h3>
<p>When the government floods the market with Treasury bonds to finance its borrowing, it must offer attractive yields to entice investors. This dynamic exerts upward pressure on benchmark interest rates. For consumers, this translates directly into higher costs for mortgages, auto loans, credit cards, and small business financing.</p>

<h3>The Inflation Tax</h3>
<p>When mounting debt becomes increasingly difficult to service through conventional taxation or spending cuts, governments sometimes face immense temptation to rely on monetary expansion. Over time, expanding the money supply dilutes purchasing power, acting as a hidden tax on savers and wage earners through persistent inflation.</p>
