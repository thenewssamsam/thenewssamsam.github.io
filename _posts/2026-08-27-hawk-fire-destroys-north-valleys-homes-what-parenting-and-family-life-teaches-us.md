<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Hawk Fire Devastation</h2>
<p>The <strong>Hawk Fire</strong> left a brutal trail of destruction across the North Valleys, reducing residential neighborhoods to ash and forcing families into a frantic, unexpected evacuation. For many residents, leaving home felt like a temporary precaution. Hilary and Luke Opperman loaded their children, pets, and livestock under the assumption they would return in a few days. Watching the flames crest the nearby hills, Hilary recalled believing their neighborhood was safe—until the fire moved within 100 feet of their property.</p>
<p>Within minutes, three decades of family history vanished. The home where the Oppermans raised their children and approached a mortgage-free future was gone, leaving behind only a lone stone fireplace standing sentinel amid the ruins. Miles away, lifelong Nevadan Ricardo Arrate faced an equally devastating reality. He had spent more than twenty years building his mountaintop home entirely by hand—every nail, beam, and design choice reflecting his own sweat and dedication. Despite believing firefighters could hold the line, the blaze overwhelmed their efforts, consuming irreplaceable mementos like his son's state-placing wrestling photographs.</p>
<p>Yet, amid the smoke and sorrow, both families expressed a quiet resolve. "We’ll rebuild. We’ll make it our home again," Hilary Opperman shared, pointing to the overwhelming support of neighbors and strangers as the catalyst for their first steps forward.</p>

<h3>Age-Banded Communication Tips for Families Experiencing Crisis</h3>
<p>Talking to children about the loss of a home requires age-appropriate honesty. For young children, keep explanations simple and concrete: "Our house was damaged by the fire, and we are safe together, but we will need to live in a new place for a while." Reassure them constantly about basic safety needs—food, shelter, and family togetherness. For teenagers, who may grieve the loss of their privacy, personal belongings, and social connections, allow them to express anger and frustration without immediately trying to fix their feelings. Validate that their grief over lost yearbooks, sports mementos, and childhood bedrooms is entirely real and justified.</p>
