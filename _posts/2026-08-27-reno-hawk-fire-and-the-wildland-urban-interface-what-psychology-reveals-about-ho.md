    <h1>Reno Hawk Fire and the Wildland-Urban Interface: What Psychology Reveals About Housing Growth</h1>
    <p class="ncg-subtitle">When the Biggest Little City in the World faces a massive wildfire evacuation, it forces a hard look at why humans willingly build homes in the direct path of danger.</p>
  </header>

  <section class="ncg-section" id="introduction">
    <p>Over the last decade and a half, Reno, Nevada, has transformed into a booming magnet for people fleeing the high cost of living and congested traffic of California. With an astonishing 26% population surge from 2010 to 2025, the city expanded rapidly into the parched scrubland and foothills of the Sierra Nevada. But that growth hit a scorching wall of reality when the Hawk fire ignited near a hiking trail, forcing the harrowing evacuation of more than 40,000 residents and incinerating dozens of homes.</p>
    
    <p>This crisis is far more than a regional weather story. It is a striking illustration of the wildland-urban interface—the fastest-growing land-use type in America—where human ambition directly collides with ecological limits. Beyond the smoke, fire retardant, and displaced families lies a deeper question: Why do humans continuously flock to beautiful, high-risk landscapes while ignoring the creeping danger until the flames are at their doorsteps?</p>
  </section>

Triggered by human activity—though officials note investigators have yet to determine whether it was intentional or accidental—the blaze exploited a perfect storm of environmental conditions.</p>

  <section class="ncg-section" id="psychology-perspective">
    <h2>A Psychology Perspective: Why We Ignore Wildfire Risk</h2>
    <p>To understand how a bustling, modern boomtown ends up threatened by walls of flame, we have to look past zoning laws and climate data into the human mind. Behavioral psychology offers powerful insights into why millions of people move into high-risk wildland-urban interfaces without fully processing the danger.</p>

    <p>First is the powerful psychological mechanism known as <strong>normalcy bias</strong>. When people visit Reno or drive through the Sierra Nevada foothills on a pleasant spring afternoon, their brains register the stunning mountain views, blue skies, and clean air. The human mind naturally extrapolates that pleasant snapshot into an eternal baseline. We assume that because a landscape looks peaceful today, it will remain benign tomorrow. We fail to internalize that rare, catastrophic events—like a 35 mph wind gust over 12% humidity—are statistical certainties over a long enough timeline.</p>
