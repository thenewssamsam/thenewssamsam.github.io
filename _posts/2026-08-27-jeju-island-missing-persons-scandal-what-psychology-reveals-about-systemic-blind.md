<p>When four bodies were recovered in rapid succession across South Korea’s scenic Jeju Island, the public shock was immediate. But behind the horrifying discovery lies a deeper, more unsettling institutional failure: a police sergeant accused of lying to grieving families and closing hundreds of missing persons files prematurely. As President Lee Jae Myung orders a sweeping overhaul of the national police force and lawmakers grill officials, the nation is left asking how such a massive breakdown could happen in one of the world's safest countries.</p>

<p>To understand this tragedy, we have to look past the immediate corruption and examine the hidden psychological mechanisms that allow public institutions to slip into catastrophic negligence.</p>

<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened on Jeju Island</h2>
<p>The unfolding crisis centers on Jeju Island, a major tourist destination that draws over 13 million visitors annually. Reports surfaced last week that local authorities had launched a belated search for a woman who had vanished months prior. That single delay cracked open a much wider investigation, leading to the arrest of a police sergeant identified by his surname, Bu.</p>

<h2>The Psychology of Institutional Blind Spots</h2>
<p>Why would law enforcement officers abandon their duty of care, leading to fabricated updates and uninvestigated disappearances? Experts point to a powerful psychological phenomenon known as frequency bias combined with normalcy bias.</p>

<p>In South Korea, violent crime is exceptionally rare, and official figures show that over 70,000 adults are reported missing each year, with an astonishing 99% resolved successfully within a single month. As Konkuk University Professor Lee Yung-hyeock noted, officers working in the field experience this high success rate daily. Over time, human brains naturally seek cognitive shortcuts, or heuristics. Officers began to assume that every missing persons report would naturally resolve itself.</p>

<p>When an assumption hardens into an unexamined rule, cognitive dissonance takes over. If a new case contradicts the comfortable narrative that "everyone turns up safe," the bureaucratic mind often chooses administrative convenience over painful reality. Closing a file with a comforting lie requires far less cognitive effort than confronting a grim truth. It is a terrifying reminder of how systemic environments can dull human empathy until vulnerable lives become mere paperwork to be cleared away.</p>
