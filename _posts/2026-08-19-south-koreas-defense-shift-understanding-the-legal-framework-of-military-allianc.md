  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>The Current Situation</h2>
    <p>Following reports that U.S. Forces Korea commander Gen. Xavier Brunson visited the Defense Ministry to discuss reducing joint military exercises, South Korean leadership has emphasized the need for self-reliance. While maintaining the U.S. alliance, President Lee highlighted the necessity of accelerating the transfer of wartime operational control and developing advanced capabilities like nuclear-powered submarines. The administration maintains that civil defense exercises are purely defensive and aimed at public safety.</p>

    <h3>The Nature of OPCON</h3>
    <p>Wartime Operational Control is a legal designation that determines which military command has the authority to direct forces during a conflict. Historically, the U.S. has held this control in Korea. The current push for transfer is a legal and structural evolution, moving toward a "Combined Command" where South Korea assumes the lead while retaining U.S. support. This shift is designed to ensure that the host nation maintains the legal authority over its own territory and military assets.</p>

    <h3>International Treaty Obligations</h3>
    <p>Under international law, countries have the inherent right to self-defense, as recognized by the UN Charter. However, when nations enter into mutual defense treaties, they often delegate certain operational authorities. The legal challenge for any state is balancing these treaty obligations with the fundamental right to protect its own citizens and borders. The current diplomatic discourse in Seoul reflects a desire to harmonize these two legal realities.</p>
