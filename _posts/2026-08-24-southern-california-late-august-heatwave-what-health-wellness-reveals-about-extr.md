  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The Late August Heatwave Unfolds</h2>
    <p>According to recent meteorological reports from Daniel Swain of Weather West, a significant and potentially extreme heatwave is setting its sights on Southern California and parts of Arizona. A very strong late-summer ridge is building over northern Mexico and the southern Great Basin, positioning its most anomalous lobe directly above Southern California. While Northern California escapes the worst of this system, areas across the inland valleys, deserts, and potentially even areas close to the coast are preparing for multiple days of intense heat.</p>
    
    <p>What makes this late August event particularly notable is not just the daytime thermometer readings—which could easily climb well into the 90s and 100s in downtown Los Angeles and even higher in the San Fernando Valley and Inland Empire—but the persistence of abnormal warmth and moisture. Unusually high ocean temperatures stretching up the coast of Mexico and California are pumping a shallow layer of dense humidity into coastal and near-coastal communities. Coupled with the possibility of weak-to-moderate offshore winds and downslope warming, this setup introduces a complex meteorological puzzle with very real human health implications.</p>
  </section>

The secret behind this statistical anomaly lies in the dark: overnight minimum temperatures have remained exceptionally high.</p>

    <h3>The Physiology of Humid Heat</h3>
    <p>Humidity is essentially invisible friction for the human body. Normally, we cool ourselves through the evaporation of sweat from our skin. When the air is saturated with moisture—especially in that narrow "battleground" strip a few miles inland where coastal humidity meets desert heat—that natural evaporative cooling process stalls. The body works harder, the heart rate increases to push blood to the skin for cooling, and fluid demands skyrocket.</p>
