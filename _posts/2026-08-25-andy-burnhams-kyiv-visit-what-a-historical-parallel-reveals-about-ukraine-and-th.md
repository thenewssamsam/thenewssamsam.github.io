    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Kyiv</h2>
    <p>During his landmark visit to the Ukrainian capital, UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham met with President Volodymyr Zelensky and chaired his first meeting of the "coalition of the willing." The alliance, originally forged by the UK and France, is focusing heavily on bolstering Kyiv's air defenses amid escalating Russian strikes.</p>

    <p>In a significant escalation of military cooperation, the UK government agreed to transfer blueprints for British-made components of the Scalp missile—a French variant of the Storm Shadow cruise missile—enabling domestic production within Ukraine. Meanwhile, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov condemned the move as direct British involvement, warning that Russian forces are gathering data to target any domestic manufacturing sites.</p>

    <h2 id="historical-perspective">A Historical Parallel: The Long Echo of Continental Defiance</h2>
    <p>When Prime Minister Burnham stood outside St. Sofia Cathedral in Kyiv and compared Ukraine's current resistance to Britain's lonely stand against Nazi Germany in the 20th century, he was not merely reaching for convenient rhetoric. He was tapping into a recurring pattern in European history: the small, beleaguered frontier state that unexpectedly becomes the bulkhead for a wider continent's security.</p>

    <p>History repeatedly shows that when powerful expansionist regimes test international borders, neighboring powers often attempt a dangerous calculus of appeasement or delayed intervention. From the Rhineland remilitarization in 1936 to the partitions of the 19th century, the temptation to view distant conflicts as localized disputes has consistently proven disastrous. Burnham's explicit invocation—"My nation kept the flame of European freedom alive in the 20th century, you hold that flame in the 21st"—highlights a profound historical parallel: the realization that containment at the periphery is far less costly than capitulation at the center.</p>
