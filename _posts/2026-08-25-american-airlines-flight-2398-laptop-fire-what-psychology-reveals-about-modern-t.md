    <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: #2d3748; border-bottom: 1px solid #edf2f7; padding-bottom: 8px; margin-top: 0;">What Happened on Flight 2398?</h2>
    <p>Air travel feels so routine for millions of people that it is easy to forget we are hurtling through the stratosphere inside a pressurized metal tube. That illusion of mundane safety was shattered abruptly on a Friday evening as American Airlines Flight 2398 approached Dallas Fort Worth International Airport (DFW).</p>
    <p>According to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and official reports, the Airbus A321 was carrying 133 people on a flight originating from Atlanta when a customer's laptop battery suddenly began smoking and caught fire roughly 30 minutes before landing. Cabin crew members acted swiftly, utilizing a specialized thermal containment bag to smother the flames before the situation could escalate into a catastrophic disaster.</p>
    <p>Passenger accounts paint a vivid picture of the tense moments inside the cabin. Jessica Irgens, seated just two rows away from the burning device, described heavy smoke filling the area and forcing passengers toward the back of the aircraft. Air traffic control audio obtained by news outlets revealed that the pilot reported potential minor burns for four to five passengers who attempted to assist. Fortunately, the aircraft taxied to the gate under its own power, where emergency medical personnel met the flight, treated the injured individuals on the scene, and released them.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.2rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 20px;">1. The Shock of Normalcy Bias</h3>
    <p>Psychologists frequently point to <em>normalcy bias</em>—our tendency to underestimate the likelihood of a disaster or its potential effects because we have never experienced one before. When smoke billowed from the laptop on Flight 2398, passengers did not immediately panic; instead, initial reports show they tried to help and problem-solve. This cognitive delay is standard human behavior. Our brains desperately want to rationalize strange smells or sudden smoke as something minor—perhaps a spilled drink or an electronic hiccup—before accepting that we are facing a genuine emergency.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.2rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 20px;">2. The Fight-or-Flight Bottleneck in Confined Spaces</h3>
    <p>When normalcy bias breaks down, the evolutionary fight-or-flight response kicks in. However, inside a narrow-body aircraft cabin like an Airbus A321, physical options are severely restricted. You cannot simply run away from the danger. Passengers trapped rows away from a chemical fire experience a spike in perceived helplessness. According to psychological studies on crowd behavior, this constraint forces two divergent reactions: altruism (passengers stepping forward to assist and clear the aisle) and acute anxiety. The fact that fellow travelers rushed to help demonstrates that prosocial behavior often overrides pure panic when leadership is visible.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.2rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 20px;">3. The Invisible Nature of Lithium-Ion Risk</h3>
    <p>Part of what makes these incidents psychologically unsettling is their invisibility. Lithium-ion batteries power nearly every modern convenience—from our smartphones to our portable work laptops. Yet, as the FAA notes, "thermal runaway" can occur without warning due to internal shorts, damage, or overcharging. Because the danger is hidden inside a device we trust implicitly every day, a sudden ignition feels like a betrayal of our environment, amplifying residual travel anxiety long after we touch down.</p>
