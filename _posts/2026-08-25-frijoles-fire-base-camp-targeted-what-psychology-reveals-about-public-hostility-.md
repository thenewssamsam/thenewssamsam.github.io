<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened at the Frijoles Fire Base Camp?</h2>
<p>Wildfires are terrifying, high-stress events that strain entire communities. In New Mexico, the lightning-caused Frijoles Fire has grown into the state's third-largest wildfire of 2026, scorching over 15,000 acres. Yet, while firefighters from agencies like the California Incident Management Team 8 made good progress—pushing containment to 45 percent and keeping communities updated with FireNet reports—they faced an unexpected threat far from the active fire lines.</p>

<p>On a Friday night, members of the public attempted to infiltrate the firefighters' base camp. This sanctuary, designed to give exhausted crews a safe space to recover between grueling 16-hour shifts, was breached by individuals who harassed incident management personnel with perceived threats before fleeing. In response, officials quickly escalated security from overnight patrols to 24-hour around-the-clock protection, which will remain until the camp is officially dismantled.</p>

<p>While communities in areas like Cundiyo, Cerro Piñon, and Borrego Mesa remain in evacuation status, and Nambe Pueblo Lake sits on ready alert, the attack on the base camp leaves many wondering: Why would anyone target the very people risking their lives to protect homes and neighborhoods?</p>

<h2>The Psychology of Crisis Hostility</h2>
<p>To understand why members of the public would harass resting firefighters, we have to look past the immediate anger and examine how human brains process prolonged catastrophe. Disasters disrupt our fundamental sense of safety, control, and predictability. When people feel helpless in the path of a massive wildfire, their internal psychological equilibrium shatters.</p>

<h3>Displacement and Scapegoating</h3>
<p>When individuals experience acute stress or loss of control, psychological defense mechanisms often kick in. Displacement occurs when helpless frustration is redirected away from an unmanageable source—like nature, wind, or lightning—and onto visible authorities. Firefighters, incident commanders, and emergency personnel become surrogate targets for displaced panic, fear, and generalized anger over evacuation orders or property loss.</p>

<h3>Normalcy Bias and Cognitive Dissonance</h3>
<p>Normalcy bias causes people to underestimate the danger of a crisis until it is directly upon them, often leading to resistance against mandatory evacuations. When reality violently shatters that bias—forcing families from their homes—cognitive dissonance sets in. The jarring friction between what people expected and the devastating reality of a 15,000-acre fire creates volatile emotional states. Some individuals struggle to process this dissonance constructively, resulting in erratic, aggressive outbursts directed at emergency responders.</p>
