<h3>Quick Take: The Shift in Bogotá</h3>
<p>Colombian President Abelardo de la Espriella has upended a decade of open-door refugee policy by ordering the immediate deportation of irregular migrants. The move marks a dramatic political realignment in South America, contrasting sharply with historical integration efforts while raising urgent moral, legal, and economic questions.</p>

<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in Colombia?</h2>
<p>In a stunning policy reversal, newly inaugurated Colombian President Abelardo de la Espriella ordered police operations to pursue and deport irregular migrants. Speaking during a security council meeting in Barranquilla, the far-right leader declared, "I will not accept any illegal immigrants, wherever they come from. They will have to leave and be deported. It is a political decision I am taking responsibility for."</p>

<p>This pronouncement shatters a decade-old consensus. Following the onset of the Venezuelan migration crisis in 2015, successive centrist, right-wing, and left-wing Colombian administrations welcomed millions fleeing economic collapse and political turmoil. Colombia absorbed roughly a third of the 7.8 million displaced Venezuelans, earning global praise for regularization initiatives. Now, under de la Espriella—who has been in office for less than three weeks—the nearly one million individuals residing in the country without regularized status face imminent expulsion. While the decree impacts all undocumented foreign nationals, it overwhelmingly affects the estimated 2.8 million Venezuelans living in Colombia, including almost 200,000 children and adolescents.</p>

<h2>The Economic and Human Reality</h2>
<p>Beyond political theater lies a complex economic ledger. Researchers like Andrés Sampayo point out that the Venezuelan diaspora contributes roughly $3 billion annually to the Colombian economy through consumption, formal taxes, and entrepreneurial activity. Regularizing workers helps integrate them into the tax base, whereas abrupt mass deportations can disrupt local commerce, construction, agriculture, and service sectors that have grown dependent on this labor force over the past ten years.</p>
