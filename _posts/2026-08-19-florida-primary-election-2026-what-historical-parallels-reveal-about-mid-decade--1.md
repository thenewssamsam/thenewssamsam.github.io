  <p class="fl-intro">Florida's 2026 primary elections are officially underway, marking a historic test of mid-decade redistricting that could flip up to four congressional seats and reshape the balance of power in the U.S. House of Representatives. But beyond the immediate political math, this high-stakes showdown over redrawn maps and prominent gubernatorial primaries echoes a long, contentious American tradition of legislative boundary manipulation and shifting political alignments.</p>

  <section class="fl-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in Florida's Primary Election?</h2>
    <p>Voters across Florida head to the polls for the state's first major primary election since the Florida legislature approved a brand-new congressional map earlier this year. Designed to strengthen Republican leverage, the map puts several Democratic-held seats directly in the crosshairs—specifically those of Reps. Darren Soto, Jared Moskowitz, Debbie Wasserman Schultz, and Kathy Castor. For incumbents like Wasserman Schultz, who opted to run in the newly redrawn, majority-minority 20th Congressional District, the shift has ignited intra-party friction with Black Democrats who point to similar mid-decade map overhauls in states like Tennessee, Alabama, and Louisiana.</p>
    <p>Meanwhile, the Republican side features its own internal battles. Rep. Cory Mills faces a tough primary challenge in the 7th Congressional District from Ryan Elijah, backed by fellow House Republicans Anna Paulina Luna and Mike Haridopolos, even as Mills retains the backing of President Donald Trump. At the top of the ticket, U.S. Rep. Byron Donalds stands as the front-runner in the GOP gubernatorial primary, buoyed by major fundraising and a presidential endorsement as term-limited Gov. Ron DeSantis prepares to step down. If Donalds wins in November, he would make history as Florida's first Black governor.</p>

    <h2>A Historical Parallel: The Long Tradition of Mid-Decade Redistricting</h2>
    <p>To modern observers, changing legislative maps outside of the standard post-Census cycle feels like an unprecedented parliamentary maneuver. Yet, history shows that redrawing lines whenever political control permits is a time-honored, if controversial, American practice.</p>
    <p>Under the U.S. Constitution, redistricting is primarily left to the states. While the standard expectation is a decennial update following the once-a-decade Census, courts and legislatures have frequently revisited maps mid-decade when power shifts hands. A classic historical parallel can be seen in the Texas redistricting battles of the early 2000s, where a mid-decade map redraw engineered by state legislative majorities dramatically shifted congressional representation and caught national attention.</p>
    <blockquote class="fl-quote-box">
      <p>"History reminds us that legislative maps have rarely been treated as permanent parchment; they are frequently shaped by the political ambitions of the party holding the levers of power at any given moment."</p>
    </blockquote>
    <p>However, history also reveals the limits of engineered maps. While structural map changes can heavily favor one party on paper, national political waves, economic conditions, and shifting voter turnout frequently defy cartographic intentions. A map designed to lock in a four-seat advantage can just as easily become vulnerable if a broad anti-incumbent sentiment sweeps through the electorate by November.</p>
  </section>
