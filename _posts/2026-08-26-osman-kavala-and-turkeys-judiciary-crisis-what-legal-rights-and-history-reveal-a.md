<p>When the European Court of Human Rights issued a blunt rebuke of Turkey's judicial system on Tuesday, it did more than revisit a high-profile imprisonment. It brought global attention back to a fundamental question of modern governance: what happens to a nation when the line separating the executive branch from the courts dissolves entirely? For philanthropist Osman Kavala, now 68 and serving a life sentence without parole, this systemic breakdown represents a personal tragedy—and a glaring warning light for the rule of law.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Kavala Case?</h2>

<p>The European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) declared that Turkey's courts suffer from a "systemic problem" characterized by the systematic detention and prosecution of political opponents, journalists, and human rights defenders. The court reiterated a demand it first made in 2019: that Kavala's conviction be regarded as null and void.</p>

<p>Kavala, the founder of a nonprofit dedicated to peace and dialogue, was initially arrested in 2017. Authorities accused him of financing the 2013 Gezi Park protests. Although a Turkish court acquitted him in 2020, he was swiftly rearrested on allegations connected to the failed 2016 coup attempt. His acquittal was overturned, his case was merged with the coup proceedings, and in April 2022, he was sentenced to life in prison without parole.</p>

<h2 id="legal-perspective">A Legal Rights Explainer: The Principle of Judicial Independence</h2>

<p>From a legal rights perspective, the core issue at stake in the Kavala ruling is not merely guilt or innocence of specific charges, but the structural integrity of due process. In a constitutional democracy, the judiciary functions as an essential check and balance on executive power. When executive influence penetrates judicial decision-making, the entire architecture of legal protection collapses.</p>
