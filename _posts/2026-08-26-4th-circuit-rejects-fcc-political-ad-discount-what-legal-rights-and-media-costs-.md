<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Fourth Circuit Sets Aside FCC Notice</h2>
<p>The legal battle centers on a public notice issued by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) earlier this spring. Under long-standing federal guidelines, broadcast stations are required to offer qualified candidates their lowest available advertising rate (LAR) during specific windows before an election—specifically 45 days prior to a primary election and 60 days before a general election.</p>

<p>However, the FCC’s spring public notice attempted to expand this discounted rate umbrella to include political parties and joint fundraising committees. The Fourth Circuit flatly rejected that expansion. In its ruling, the court determined that the clear, unambiguous language of the lowest-price policy applies exclusively to candidates themselves. Because the agency stretched its interpretation beyond what the statute actually authorized, the Fourth Circuit declared the public notice to be "set aside and held for naught."</p>

<h2 id="legal-perspective">A Legal Rights Explainer: Statutory Interpretation and Broadcaster Rates</h2>
<p>From a legal standpoint, this case underscores a foundational principle of administrative law: federal agencies must operate within the strict boundaries set by Congress. They cannot rewrite statutes through policy notices or informal guidance simply because they believe an expanded rule would be fairer or more politically expedient.</p>

<p>The Communications Act grants specific privileges to legally qualified candidates because federal law recognizes the unique role individual candidates play in democratic accountability. Stations must give them reasonable access to airwaves and cannot charge them more than their most favored commercial advertisers during those pre-election windows. Political parties, while vital to the electoral process, operate under different statutory frameworks. When the FCC attempted to bridge that statutory gap by administrative fiat, it overstepped its bounds.</p>
