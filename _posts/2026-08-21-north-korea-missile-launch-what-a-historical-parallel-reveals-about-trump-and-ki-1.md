<h2>What Happened: Missiles and Meeting Proposals</h2>
<p>Just hours after President Trump announced intentions to rekindle nuclear talks with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un later this year, Pyongyang launched approximately ten short-range ballistic missiles off its eastern coast. Seoul's military confirmed the launches, which followed a U.S. decision to scale back the ongoing Ulchi Freedom Shield joint military exercises with South Korea—drills that Pyongyang has historically condemned as rehearsals for invasion.</p>

<p>President Trump defended the reduction of the war games by pointing to his relationship with Kim, while also noting South Korea's lack of assistance in separate conflicts, such as the ongoing war with Iran. The American president stated that it was vital to "get along" with Kim, noting that North Korea now possesses 57 "very powerful" nuclear weapons—though South Korean officials estimate the stockpile is closer to 80 to 120 warheads. Meanwhile, Kim Yo Jong, the powerful sister of the North Korean leader, remarked that her brother still held "good memories" of Trump, even as she noted a lack of active communication between the two camps.</p>

<p>This rapid diplomatic pivot has left regional allies in Seoul and Tokyo anxious. With the Iranian conflict grinding on in a costly stalemate, critics speculate that Washington may be seeking a quick foreign policy victory in Asia. Yet, as past summits demonstrate, friendly rhetoric has consistently failed to halt the steady advancement of North Korea's nuclear arsenal.</p>

The historical pattern suggests that summitry without rigid, step-by-step verification often rewards belligerence rather than curbing it.</p>
