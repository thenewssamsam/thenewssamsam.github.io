  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 40px;">
    <h2 style="font-size: 24px; color: #111111; margin-bottom: 15px; border-left: 4px solid #0366d6; padding-left: 12px;">What Happened in the Interview?</h2>
    <p>During an appearance on Fox News Channel's "Jesse Watters Primetime," Michigan Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed faced pointed questions regarding his past opposition to legislative bans on Sharia law. Host Jesse Watters pressed the candidate on remarks he made in 2022, where El-Sayed suggested that efforts to outlaw Sharia were rooted in forces akin to White supremacy and historical American injustices.</p>
    
    <p>Rather than distancing himself from the comments, El-Sayed defended his position by drawing a direct parallel between Islamic religious law and Christian Church governance. "Nobody is trying to push Sharia law on anyone else, just like I hope nobody is trying to push canon law on anybody else, because we live in an America where you should have the freedom of your religion," El-Sayed stated during the broadcast.</p>

      <p style="margin: 8px 0 0 0; color: #586069;">The controversy centers not merely on political rhetoric, but on deeply held definitions of civil jurisprudence, religious liberty, and the distinct historical frameworks that govern religious communities versus civil society.</p>

    <p>The response was swift. Prominent conservative voices, religious commentators, and legal observers immediately took to social media to challenge the equivalence. Critics argued that treating Sharia and Christian canon law as functional equivalents ignores fundamental differences in scope, historical application, and their relationship with civil authority in Western democracies.</p>
