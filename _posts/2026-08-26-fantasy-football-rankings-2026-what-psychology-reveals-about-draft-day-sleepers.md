<h3>Recency Bias and the Rookie Trap</h3>
<p>Rookies and second-year players are perpetual magnets for attention during draft season. Part of this is driven by <em>recency bias</em>—our mental shortcut of giving disproportionate weight to the most recent information we consumed. When a computer model highlights a player's college dominance or a late-season surge, our imagination fills in the gaps with best-case scenarios.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Loss Aversion:</strong> The psychological pain of drafting a bust vastly outweighs the joy of hitting on a sleeper. This is why many managers hoard safe, boring veterans instead of swinging for high-upside rookies.</li>
<li><strong>Confirmation Bias:</strong> Once we decide we like a sleeper—such as a breakout tight end ranked in the top 8—we actively seek out articles and projection models that validate our choice while dismissing any contrary injury reports.</li>
<li><strong>Groupthink:</strong> In a live draft room, ad-hoc consensus forms rapidly. If three managers panic and run on running backs in the first round, others quickly abandon their draft boards out of sheer anxiety.</li>
</ul>
