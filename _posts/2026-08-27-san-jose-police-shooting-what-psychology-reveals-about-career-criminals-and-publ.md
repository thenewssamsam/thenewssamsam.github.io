    <h2>What Happened in San Jose</h2>
    <p>A routine vehicle pursuit in San Jose ended in a violent confrontation inside an innocent family's home, leaving a suspect dead and a veteran police officer recovering from critical wounds. According to San Jose Police Chief Paul Joseph, the incident began unfolding when officers spotted a stolen Chevrolet Tahoe linked to a string of armed robberies across the city.</p>

    <p>The suspect, identified as Anthony Chavez of Campbell, possessed an extensive criminal history and an active felony warrant. After evading a SWAT vehicle blockade in a high-speed chase that damaged multiple cars, Chavez crashed near Goodyear Street and Lick Avenue. Desperate to escape police pursuit, he fled on foot and slipped through an unlocked back door into an occupied residence, creating a high-stakes hostage-style crisis for an unsuspecting family.</p>
    
    <p>San Jose's MERGE unit executed a swift crisis entry, evacuating the terrified family safely before locating Chavez hiding in a bathroom. Sgt. Matt Walsh, leading the entry, was struck twice by gunfire but maintained extraordinary composure, directing his team and walking out under his own power. Chavez was fatally wounded when officers returned fire. Chief Joseph later revealed that Chavez was a career violent offender who had repeatedly received early releases and supervision leniency from the judicial system.</p>

    <h3>Temporal Discounting and Immediate Gratification</h3>
    <p>Psychologists often study <em>temporal discounting</em>—the human tendency to devalue future consequences in favor of immediate rewards. For chronic offenders embedded in antisocial behavior loops, long-term threats like prison sentences or parole violations feel abstract and distant. In contrast, the immediate thrill or perceived utility of theft provides instant gratification. This cognitive distortion allows individuals to completely detach their current actions from inevitable catastrophic outcomes.</p>

    <h3>The Illusion of Invulnerability and Normalcy Bias</h3>
    <p>Another powerful psychological phenomenon is <em>normalcy bias</em> combined with extreme compartmentalization. When repeat offenders successfully evade accountability multiple times—such as avoiding GPS monitoring compliance or skipping court dates—their brains adapt by normalizing high-risk behavior. They begin to operate under an unconscious belief of invulnerability: <em>"I got away with it yesterday, so I will get away with it today."</em> This dangerous illusion blinds individuals to escalating stakes until they find themselves cornered in a bathroom with no escape.</p>
