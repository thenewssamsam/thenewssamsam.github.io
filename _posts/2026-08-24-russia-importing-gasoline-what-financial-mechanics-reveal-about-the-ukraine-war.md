  <p class="ncg-lead">For decades, Russia stood as one of the world's most dominant petroleum exporters, shipping refined fuels across global markets. Now, on day 1,639 of the conflict, the Russian government has officially confirmed a stunning reversal: the nation is importing gasoline.</p>

  <p>Following a sustained campaign of targeted Ukrainian drone and missile strikes against Russian refineries and fuel depots, domestic processing capabilities have taken a severe hit. A shipload of gasoline originating from India has already entered the Russian domestic market, while rail imports are being arranged from neighboring Belarus and Kazakhstan. For everyday observers of the global economy, this development offers a stark lesson in how modern warfare alters basic supply chains, shifts trade flows, and reshapes financial realities.</p>

  <div class="ncg-quick-take">
    <h3>Quick Take: The Shift in Energy Flows</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>The Core Event:</strong> Russia has begun importing refined gasoline from India via ship-to-ship transfers, alongside planned rail imports from Belarus and Kazakhstan.</li>
      <li><strong>The Catalyst:</strong> Ukrainian military strikes on domestic oil refineries and depots forced Moscow to restrict domestic sales, capping petrol purchases at 30 liters across Rosneft stations.</li>
      <li><strong>The Financial Lesson:</strong> Energy independence is fragile, and prolonged conflict inevitably introduces heavy logistical costs and import markups.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Mechanics of a Reversed Supply Chain</h2>

  <p>According to recent reports, an Oman-flagged tanker transferred approximately 68,000 tonnes of gasoline at Port Said from the vessel <em>Agni</em>, which had loaded the fuel at India's Vadinar port. This cargo successfully unloaded at Russia's Arctic port of Vitino in early August, with at least two more gasoline cargoes expected in the coming weeks. At the same time, Russian Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak acknowledged the onset of imports.</p>

  <p>Domestically, the squeeze is tightening. Witnesses and suppliers report that fuel stations in Moscow and across the country—most notably state-owned Rosneft—have reimposed strict purchasing limits, capping petrol at 30 liters per customer. This domestic rationing comes as exports have been entirely banned to preserve remaining internal supplies.</p>
