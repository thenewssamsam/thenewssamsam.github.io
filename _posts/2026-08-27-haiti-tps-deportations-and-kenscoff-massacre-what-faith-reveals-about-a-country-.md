<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Kenscoff Massacre</h2>
<p>On August 23, 2026, violence flared anew in Haiti when an armed gang attack ripped through Kenscoff, a village nestled in the hills outside the capital of Port-au-Prince. The raid resulted in the deaths of more than 47 people, while at least 50 others were abducted. Among the most chilling details reported by United Nations officials is that 22 of the victims were brutally executed inside a church compound—a sacred space where they had sought refuge from the chaos.</p>

<h2 id="the-tps-crisis">The Policy Reality: Losing TPS in America</h2>
<p>Against this backdrop of systemic violence, U.S. policy regarding Haitian migrants has shifted dramatically. Following a June Supreme Court ruling and an August 5 affirmation by U.S. District Court Judge Ana C. Reyes, the termination of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for Haiti has been cleared to proceed. Created under the Immigration Act of 1990, TPS was designed precisely for nationals of countries experiencing war, environmental disaster, or acute crises—conditions that undeniably describe modern Haiti.</p>

<p>Over 330,000 Haitians held TPS protection as of 2025. Now, with those protections revoked, hundreds of thousands face the daunting prospect of forced repatriation to a nation unable to protect them. Archbishop Wenski points out that while a Senate bill exists to extend TPS through early 2029, its passage hinges largely on executive support.</p>

<p>The cascading effects of losing legal status are immediate and severe. Without work permits and driver's licenses, individuals lose their livelihoods. Employers—including the Archdiocese of Miami, which reportedly had to let go of roughly 45 workers on TPS—are legally barred from keeping them employed. Furthermore, families back in Haiti who rely on remittances sent home by migrant workers are cut off from critical funds needed for basic survival and children's education.</p>
