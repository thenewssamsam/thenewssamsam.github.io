  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Ongoing Northwest Indiana Power Crisis</h2>
    <p>The severe weather event that triggered the current crisis struck on August 11, delivering a powerful derecho that knocked out power to more than 60% of NIPSCO's customer base—impacting over 350,000 homes and businesses. While the utility company has managed to restore service to the vast majority of those affected, pockets of Northwest Indiana remain without electricity nearly two weeks later.</p>
    
    <p>As of late August, more than 21,000 customers are still waiting for the lights to turn back on. Municipalities like Munster, Gary, and Portage have borne the brunt of the lingering outages. While NIPSCO has stated that crews are working around the clock to repair historic damage to the regional grid, restoration estimates have stretched into late Tuesday night—marking a full 14 days without power for families and small businesses.</p>

    <p>The agonizingly slow recovery prompted sharp words from state leadership. Governor Mike Braun took to social media to voice his mounting frustration, stating that NIPSCO possesses "ample resources to get the job done in northwest Indiana, but they continue to fail Hoosiers." Highlighting the unique position of the utility, Braun added, "NIPSCO is a monopoly with a responsibility to ratepayers. Residents are forced to rely on them for safety and comfort and they must get the power back on NOW." Local leaders, including Gary Mayor Eddie Melton, echoed the sentiment, noting that residents in affected areas feel increasingly forgotten as the calendar ticks toward the two-week mark.</p>

    <h3>The Economics of Emergency Surge Capacity</h3>
    <p>Utility companies face a perpetual economic optimization problem: how much capital should be allocated toward preparing for once-in-a-generation catastrophic events versus day-to-day operations? Maintaining an idle, massive surplus of crews and equipment year-round drives up baseline operational costs, which regulators eventually allow utilities to pass on to ratepayers through higher monthly bills.</p>
