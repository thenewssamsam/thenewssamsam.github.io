    <h1>Structural Excess Capacity: What the New Section 301 Tariffs Mean for Global Trade and Your Wallet</h1>
    <p class="ncg-lead">The United States is expanding its trade war toolkit by invoking a novel economic concept called "structural excess capacity" to justify a wave of new tariffs against 16 economies. As Washington prepares a 7.5% duty on Chinese goods alongside broader investigations, everyday consumers and financial markets are left asking a fundamental question: what does this abstract economic term actually mean, and who ultimately pays the bill?</p>
  </header>

  <section class="ncg-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened? The New Section 301 Expansion</h2>
    <p>According to recent reports from Bloomberg and trade analysis by Econbrowser, the United States is set to impose a 7.5% tariff on Chinese goods under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. This move targets alleged "structural excess capacity" resulting from foreign government intervention, pushing total recent US duties on Chinese imports to roughly 20% ahead of high-level diplomatic meetings.</p>
    
    <p>This policy is not isolated to Beijing. Earlier announcements from the Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR) outlined sixteen simultaneous investigations into nations including the European Union, Japan, Switzerland, Norway, Mexico, India, and others. The core justification rests on the idea that foreign industrial subsidies, state-backed financing, and economic planning allow overseas factories to keep producing goods even when global market demand falls flat.</p>
    
    <p>Yet, as economists have pointed out, the very phrase "structural excess capacity" is largely absent from standard international trade textbooks. While capacity utilization metrics are common in domestic industrial sectors—traditionally pegged around an 80% healthy threshold—applying them across international borders as a legal instrument for trade retaliation marks a distinct pivot in modern economic policy.</p>

    <h2>The Financial Mechanics: Who Really Pays for Tariffs?</h2>
    <p>When policymakers announce tariffs, political rhetoric often suggests that the exporting country pays the tax. From a purely financial and accounting perspective, however, the reality is far more direct: import tariffs are taxes paid at the border by domestic importers—the US companies, distributors, and retailers bringing foreign goods into the country.</p>

    <h2>Unpacking "Structural Excess Capacity" in Trade Law</h2>
    <p>The USTR’s analytical framework attempts to link two separate layers: seven specific policy interventions (such as subsidies, wage suppression, and subsidised lending) and 33 evidentiary indicators (such as low capacity utilization, trade surpluses, and unprofitable firms). Interestingly, trade data reveals a striking divergence between existing government reports—like the National Trade Estimate (NTE) Report—and the new Section 301 documents.</p>
