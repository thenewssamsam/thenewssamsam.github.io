  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: Six Months in the Strait of Hormuz</h2>
    <p>When military strikes involving the United States and Israel erupted against Iran on February 28, early assurances suggested the conflict would be a short-lived affair lasting merely four to five weeks. Instead, six months have passed, and the nature of the confrontation has fundamentally shifted. Rather than sustained combat strikes, the world is now witnessing a prolonged economic and strategic standoff centered on the Strait of Hormuz—a vital maritime bottleneck that routinely handles roughly one-fifth of the global oil trade.</p>
    
    <p>Recent diplomatic maneuvers, including new sanctions announced by the White House, aim to exert maximum economic pressure on Tehran. Yet, the domestic fallout within the United States is becoming increasingly tangible. Consumer sentiment has dropped sharply, driven primarily by fears over sticky inflation, climbing energy costs, and disruptions in vital global shipping lanes. To understand why this overseas conflict is hitting domestic wallets so directly, we have to examine the mechanical chain reactions coursing through global supply chains.</p>

    <p>First, consider the cost of driving and energy. Gasoline prices are directly tied to the price of crude oil. With freight cuts biting into these shipments, the cost of fertilizing crops has skyrocketed. Farmers face higher operational overhead, which inevitably translates to higher food prices at home and in restaurants.</p>
