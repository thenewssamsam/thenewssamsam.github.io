        <h2 id="what-happened" class="ncg-h2">What Happened in the New Poll</h2>
        <p class="ncg-p">President Donald Trump’s administration is facing a sharp headwind as public polling numbers reach a new low for his second term. According to a Reuters/Ipsos survey released on August 17, 2026, only 33% of Americans approve of his job performance. This marks a 2-percentage-point drop from earlier in the month and ties his lowest approval rating recorded during his first term in December 2017.</p>
        <p class="ncg-p">The downward trend is not an isolated data point. Major polling aggregators echo the sentiment: RealClearPolitics puts his approval at 39.2% against a 58.4% disapproval rate, while the New York Times and Silver Bulletin averages reflect similar discontent, with disapproval hovering near 60%. Behind these numbers lies a palpable sense of unease driven primarily by foreign policy conflicts and mounting household financial pressures.</p>
    </section>

        <h2 id="psychology-angle" class="ncg-h2">The Psychology of Perceived Loss and Economic Anxiety</h2>
        <p class="ncg-p">Why do poll numbers shift so dramatically during geopolitical crises? From a psychological perspective, public reaction to events like the ongoing war on Iran is heavily influenced by loss aversion and normalcy bias. When conflict threatens roughly a fifth of the global oil trade, the immediate psychological impact hits home the moment drivers pull up to the pump.</p>
        <p class="ncg-p">According to AAA data cited in the report, the average price of a gallon of gas hit  on August 17—a striking jump from  a year prior. When everyday essentials surge in cost, citizens experience a profound sense of instability. Behavioral economics shows that people feel the pain of a financial loss far more acutely than the joy of a gain. Combine this economic sting with the sobering reality that 80% of Americans—including 87% of Democrats and 71% of Republicans—believe the conflict with Iran will drag on indefinitely, and a clear psychological pattern emerges. Chronic uncertainty breeds widespread dissatisfaction.</p>
        
        <div class="ncg-quote-box">
            <p>&ldquo;When fuel prices surge and foreign conflicts stretch into uncertainty, the human brain shifts instantly from optimism to defense, driving down political trust.&rdquo;</p>
        </div>

        <p class="ncg-p">Furthermore, threats of heightened escalation—such as warnings regarding negotiations involving regional allies like Oman—amplify collective anxiety. People crave predictability and safety. When national news regularly broadcasts the specter of prolonged conflict and economic strain, public approval ratings naturally become the barometer of that collective stress.</p>
