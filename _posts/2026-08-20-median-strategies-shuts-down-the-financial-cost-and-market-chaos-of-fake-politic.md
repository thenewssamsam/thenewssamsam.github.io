    <h1 style="font-size: 2.25rem; font-weight: 800; color: #0f172a; line-height: 1.2; margin: 0 0 15px 0;">
      Median Strategies Shuts Down: The Financial Cost and Market Chaos of Fake Political Polls
    </h1>
    <p style="font-size: 1.15rem; color: #475569; margin: 0; font-weight: 400;">
      When a mysterious polling outfit admits its high-profile election surveys were just a "social experiment," the real casualties aren't just academic trust—they are campaign budgets, donor dollars, and media market efficiency.
    </p>
  </header>

  <section id="ncg-hook-facts" style="margin-bottom: 35px;">
    <p>
      The political information ecosystem took a direct financial hit this week as Median Strategies—the shadowy polling outfit responsible for fabricating high-profile election surveys in Los Angeles and Wisconsin—officially closed its doors. The firm admitted that its widely circulated polls showing Democratic Mayor Karen Bass leading her primary rival by wide margins, alongside a similarly inflated survey in a Wisconsin gubernatorial primary, were complete hoaxes designed as a "short-term social experiment."
    </p>
    
    <p>
      While media outlets scrambled to correct their broadcasts and campaign strategists re-evaluated their data sources, ordinary observers might have viewed this as a mere curiosity or a prank on political junkies. But from a strict economic standpoint, bad data is never free. In modern American politics, polling data functions as a critical economic signal. When that signal is intentionally corrupted, it distorts capital allocation, misdirects donor contributions, and inflicts tangible financial damage on campaigns trying to navigate hyper-competitive races.
    </p>

    <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: 700; color: #1e293b; margin-top: 0; border-bottom: 1px solid #cbd5e1; padding-bottom: 8px;">
      The Cost of Information Asymmetry in Media Markets
    </h2>
    
    <p>
      In financial markets, the SEC polices material misstatements because false data destroys market integrity and punishes honest investors. In the political economy, however, the barrier to entry for releasing "survey data" is remarkably low. Median Strategies operated with minimal initial footprint, yet its numbers were picked up by local TV newscasts, online reporting outlets, and social media influencers.
    </p>
