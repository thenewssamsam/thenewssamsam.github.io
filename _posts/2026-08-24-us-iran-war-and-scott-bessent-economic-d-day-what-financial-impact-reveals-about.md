This declaration precedes a crucial Monday news conference where the Treasury Department is expected to unveil sweeping new sanctions targeting Iran's petroleum enablers, banking networks, and international trade partners.</p>

    <h2>The Financial Mechanics of the Strait of Hormuz Chokepoint</h2>
    <p>To understand why a diplomatic and military conflict in the Persian Gulf instantly rattles household budgets globally, one must look at the basic financial mechanics of oil supply chains. Prior to the war, approximately 20 million barrels of oil per day traversed the Strait of Hormuz. Recent Department of Energy estimates indicate that volume has plummeted to between 8 million and 9 million barrels daily as tanker traffic slows to a trickle.</p>
    
    <p>When supply contracts abruptly while global demand remains relatively inelastic, basic market economics dictate that prices must rise. Brent crude hovered around $93.77 a barrel over the weekend, while US benchmark crude sat near $86.47. In financial markets, volatility is the ultimate tax. Tanker operators facing seizure risks must factor higher insurance premiums, rerouting costs, and security outlays into their operating expenses. These costs are ultimately passed down the line, affecting petrochemical producers, manufacturers, and transportation firms worldwide.</p>

    <h2>Ripple Effects: Gas Prices and Federal Reserve Policy</h2>
    <p>The macroeconomic shockwaves from the US-Iran war are rippling directly into domestic American consumer prices. According to AAA data, the national average for a gallon of gasoline settled at $4.09, sitting roughly 37.5% higher than before hostilities began in late February. Energy prices overall are running nearly 15% higher than they were a year ago, injecting persistent inflationary pressure into the broader economy.</p>
