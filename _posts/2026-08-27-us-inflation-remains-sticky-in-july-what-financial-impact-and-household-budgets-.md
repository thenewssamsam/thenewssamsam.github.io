  <section class="ncg-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the July Economic Data</h2>
    <p>Annual U.S. inflation held steady in July, coming in well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target for the 65th consecutive month. According to data released by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Economic Analysis, the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index increased 3.7% in the 12 months through July, unchanged from June and slightly higher than the 3.6% forecast by economists.</p>
    <p>Simultaneously, the broader economy showed surprising resilience. Orders for major capital goods rebounded, business investment in artificial intelligence remained brisk, and corporate profits surged in the second quarter. Gross domestic product (GDP) growth for the third quarter is now projected to run at a robust 3% or higher, doubling the previous quarter's unrevised annualized growth rate of 1.5%.</p>
    <p>However, this economic acceleration comes with a steep price tag for ordinary budgets. The unexpected pause in the downward trend of inflation—exacerbated by ongoing geopolitical conflicts affecting energy markets and the introduction of new import tariffs—has increased the likelihood that the Federal Reserve may feel compelled to lift interest rates rather than cut them.</p>

    <h2>The Financial Impact: Understanding the Mechanics of Sticky Inflation</h2>
    <p>When economists talk about &#8220;sticky inflation,&#8222; they are describing a situation where prices refuse to drop quickly even after the initial shocks—such as commodity spikes or supply chain disruptions—begin to subside. For household finances, this creates a quiet, persistent erosion of purchasing power.</p>

Companies that successfully passed tariff-induced and supply-chain costs down to the consumer maintained healthy margins, while household savings felt the pinch.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-subhead">Interest Rates and Borrowing Costs</h3>
    <p>The stubborn inflation print has immediate consequences for borrowing. Following the report, market pricing reflected about a 40% probability of a Federal Reserve rate hike at its September meeting, up from 36% just prior. For consumers, a higher-for-longer interest rate environment means borrowing costs for mortgages, auto loans, and credit cards will remain elevated. If you carry variable-rate debt, debt service payments consume a larger share of monthly income.</p>
