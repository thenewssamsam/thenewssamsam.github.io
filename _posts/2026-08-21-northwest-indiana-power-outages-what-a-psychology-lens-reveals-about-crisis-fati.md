  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: Ten Days in the Dark</h2>
    <p>Severe storms tore through the Midwest leaving a trail of heavy damage to regional power infrastructure. For days, tens of thousands of customers serviced by NIPSCO found themselves grappling with total electrical failure. Just as restoration crews made progress, fresh setbacks—including downed power lines near US 41 and Main Street in Schererville that triggered smoke and another massive outage for 9,000 customers—reset the timeline.</p>
    
    <p>Local small business owners like Tony Vicario, who watched $25,000 in perishable inventory spoil at his Italian deli in Dyer, joined exhausted parents like Shay Bolden in Gary, who spent ten days without power for her three children. While Indiana Governor Mike Braun voiced sharp frustration with utility management and deployed state agencies to assist, the human element of the disaster remained centered on the endless waiting, the buzzing borrowed generators, and the sheer unpredictability of an uncertain restoration schedule.</p>
  </section>

    <p>Human beings are wired for routine. We rely on invisible systems—electricity, running water, predictable supply chains—to anchor our sense of safety. When those systems vanish overnight, our brains react to the sudden loss of control.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias and the Moving Target</h3>
    <p>In the initial hours of a blackout, most people exhibit <em>normalcy bias</em>—the psychological tendency to underestimate the likelihood of a disaster or assume things will return to normal quickly. When NIPSCO initially estimated power would return by Friday, residents planned accordingly. But as deadlines slipped from days into weeks, that mild inconvenience morphed into chronic cognitive dissonance. The brain struggles to adapt when expected milestones are repeatedly missed.</p>

    <h3>Decision Fatigue and Daily Friction</h3>
    <p>Simple tasks that normally happen on autopilot—washing clothes, cooking dinner, checking the refrigerator—suddenly require conscious, exhausting effort. Psychologists call this acute accumulation of micro-decisions <strong>decision fatigue</strong>. Without the basic infrastructure of home comfort, mental bandwidth is entirely consumed by survival logistics, leaving little emotional reserve for patience or optimism.</p>

    <h3>The Collective Impact of Helplessness</h3>
    <p>Prolonged power loss can induce feelings akin to learned helplessness, particularly when communication from utility providers feels opaque or unresponsive. Frustration boils over not just because the lights are out, but because residents feel powerless to influence the timeline of their own lives.</p>
