<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Army's Nuclear Pivot</h2>
<p>The U.S. Army is officially taking energy independence into its own hands. Through the newly unveiled Janus Program, the military has selected five private nuclear energy companies to build, own, and operate nuclear microreactors across five major installations in North Carolina, Kentucky, Texas, Georgia, and New York. This initiative responds directly to a September 2028 operational target set out in executive leadership directives aimed at cutting reliance on civilian power grids that officials view as increasingly vulnerable to cyberattacks and physical disruptions.</p>

<p>The initial buildout features prominent private sector players: Antares Nuclear at Fort Bragg, North Carolina; BWXT Advanced Technologies at Fort Campbell, Kentucky; and General Atomics Electromagnetic Systems at Fort Hood, Texas. Additionally, Radiant Industries will install a reactor at Fort Benning, Georgia, while Westinghouse Government Services takes on Fort Drum, New York. Far from being a short-term scientific experiment, the initiative relies on a milestone-based funding model spanning fiscal years 2027 through 2031, requiring companies to hit strict technical markers before government funds are released. Crucially, these firms are also contributing significant private capital.</p>

The Janus Program answers this by shifting away from traditional cost-plus defense contracting toward a risk-sharing, milestone-based model.</p>

<p>From a macroeconomic perspective, this funding structure creates a controlled incubator for advanced nuclear technology. Developing small modular reactors (SMRs) and microreactors has historically faced steep regulatory hurdles and high upfront capital expenditure barriers. By acting as an anchor tenant with deep pockets, the military is effectively subsidizing the research, development, and scaling phases for the private sector. If these companies successfully drive down production costs and prove operational reliability, the commercial spinoff effects could revolutionize civilian industrial power, remote community microgrids, and heavy manufacturing energy costs.</p>

<h2>Energy Security as Economic Defense</h2>
<p>In modern economics, energy is the ultimate baseline currency. Every supply chain, manufacturing plant, and digital data center relies uninterruptedly on stable electrical grids. When military installations rely entirely on public utility grids, they inherit external vulnerabilities—ranging from severe weather events to sophisticated foreign cyber threats targeting critical infrastructure.</p>
