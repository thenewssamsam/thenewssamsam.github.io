<section id="what-happened">
<p>Long lines are wrapping around gas stations across Tehran, with motorists waiting two hours or more just to fill their tanks. This visible sign of strain comes nearly six months into the military and economic conflict launched by the United States and Israel against Iran. As U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent vows to fully sever the Islamic Republic from the global economy and threatens secondary sanctions on its remaining trading partners, the financial architecture connecting ordinary Iranians to basic goods is fraying.</p>

<p>Yet, despite the Iranian rial plunging to a staggering 2.02 million to the U.S. dollar and double-digit inflation driving up the cost of everyday staples like milk and pasta, government leaders have given no sign of bowing to Washington. Instead, both sides are engaged in a high-stakes war of attrition: Washington is tightening a naval blockade to dry up state revenues, while Tehran has largely shut down the vital Strait of Hormuz, betting that global economic pain and rising U.S. fuel prices will force a retreat before domestic endurance runs out.</p>
</section>

<section id="financial-mechanics">
<h2>The Financial Mechanics of a Naval Blockade and Collapsing Currency</h2>

<p>To understand the current crisis in Tehran, one must look past the geopolitical rhetoric and examine the cold mechanics of international finance under siege. When a nation is subjected to a comprehensive naval blockade and targeted financial isolation, the immediate shock hits the currency market. A collapsing currency like the rial destroys purchasing power almost overnight, turning routine grocery shopping into a complex balancing act where merchants must offer buy-now-pay-later arrangements to regular customers.</p>

<p>At the same time, the Iranian state is attempting to leverage its geographic chokehold on the Strait of Hormuz, which previously carried a fifth of the world's oil and gas. By restricting this critical energy corridor, Tehran is deliberately exporting economic instability outward, driving up global energy costs in the hope that political pressure at the pump will alter Washington's calculations. It is a dual-clock scenario: one clock measures how long ordinary citizens can absorb skyrocketing inflation and fuel shortages before reaching a breaking point, while the other measures how long global markets and Western voters can stomach high energy prices.</p>

Economic systems can be rebuilt, but the human toll of protracted conflict leaves a mark that lingers long after the sanctions are lifted.</p>
