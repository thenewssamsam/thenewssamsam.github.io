    <h1>Recent College Grads Are Struggling to Find Work: What Career and Industry Trends Reveal About AI and the Job Market</h1>
  </header>

  <p class="ncg-lead">Submitting hundreds of applications with nothing to show for it has become the harsh reality for many recent college graduates. While nearly half of young job seekers blame artificial intelligence for their employment struggles, top economists point to a much more complex tug-of-war between automation, remote work styles, and the foundational realities of starting a career today.</p>

  <p>For 21-year-old Irene Chang, the entry-level job search has turned into an exhausting marathon. An industrial and systems engineering graduate from Georgia Tech, Chang began sending out applications a year before collecting her diploma. Despite firing off roughly 450 resumes and landing about 19 interviews, she has yet to secure a single job offer. Jacqueline Kline, a 25-year-old Florida State University communications master's graduate, shares a similar story after submitting over 500 applications since December.</p>

  <p>When young professionals put in relentless effort—networking, earning advanced degrees, and completing multiple internships—only to face constant rejection, the frustration is entirely understandable. Naturally, many point the finger at the most visible technological shift of our era: artificial intelligence. But according to labor economists studying the data, the truth behind today's tough entry-level market goes far beyond simple tech displacement.</p>



  <div class="ncg-quick-take">
    <h3>Quick Take: The Entry-Level Job Crunch</h3>
    <ul>
      Recent college graduate unemployment sits at 5.7%, noticeably higher than the broader 4.1% worker unemployment rate.
      Nearly half of recent grads in a recent ZipRecruiter survey believe AI has directly impacted hiring in their respective fields.
      Prominent economists remain divided on whether generative AI or the post-pandemic rise of remote work is the primary driver behind sluggish junior hiring.
    </ul>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Entry-Level Job Market?</h2>

  <p>According to figures from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the unemployment rate for recent graduates—categorized as 22-to-27-year-olds holding a bachelor's degree or higher—stood at 5.7% as of June. That number significantly outpaces the general worker unemployment rate of 4.1%. Young workers feel the squeeze acutely because economic cooling cycles almost always hit junior roles first. When businesses tighten their belts, entry-level hiring is usually the first line item to get trimmed.</p>

  <p>At the same time, generative AI tools like ChatGPT have gone mainstream since late 2022. Young job seekers notice an undeniable overlap between the entry-level tasks they spent years studying and the baseline competencies that large language models handle efficiently. Writing basic code, drafting initial marketing copy, and sorting through structured data are precisely the kinds of codified knowledge tasks that junior employees historically cut their teeth on.</p>

  <h2 id="career-industry-perspective">A Career and Industry Perspective: Is AI Really to Blame?</h2>

  <p>To understand how career paths are shifting, leading economists have dug deep into payroll records, firm spending habits, and historical hiring timelines. Their findings reveal a nuanced debate.</p>

  <p>Stanford University economist Erik Brynjolfsson acknowledges that AI is playing a measurable role. Analyzing payroll data alongside his co-authors, Brynjolfsson discovered that early-career workers aged 22 to 25 in heavily AI-exposed fields—such as software development and marketing management—experienced a 16% relative employment decline since late 2022. By contrast, older workers in those same fields, alongside workers in hands-on, non-automated jobs like physical therapy and construction, saw stable or growing employment.</p>

  <p>Brynjolfsson explains that large language models are trained primarily on codified knowledge found in textbooks and documents. Because that mirrors the exact type of information inexperienced college graduates rely on when entering the workforce, junior employees face a unique competitive friction. Senior workers, on the other hand, possess invaluable tacit knowledge and wisdom gained through years of on-the-job experience that AI cannot easily replicate.</p>

  <p>Yet other experts urge caution against laying all the blame on automation. Harvard University economist David Deming points out a subtle chronological detail: the decline in junior hiring actually began roughly six months before ChatGPT launched. Instead of AI, Deming argues that remote work is the more significant culprit.</p>

  <p>An analysis by the New York Fed supports the idea that companies are far less willing to hire recent college graduates into remote or hybrid roles. Training a junior worker requires a substantial investment of time and mentorship from senior staff. When teams operate entirely from afar, onboarding a novice becomes exponentially harder. Consequently, the value proposition of hiring an entry-level worker drops, while the temptation to hire experienced senior talent from a nationwide remote talent pool grows.</p>

  <div class="ncg-quote-box">
    "The value proposition of hiring a junior person when you know they're going to be at home is just not as great, and because of remote work, you have access to a broader pool of talent from around the country." — David Deming, Economist
  </div>

  <p>Adding another twist to the narrative, University of Chicago economist Anders Humlum highlights firm-level spending data. A study by accounting firm Ramp and workforce research company Revelio Labs examined AI spending and head counts across more than 21,000 U.S. companies from 2021 to 2026. Surprisingly, firms making the largest financial investments in AI tools actually grew their entry-level head counts by 12% over the two years following adoption.</p>

  <p>If AI were simply replacing entry-level staff wholesale, heavy tech-spending firms would be shrinking their junior ranks. Instead, the companies leaning hardest into technological adoption are expanding their workforces more aggressively than anyone else.</p>

  <h2 id="wisdom-and-perspective">Finding Purpose and Perseverance Amid Industry Shifts</h2>

  <p>Times of rapid transition often stir up deep anxiety, prompting us to reevaluate the stability of our plans and the security of our livelihoods. It is completely natural to feel weary when hard work and long hours of preparation do not yield immediate professional rewards.</p>

  <p>This reality brings to mind the quiet wisdom found in Scripture regarding diligence, patience, and the value of steady perseverance. As the writer of Ecclesiastes observes, success is not always governed solely by human effort or the speed of the race: <em>"I have seen something else under the sun: The race is not to the swift or the battle to the strong, nor does food come to the wise or wealth to the brilliant or favor to the learned; but time and chance happen to them all."</em> (Ecclesiastes 9:11). This ancient observation reminds us that economic tides and technological waves operate outside our total control, humbling us to remember that our true worth is never defined solely by our job title or employment status.</p>

  <p>At the same time, practical wisdom encourages us to remain steadfast. Proverbs 16:3 offers a grounding principle for any professional navigating uncertainty: <em>"Commit to the Lord whatever you do, and he will establish your plans."</em> This perspective invites workers to pour their best effort into what they can control—cultivating resilience, adaptability, and lifelong learning—while releasing the heavy burden of anxiety over factors beyond their influence.</p>

  <h2 id="how-readers-respond">How Should Job Seekers and Professionals Respond?</h2>

  <p>Navigating a bumpy labor market requires practical strategy rather than despair. Here are concrete ways recent graduates and early-career professionals can adapt to changing industry dynamics:</p>

  <ul>
    <strong>Prioritize In-Person or Hybrid Connections:</strong> Given that remote onboarding remains a hurdle for junior hiring, seek out roles that offer structured in-person mentorship, office collaboration, or robust training programs where experienced colleagues can invest in your growth.
    <strong>Learn to Collaborate with AI:</strong> Instead of viewing artificial intelligence as an adversary, treat it as a powerful assistant. Master how to prompt, verify, and leverage AI tools to boost your daily productivity so you can showcase advanced technical fluency to prospective employers.
    <strong>Highlight Tacit Knowledge and Soft Skills:</strong> Emphasize human-centric abilities that algorithms cannot replicate—such as cross-functional teamwork, emotional intelligence, complex problem-solving, and relationship management.
    <strong>Build a Portfolio of Practical Projects:</strong> When resumes and automated application trackers fall short, standalone project portfolios demonstrating real-world problem-solving can help your capabilities stand out to hiring managers.
  </ul>

Major technological transitions always bring uncertainty and frustration, but they also unlock entirely new avenues for human creativity and capability.</p>
