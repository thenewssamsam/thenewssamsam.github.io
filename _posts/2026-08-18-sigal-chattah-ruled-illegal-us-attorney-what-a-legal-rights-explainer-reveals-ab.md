  <p>A federal appeals court has ruled that Sigal Chattah was unlawfully appointed as Nevada's top federal law enforcement officer, marking a major legal defeat for executive branch efforts to install political allies without Senate confirmation. The unanimous decision from the U.S. Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals centers on how federal vacancies are filled, shining a spotlight on the delicate constitutional checks and balances governing the U.S. Department of Justice.</p>

      <li><strong>The Impact:</strong> Chattah can no longer oversee federal prosecutions in Nevada, though existing indictments remain intact pending further legal maneuvering.</li>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the Ninth Circuit</h2>
    <p>On Monday, a three-judge panel of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals upheld a lower court's decision, ruling that Sigal Chattah's continued leadership of the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Nevada violated federal law. The legal challenge originated in September, when the Nevada federal public defender's office sued to disqualify Chattah, arguing that the administration executed a series of unlawful personnel maneuvers to keep her atop the office.</p>

    <p>President Donald Trump initially appointed Chattah as interim U.S. attorney, a position restricted to a 180-day term. On the eve of that expiration, Chattah was designated as first assistant U.S. attorney—a maneuver the administration argued allowed her to serve an additional 210 days as acting U.S. attorney. However, the appeals court dismantled that logic, writing that the Attorney General cannot bypass the Senate-confirmation requirement by designating someone as a first assistant to an already vacant office.</p>
    <p>While the ruling bars Chattah from supervising ongoing prosecutions, the court noted it lacked jurisdiction to completely nullify all indictments secured during her tenure, leaving a complex procedural trail for defendants and defense attorneys to navigate.</p>

  <section id="legal-explainer">
    <h2>A Legal Rights Perspective: The Limits of Executive Appointments</h2>
    <p>From a legal rights standpoint, this case is not merely about political personnel in Nevada; it strikes at the heart of the separation of powers enshrined in the U.S. Constitution. The Framers deliberately designed the Appointments Clause (Article II, Section 2) to require Senate confirmation for principal officers, including U.S. attorneys, to ensure public accountability and legislative oversight.</p>
    <p>Statutes like the Federal Vacancies Reform Act (FVRA) provide narrow, temporary pathways for filling unexpected government vacancies without immediate confirmation. Yet, as federal courts in New Jersey, Virginia, California, and now Nevada have repeatedly found, executive branch agencies have sometimes pushed those temporary mechanisms past their legal breaking point.</p>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>"The executive branch cannot create an endless loop of temporary leadership to bypass the constitutional requirement of Senate confirmation."</p>
    </div>

    <p>This ruling reinforces a fundamental principle of administrative and criminal procedure: prosecutorial authority must flow from a lawfully constituted office. When an unconfirmed or improperly appointed official exercises the immense coercive power of the state—bringing indictments, freezing assets, and conducting investigations—defendants retain the legal right to challenge the legitimacy of that authority. While this ruling does not automatically throw out past indictments, it validates the procedural safeguards designed to keep executive power within statutory bounds.</p>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Can Sigal Chattah appeal this ruling?</summary>
      <p>Yes. The administration has the option to appeal the Ninth Circuit's decision to the U.S. Supreme Court, though it has not pursued Supreme Court reviews in similar vacancy defeats in other states.</p>
    </details>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>What happens to the criminal cases filed during Chattah's tenure?</summary>
      <p>The appeals court ruled that Chattah can no longer supervise these prosecutions, but it declined to decide whether past indictments should be completely nullified, leaving those challenges to lower courts.</p>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Who will permanently replace Chattah?</summary>
