    <h2>What Happened in the Himalayas?</h2>
    <p>At 8:37 AM on a Wednesday, an enormous section of a glacier in the Himalayas sheared free and crashed onto the valley floor in Nepal's Langtang National Park, roughly 60 kilometers north of Kathmandu. The impact was so violent that the United States Geological Survey initially registered the resulting tremor as a magnitude 4.4 earthquake, later upgrading it to a 5.2 as scientists confirmed it was caused by cascading rock and ice.</p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Sudden Disaster</h2>
    <p>When an event like the Nepal and Tibet flash flood unfolds in seconds, the human brain undergoes a massive psychological shockwave. Decades of behavioral research into emergency response demonstrate that humans rarely react to sudden, catastrophic threats with immediate, perfectly calculated action. Instead, we experience predictable cognitive phases: disbelief, a desperate search for normalcy, and finally, hyper-accelerated decision-making.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias and Cognitive Dissonance</h3>
    <p>In the opening moments of the disaster, videos showed people attempting to process the impossible: a wall of mud and debris roaring down a mountain valley. This hesitation stems from normalcy bias—our brain's innate tendency to minimize warning signs and assume things will continue as they normally do. When survivors heard the initial roar, many initially doubted their own senses, mistaking a catastrophic glacial collapse for a routine rumble until the physical evidence made disbelief impossible.</p>

    <h3>The Instinct of Action: Fight, Flight, or Cling</h3>
    <p>Once normalcy bias shatters, adrenaline floods the system. Accounts from survivors like Keshav Prasad Baral, who attempted to save his wife before she was swept away, and Bibek Kumal, who credited a mango tree for saving his life, highlight the raw, instinctual drive to secure physical survival. In split-second crises, higher-order cognitive planning gives way to immediate, tactile reactions: running for high ground, grabbing onto branches, or pulling loved ones toward safety.</p>
