<h3>Why Tone Matters More Than Talking Points</h3>
<p>Children look to parents as emotional barometers. If we react to primary election results with deep despair or unbridled rage, children internalize the idea that elections threaten their safety. Conversely, when we discuss election outcomes with steady curiosity—acknowledging different viewpoints without personalizing animosity—we model emotional regulation and civic resilience.</p>

<h3>For Young Children (Ages 5–9)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep it simple:</strong> Describe voting as a giant community poll where everyone gets a turn to pick helpers.</li>
<li><strong>Limit exposure:</strong> Turn off continuous cable news loops in the background during dinner time to prevent unnecessary sensory overload.</li>
<li><strong>Focus on helpers:</strong> Remind them that many people are working hard to make their community a safe, fair place to live.</li>
</ul>

<h3>For Tweens and Teens (Ages 10–18)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ask open-ended questions:</strong> Instead of lecturing, start by asking: "Have you heard anything about the elections at school today? What do you think about it?"</li>
<li><strong>Teach media literacy:</strong> Discuss how different news outlets frame stories and encourage them to look for verified facts rather than emotional headlines.</li>
<li><strong>Validate respectful disagreement:</strong> Model how friends and family members can hold differing political beliefs while maintaining mutual respect and kindness.</li>
</ul>
