  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 2rem;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3748; font-size: 1.5rem; margin-bottom: 1rem; border-bottom: 2px solid #edf2f7; padding-bottom: 0.5rem;">What Happened in the Texas Courtroom</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.6; color: #4a5568; margin-bottom: 1rem;">
      On Tuesday, US District Judge David Hittner issued a decisive ruling blocking Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton from enforcing Senate Bill 12 (SB 12). Passed initially in March 2023, the legislation sought to penalize drag performers who dance suggestively or wear specific prosthetics on public property or in front of anyone under the age of 18.
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.6; color: #4a5568; margin-bottom: 1rem;">
      Judge Hittner concluded that the measure improperly restricts speech and runs afoul of the First Amendment of the US Constitution. The legal saga has bounced through federal courts since late 2023, when Hittner first declared the law unconstitutional before the 5th US Circuit Court of Appeals temporarily unblocked it in 2025. With this latest injunction, the battle lines are drawn once more, as Paxton has vowed to appeal the decision.
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.6; color: #4a5568; margin-bottom: 1rem;">
      In his written opinion, Hittner pointed out the vast and unpredictable reach of the statute's phrasing. He noted that terms like "prurient interest in sex" were open-ended enough to sweep up everyday public occurrences, live theater, cheerleading, and even celebrated cultural icons like the late Dolly Parton, Lady Gaga, or Miley Cyrus. For those offended by such performances, Hittner offered a simple, time-tested constitutional remedy: "just don't go."
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="legal-perspective" style="margin-bottom: 2rem;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3748; font-size: 1.5rem; margin-bottom: 1rem; border-bottom: 2px solid #edf2f7; padding-bottom: 0.5rem;">A Legal Rights Perspective on SB 12</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.6; color: #4a5568; margin-bottom: 1rem;">
      From a constitutional standpoint, SB 12 collided head-on with established legal standards regarding vagueness and overbreadth. In American jurisprudence, a law regulating speech must provide clear, explicit notice of what conduct is forbidden so that ordinary citizens can conform their behavior without guessing. When a statute is drafted so broadly that it criminalizes routine artistic or athletic expression—such as school cheerleading or mainstream pop concerts—it fails the rigorous scrutiny required by the First Amendment.
    </p>

    <div class="ncg-quote-box" style="background: #ebf8ff; border-left: 4px solid #3182ce; padding: 1rem 1.25rem; border-radius: 4px; margin: 1.5rem 0; font-style: italic; color: #2b6cb0;">
      "There are 'erotic' elements in countless popular performances that could be subject to both civil and criminal penalties under SB 12." — US District Judge David Hittner
    </div>

    <p style="line-height: 1.6; color: #4a5568; margin-bottom: 1rem;">
      Furthermore, the legal challenge highlights the delicate balance between protecting minors and safeguarding expressive liberties. While states possess a legitimate interest in regulating obscene material, that power is bounded by clear Supreme Court definitions, such as the historic <em>Miller v. California</em> test, which requires work to lack serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value before it can be deemed legally obscene. Because SB 12 bypassed these careful distinctions and imposed broad civil and criminal penalties, it invited federal intervention.
    </p>
