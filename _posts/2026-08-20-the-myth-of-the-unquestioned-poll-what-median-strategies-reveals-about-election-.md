  <p class="ncg-lead">When Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass shared social media numbers from a newly surfaced group called Median Strategies, she thought she was highlighting campaign momentum—only to find out days later that the poll was entirely fabricated. As elections grow increasingly digitized, the recent scandal surrounding fake polls in California, Wisconsin, and Nevada exposes dangerous vulnerabilities in our information ecosystem and proves why basic verification is more critical than ever.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Anatomy of a Fabricated Poll</h2>
  
  <p>In mid-March, a previously unknown organization calling itself Median Strategies posted purported poll results across three separate states online. The fabricated data claimed to measure support in three prominent Democratic primary and general races: a gubernatorial race in Wisconsin, a gubernatorial contest in Nevada, and a mayoral race in Los Angeles.</p>

  <p>In the Los Angeles contest, the fake survey claimed that incumbent Mayor Karen Bass was leading progressive councilmember Nithya Raman by roughly 12 percentage points. Eager to project strength in a competitive race, the Bass campaign shared the numbers on social media, writing that the poll was evidence of growing momentum. The numbers were even picked up by local reporting before independent fact-checking and scrutiny from major aggregators unraveled the deception.</p>

  <p>Median Strategies abruptly shut down its website and issued a vague statement admitting the results were fake, framing the stunt as a "short-term social experiment" to see how unverified polling enters the political ecosystem. Despite the admission, the group left behind zero explanation of who funded the experiment or what their ultimate goal was, leaving campaigns, journalists, and voters to deal with the fallout.</p>

  <h2 id="how-to-respond">How Should Readers Respond?</h2>

  <p>Protecting yourself against political disinformation and fake polling doesn't require a degree in data science. You can adopt three simple habits when encountering surprising election numbers:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>Check the Methodology:</strong> If a poll does not disclose who conducted it, how many people were surveyed, what questions were asked, and how the sample was weighted, treat it as fictional until proven otherwise.</li>
    <li><strong>Look for Independent Aggregators:</strong> Check whether reputable nonpartisan aggregators like The New York Times, Real Clear Politics, or FiftyPlusOne have included the survey in their databases. If they skipped it, there is usually a serious reason.</li>
    <li><strong>Pause Before You Share:</strong> Be especially skeptical of polls that instantly confirm your preferred candidate's landslide victory or your worst fears. Fabricators rely on confirmation bias to make their numbers spread virally before fact-checkers can intervene.</li>
  </ul>
