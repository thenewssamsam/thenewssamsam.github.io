Instead, voters soundly rejected numerous incumbents and caucus-aligned candidates.</p>

    <h3>1. The Fatigue of Constant Conflict and Groupthink</h3>
    <p>In political science and social psychology, groups that operate under rigid ideological filters frequently fall victim to groupthink. Inside a tight-knit political faction, members reinforce each other's certainty, gradually losing touch with how their actions are perceived by the broader public. When legislative sessions are defined by endless conflict—such as high-profile battles over funding for the University of Wyoming or ethics controversies involving campaign contributions—ordinary citizens experience cognitive fatigue.</p>

    <h3>2. Loss Aversion and the Protection of Community Staples</h3>
    <p>Psychologically, people are more heavily motivated by the fear of losing what they value than by the promise of abstract ideological victories. When the Freedom Caucus targeted foundational state institutions like the University of Wyoming and the Wyoming Business Council for deep budget cuts, they inadvertently triggered <em>loss aversion</em> among everyday residents. For many citizens, these institutions represent community stability and economic security. Threatening them felt less like reform and more like an unnecessary risk to their daily lives.</p>
