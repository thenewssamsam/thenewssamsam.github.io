    <h1 class="ncg-main-title">Iran and Oman's Temporary Hormuz Route: What a Financial Lens Reveals About Global Energy Shocks</h1>
    <p class="ncg-lead-paragraph">The fragile announcement of a temporary transit corridor through the Strait of Hormuz by Iran and Oman offers a slight reprieve to strained global markets, yet it leaves the underlying economic plumbing of international energy trade deeply vulnerable. For everyday consumers and global markets alike, this diplomatic maneuver highlights just how quickly geopolitical friction translates into painful spikes at the fuel pump and grocery store checkout.</p>
  </header>

  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2 class="ncg-heading">What Happened in the Strait of Hormuz?</h2>
    <p>Iran’s Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi announced that Tehran and Oman have forged an agreement on a temporary 7-mile-wide (11.3km) maritime transit route through the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz. However, the announcement comes with severe caveats: Tehran insists the waterway is not fully reopened and will remain under strict Iranian surveillance, with vessels subject to prior authorization.</p>
    <p>This development follows months of severe disruption sparked by the US-Israel war on Iran that began earlier in the year, effectively shutting down a chokepoint through which over 20 percent of global oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) normally flows. Compounding the tension, Washington recently escalated economic pressures by rolling out fresh sanctions targeting entities facilitating Iranian oil sales, leaving commercial shippers caught in a high-stakes geopolitical tug-of-war.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="financial-impact" class="ncg-section">
    <h2 class="ncg-heading">The Financial Mechanics: Who Pays When Chokepoints Close?</h2>
    <p>When studying geopolitical conflicts through a financial lens, the core question is always about transmission mechanisms—how a disruption in a remote stretch of water turns into higher expenses for ordinary families halfway across the world. The Strait of Hormuz is not merely a regional shipping lane; it is the financial carotid artery of global energy markets. Before hostilities began, Brent crude hovered around $66 a barrel, supporting stable manufacturing, transportation, and utility costs.</p>
