    <p>Tens of thousands of residents across northwest Indiana are still grappling with the realities of life without electricity more than a week after a ferocious storm swept through the region with 99 mph winds. Northern Indiana Public Service Co. (NIPSCO) reported that approximately 75,000 customers remained without power by late afternoon, with heavily impacted areas like Gary facing rolling restoration timelines stretching deep into August. For families tossing out freezers full of spoiled meat, spending small fortunes on daily generator fuel, or driving across state lines simply to secure ice to keep insulin cold, the crisis is no longer just a logistical emergency. It has evolved into an endurance test of the human mind.</p>

    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: A Historic Storm and Lingering Darkness</h2>
    <p>The severe weather event began on August 11, unleashing destructive winds that claimed seven lives—including a 4-year-old child, three men, and three women—and initially plunging roughly 300,000 NIPSCO customers into darkness. While power has been restored to many, the remaining outages are concentrated heavily in Lake County, including Gary, where roughly 300 main poles and 44 major power transmission poles require complete reconstruction rather than simple repairs.</p>
    <p>NIPSCO executive Melody Birmingham emphasized the immense scope of the structural rebuild at a recent news conference, noting that crews are essentially replacing foundational electrical systems rather than fixing isolated lines. Yet, for residents like Myra Carden of Whiting—whose home sustained no yard damage from the storm itself, yet suffered a frustrating second blackout after power was temporarily restored—the day-to-day reality is marked by confusion, repetitive automated outage responses, and mounting exhaustion.</p>

    <h2 id="psychology-perspective">The Psychology of Disaster Fatigue and Loss of Control</h2>
    <p>When a crisis hits, human psychology is well-equipped for an acute emergency. Adrenaline spikes, communities rally, and people mobilize to weather the immediate storm. However, when the crisis stretches past a week, a distinct mental shift occurs: acute stress transitions into chronic disaster fatigue.</p>

When the simple act of flipping a light switch, preserving groceries, or keeping a home at a comfortable temperature becomes impossible, the brain registers this ongoing disruption as a continuous low-grade threat.</p>

    <h2 id="reader-response">How Should Readers Respond?</h2>
    <p>Navigating disaster fatigue requires both practical preparation and emotional self-care. Here are concrete ways to respond if you or someone you know is facing prolonged disruption:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Protect Your Routine:</strong> Even without electricity, try to maintain regular sleep schedules, meal times, and physical movement to ground your nervous system.</li>
      <li><strong>Check on Vulnerable Neighbors:</strong> Elderly residents or neighbors relying on medical devices like insulin storage may need immediate practical assistance with ice, charging, or transportation.</li>
      <li><strong>Filter Information Wisely:</strong> Rely on official utility updates and verified municipal announcements rather than unverified social media claims that heighten anxiety.</li>
      <li><strong>Build an Emergency Buffer:</strong> Use this event as a prompt to review your household emergency plan, keeping backup batteries, nonperishable food, and medical supplies updated.</li>
    </ul>
