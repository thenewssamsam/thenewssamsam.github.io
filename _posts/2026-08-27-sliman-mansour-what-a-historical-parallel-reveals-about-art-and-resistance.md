  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened</h2>
    <p>The funeral for celebrated Palestinian artist Sliman Mansour recently took place at a packed church in Birzeit, near Ramallah in the occupied West Bank. Mansour passed away this week at the age of 79, leaving behind a monumental legacy as one of the most prominent visual chroniclers of the Palestinian experience. Best known for his vibrant paintings of olive groves, citrus trees, and traditional cultural symbols, Mansour captured the resilience and identity of a people navigating decades of nationalist struggle and displacement.</p>
    
    <p>Born in Birzeit in 1947—just a year before the establishment of the State of Israel and the events of the Nakba—Mansour studied fine art in Jerusalem before developing a deeply distinctive style. Over a prolific career spanning decades, he co-founded the League of Palestinian Artists and the International Academy of Art, while also creating children's illustrations, cartoons, and profound public murals. His works are housed in prestigious institutions worldwide, including the British Museum and the Guggenheim Museum in Abu Dhabi, yet he remained deeply committed to making his art accessible to everyday people through affordable prints and community-focused projects.</p>

    <h2>A Historical Parallel Perspective</h2>
    <p>When looking at the life and career of Sliman Mansour through the lens of history, we see a recurring pattern that transcends the specific geography of the West Bank: the unbreakable bond between cultural production and political survival under occupation or systemic pressure. Throughout history, whenever physical autonomy has been restricted or suppressed, communities have consistently turned to art not merely as decoration, but as an archive of memory and a quiet form of defiance.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">The Power of the Symbolic Archive</h3>
    <p>In the 1970s and 1980s, when Israeli security forces frequently confiscated political posters and scrutinized gallery openings, Mansour and his contemporaries did not stop creating. Instead, they adapted. As documented in accounts of his work, they shifted toward deeply symbolic imagery—such as his iconic 1973 painting <em>Camel of Hardship</em>, which depicted an elderly porter carrying Jerusalem on his back—so that human experiences could be communicated through metaphor even when direct political expression was heavily policed.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">The Mud and Straw of Resistance</h3>
    <p>A striking historical parallel emerged during the first intifada in 1987, when Mansour and the New Vision collective enacted a deliberate boycott of imported, Israeli-made art supplies like oil and acrylic paints. Stripped of conventional commercial materials, they turned to the earth itself—experimenting with mud, straw, and henna. This pivot is remarkably reminiscent of historical movements worldwide where marginalized populations utilize indigenous, locally sourced elements to assert their rootedness. By crafting surfaces from cracked mud and straw, Mansour reminded viewers of deep history, time, and physical belonging to the soil.</p>

  <section id="closing-takeaway" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>Closing Takeaway</h2>
    <p>Sliman Mansour leaves behind a legacy that extends far beyond galleries in Paris, Doha, or London. By using his brush, and eventually the mud and straw of the earth itself, he ensured that the landscape, struggles, and identity of his people were recorded for generations to come. His life stands as a testament to the fact that while physical structures can be challenged or altered, the cultural memory preserved through dedicated art remains remarkably difficult to erase.</p>
