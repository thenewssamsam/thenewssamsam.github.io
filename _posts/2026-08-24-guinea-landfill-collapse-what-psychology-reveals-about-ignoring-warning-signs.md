  <p class="ncg-lead">When a massive mound of rubbish collapsed at the Dar Es Salam landfill in Conakry, Guinea, overnight after heavy rains, it left at least 30 people dead and homes buried before dawn. Behind the immediate tragedy lies a troubling human pattern: the dangerous gap between recognizing a severe risk and taking definitive action before catastrophe strikes.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened at the Dar Es Salam Landfill?</h2>
  
  <p>The disaster struck unexpectedly in the dark hours of the morning. Heavy seasonal rains triggered a catastrophic collapse of towering trash heaps at the primary garbage site in Guinea's capital. The resulting avalanche of refuse completely buried nearby informal housing structures while residents were asleep.</p>

  <p>Emergency responders and the Military Engineering Battalion quickly deployed excavators to search the debris field. Officials confirmed that at least 30 individuals lost their lives, while another six sustained severe injuries. Local authorities noted that the hazardous site was actually slated for official closure on the very day of the disaster, and the government had previously attempted to mandate the expulsion of nearby residents due to well-documented fears of structural collapses.</p>

  <h3 id="normalcy-bias">Normalcy Bias and Habituation</h3>
  <p>Normalcy bias is a mental state that causes people to underestimate the possibility of a disaster and its possible effects. When families live next to a massive landfill for years, enduring seasonal rains without fatal incidents, the brain normalizes the danger. The mountain of garbage stops looking like an impending avalanche and starts looking like part of the landscape.</p>

  <h3 id="the-economics-of-risk">The Economics of Risk versus Desperation</h3>
  <p>It is rarely simple to evacuate informal settlements. Economic survival often forces individuals to live where housing is accessible, regardless of environmental hazards. Cognitive dissonance allows people to minimize risks when viable alternatives simply do not exist. They acknowledge the danger intellectually, but the immediate necessity of shelter outweighs a distant, probabilistic threat.</p>
