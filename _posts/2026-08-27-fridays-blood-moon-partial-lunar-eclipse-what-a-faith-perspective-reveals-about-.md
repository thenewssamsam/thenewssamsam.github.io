    <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 40px;">
        <h2 style="font-size: 1.6rem; color: #2c3e50; margin-bottom: 20px; border-left: 4px solid #3498db; padding-left: 12px;">What Happened? The 2026 Partial Lunar Eclipse</h2>
        <p>In the early hours of Friday morning, millions of astronomy enthusiasts across the West Country will have a unique opportunity to witness a striking celestial event. Following closely on the heels of the UK's most dramatic partial solar eclipse in nearly thirty years, the Sun, Earth, and Moon are set to align once again in the night sky.</p>
        <p>According to reports from the BBC, a partial lunar eclipse will take place when the Moon moves directly into Earth's darkest shadow, known as the umbra. Because the Earth blocks direct sunlight from reaching the lunar surface, the filtered light refracting through our atmosphere casts an eerie, reddish hue across the Moon, creating what observers popularly call a "Blood Moon."</p>
        
        <div style="background: #fdfbf7; border-left: 4px solid #d4af37; padding: 18px; margin: 25px 0; border-radius: 4px;">
            <p style="margin: 0; font-size: 1.05rem; font-style: italic; color: #444444;">&ldquo;I think they [Blood Moons] have a certain sort of magical feeling about them.&rdquo;</p>
            <p style="margin: 8px 0 0 0; font-size: 0.9rem; color: #666666; font-weight: 600;">&mdash; Jonathan Hall, Bath Astronomers Society</p>
        </div>

        <p>Adding to the visual spectacle, the Moon will sit very low on the southwestern horizon during the peak of the eclipse. This position triggers a well-known psychological and optical phenomenon called the &ldquo;Moon Illusion.&rdquo; Because the Moon is low to the horizon, surrounded by terrestrial reference points like trees and buildings, the human eye perceives it as much larger than it appears high in the night sky.</p>
