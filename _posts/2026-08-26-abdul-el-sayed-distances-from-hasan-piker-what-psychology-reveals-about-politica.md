  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in Michigan</h2>
    <p>In high-stakes modern politics, finding a megaphone to reach younger voters is essential. But as Democratic U.S. Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed recently discovered in Michigan, digital allies who command massive online audiences can quickly become a double-edged sword. El-Sayed formally distanced himself from progressive commentator Hasan Piker following controversial remarks Piker made regarding pro-Israel rhetoric and American Jews during a livestream.</p>

    <p>According to the Associated Press, Piker argued during his broadcast that some pro-Israel rhetoric pushed by Jewish institutions risks creating a dangerous backlash against American Jews. The remarks drew swift and fierce condemnation from figures across the political spectrum, including House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, who labeled the comments dangerous and antisemitic. State-level figures, such as Jewish Democratic House candidate Jeremy Moss, also pushed back forcefully against the commentary.</p>

    <p>El-Sayed issued a statement emphasizing that antisemitism is a scourge that must be addressed, clarifying that nobody speaks for his campaign except his official spokespeople. While Republican opponent Mike Rogers argued the response was inadequate, Democratic strategists noted that managing the fallout from online surrogates is one of the trickiest tightropes in modern battleground politics. Beyond the immediate campaign friction, the episode highlights a fascinating behavioral pattern about how human beings perceive guilt by association.</p>

    <h3>1. The Halo Effect and Its Reverse</h3>
    <p>In psychology, the halo effect occurs when our overall impression of a person influences how we feel about their character. Early in primary campaigns, candidates often lean into the positive "halo" of popular digital creators to signal authenticity and reach tech-savvy demographics. However, when that surrogate stumbles or makes inflammatory remarks, the psychological mechanism reverses into what researchers might call a "horns effect." Voters suddenly project the surrogate's controversial statements onto the candidate, assuming an ideological contamination that may not reflect the candidate's actual beliefs.</p>

Viewers tend to merge separate identities into a single monolithic block. This cognitive shortcut is precisely what El-Sayed attempted to break by declaring firmly that no outside pundit speaks for his platform.</p>
