Just two weeks into his presidency, Colombia's far-right leader Abelardo de la Espriella has ordered an aggressive immigration crackdown targeting irregular migrants and criminal groups, echoing Donald Trump's "America First" rhetoric while the country still reels from a devastating 7.4-magnitude earthquake. But beneath the political posturing lies a predictable human reaction to collective vulnerability.</p>

<section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
<h2>What Happened: The Decree in Barranquilla</h2>
<p>During a cabinet meeting in Barranquilla—which has effectively functioned as an alternative capital—President Abelardo de la Espriella announced that joint police and migration operations would begin immediately. The millionaire former lawyer outlined a two-pronged enforcement strategy: first, hunting down individuals accused of committing crimes; second, detaining and immediately deporting migrants who have failed to regularize their status.</p>

<p>The announcement arrives while Colombia continues to process the horrific aftermath of an August 10 earthquake measuring 7.4 in magnitude, which claimed over 300 lives and left more than 30,000 homes in ruins. De la Espriella invoked an explicit "Colombians first, Colombians second and Colombians third" doctrine, aligning himself ideologically with regional figures like Argentina's Javier Milei and Chile's José Antonio Kast.</p>

<h2>The Psychology of Crisis and Ingroup Favoritism</h2>
<p>To understand why immigration crackdowns so reliably follow national disasters and economic instability, we have to look past political manifestos and examine basic human behavioral science. When societies experience profound collective trauma—such as the loss of 30,000 homes and hundreds of lives in an earthquake—the human nervous system experiences a spike in existential dread. In psychology, this triggers defensive cognitive mechanisms.</p>

<h3 class="ncg-h3">1. Normalcy Bias and the Search for Control</h3>
<p>When an unpredictable event like a 7.4-magnitude earthquake shatters the illusion of safety, people experience intense cognitive dissonance. Nature is indifferent and uncontrollable. Humans, however, desperately crave agency. Directing state machinery toward a visible, human target—such as undocumented migrants—creates an illusion of control. It replaces the terrifying chaos of tectonic plates shifting with the comforting, if simplistic, narrative of law and order.</p>

<h3 class="ncg-h3">2. Ingroup Favoritism and Outgroup Derogation</h3>
<p>In times of scarcity, evolutionary psychology activates strict tribal boundaries. The emergence of the "Colombians first, second, and third" slogan is a textbook example of hyper-ingroup favoritism. By dividing the population into strict binary categories—citizens versus outsiders—leaders tap into ancient survival instincts. Evolutionary history taught our ancestors that sharing limited resources with outgroup members during a famine or disaster could threaten group survival. Modern politicians weaponize this evolutionary wiring, framing vulnerable populations as competitors for scarce rebuilding funds.</p>

<h3 class="ncg-h3">3. Moral Panic as a Distraction Tool</h3>
<p>Governments facing massive structural challenges—like rebuilding housing for tens of thousands of displaced citizens—frequently utilize moral panic. By conflating administrative immigration status with violent criminality ("terrorists"), leadership creates an urgent moral crusade. Psychologically, it unites a fractured populace against an external scapegoat, temporarily masking the state's logistical shortcomings in disaster recovery.</p>

<h2>How Should Readers Respond?</h2>
<p>Discussions surrounding national borders and immigration policy can easily dissolve into polarized shouting matches on social media. Here are three grounded, constructive ways to engage with news of this magnitude:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Examine Your Own Emotional Triggers:</strong> Recognize when news of a crisis evokes anxiety in your own life. Notice whether that anxiety makes you more prone to cynicism and hostility toward outsiders.</li>
<li><strong>Support Local Humanitarian Efforts:</strong> Look for grassroots organizations in your region or across Latin America that provide legal aid, food security, and earthquake relief to both displaced citizens and vulnerable migrants without discrimination.</li>
<li><strong>Demand Nuance in Public Discourse:</strong> Resist simplistic slogans that reduce complex human realities to political footballs. Encourage civil, evidence-based conversations about immigration reform that respect both national sovereignty and human dignity.</li>

<section id="conclusion" class="ncg-section">
<h2>One Last Thought</h2>
<p>Crises reveal what a society is made of. When earthquakes shatter homes and political leaders promise iron-fist solutions, it is easy to surrender to the tribal instinct of guarding our own at all costs. But a truly resilient society is measured not by how effectively it excludes the vulnerable, but by how justly and compassionately it navigates its own darkest hours. True strength does not close its eyes to human need; it finds a way to uphold justice and mercy at the exact same time.</p>
<p class="ncg-closing-line"><em>In times of shaking, our highest calling is to build bridges, not walls.</em></p>
