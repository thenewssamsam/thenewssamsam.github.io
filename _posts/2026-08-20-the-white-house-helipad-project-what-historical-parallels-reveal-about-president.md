  <section id="introduction">
    <p>President Donald Trump recently transformed the White House South Lawn into a construction tour, showcasing a new granite helipad and an expansive ballroom currently under development. While the president framed the project as a matter of strength and durability, the focus on these structural additions amidst ongoing national challenges invites a broader conversation about how leaders attempt to leave a permanent mark on the nation's most iconic residence.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened?</h2>
    <p>During a Wednesday press appearance, President Trump gave reporters a firsthand look at the construction of a new granite helipad on the South Lawn. He emphasized the material's durability, noting its ability to withstand significant pressure. Beyond the helipad, the president highlighted progress on a $400 million ballroom, which he argues will provide critical security enhancements. Despite legal challenges regarding congressional oversight, the president expressed confidence that the project would reach completion and serve as a lasting fixture of the White House grounds.</p>

    <p>Thomas Jefferson, an architect in his own right, famously made significant modifications to the White House, including the addition of terraces and the installation of indoor plumbing. Like today's projects, Jefferson's work was sometimes met with skepticism regarding cost and intent, yet these modifications eventually became integrated into the building's permanent character.</p>

    <h3>The Roosevelt Expansion</h3>
    <p>The most significant structural change in the 20th century was the creation of the West Wing by Theodore Roosevelt. Before his intervention, the president's office was located within the residence itself. By moving the executive office to a new structure, Roosevelt physically separated the home from the seat of power, a move that fundamentally altered the presidency. Today, projects like the new ballroom—which the administration describes as a "military complex slash ballroom"—reflect a similar impulse to adapt the grounds to modern perceived needs, whether for security or executive function.</p>
