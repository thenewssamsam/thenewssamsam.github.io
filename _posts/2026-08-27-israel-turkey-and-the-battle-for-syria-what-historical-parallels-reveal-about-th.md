<p>When Syrian and Israeli representatives sat down for mediated talks in Jordan last weekend, the meeting marked a quiet, tense milestone. Sparked by an Israeli airstrike on the Abu Duhur military airfield in Syria's Idlib Province, the diplomatic scramble revealed a deeper, unfolding struggle: a high-stakes strategic rivalry between Israel and a resurgent Turkey over who will ultimately shape the security order of a post-Assad Middle East.</p>

<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Abu Duhur Airstrike and Jordan Talks</h2>
<p>The immediate catalyst for the diplomatic alarm was an August 18 Israeli attack targeting the Abu Duhur military airfield. According to Syrian reports, the strike damaged the runway and storage facilities without causing casualties. Jerusalem defended the action by asserting that Syria had violated security agreements by allegedly allowing Turkish troops to utilize the facility—an allegation both Damascus and Ankara quickly denied.</p>

<p>The United States criticized the strike as an unnecessary escalation, offering rhetorical backing to its allies in Ankara and Damascus even as Washington actively recalibrates its own relationship with the new Syrian leadership under President Ahmad al-Sharaa. Yet beneath the immediate diplomatic posturing lies a profound structural clash. Israel views any permanent Turkish military foothold in Syria as a critical red line, arguing that a sovereign, unchecked Syrian state could obstruct its military freedom of action—particularly its corridor for targeting hostile proxies linked to Iran. Meanwhile, Turkey views a stable, unified Syria as essential to its southern border security and its broader ambition to project regional influence.</p>

<h2>A Historical Perspective: The Mechanics of Vacuum Politics</h2>
<p>History rarely repeats itself neatly, but it often rhymes with alarming precision. To understand why Syria has suddenly become the flashpoint for an Israeli-Turkish cold war, we can look back at classic patterns of imperial collapse and power vacuums. Throughout modern history, whenever a dominant, long-standing central authority implodes—much like the Bashar Assad regime did in December 2024—neighboring regional powers rarely stand by.</p>

<p>Consider the classic geopolitical parallel of post-Ottoman state formation in the early 20th century, or more recently, the fractured power dynamics following the 2003 vacuum in Iraq. In this light, Israel's insistence on buffer zones and preemptive airstrikes echoes historical doctrines of strategic depth, where small or mid-sized states seek to control neighboring territory to neutralize long-term threats.</p>
