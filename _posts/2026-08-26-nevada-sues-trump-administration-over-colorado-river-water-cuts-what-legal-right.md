  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p>When the Department of the Interior announced sweeping new operating guidelines requiring up to 20% water cuts across the lower basin states of Arizona, California, and Nevada, it did not take long for the legal fallout to begin. Nevada became the first state out of the gate, filing a federal lawsuit challenging a plan its leaders call fundamentally flawed and catastrophic for Southern Nevada.</p>
    
    <p>This high-stakes legal challenge brings the mounting pressures of climate change, regional politics, and constitutional water allocation rights into sharp focus. For millions of residents relying on the dwindling reservoirs of the Colorado River, this courtroom clash is much more than a routine bureaucratic dispute—it is a fight over who bears the burden of an unfolding ecological reality.</p>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened? The Colorado River Mandate and Nevada's Response</h2>
    <p>The latest flashpoint in the decades-long Colorado River crisis arrived when federal regulators stepped in after years of stalled negotiations among the seven states dependent on the waterway. The Department of the Interior’s new framework mandates severe reductions for lower basin states over the next two years to combat record-low levels at both Lake Mead and Lake Powell.</p>

When compact negotiations fail, federal regulators step in to prevent catastrophic reservoir collapse. Yet, federal power is bounded by statutory limitations, raising questions about whether regulatory distribution formulas arbitrarily discriminate against specific states.</p>

Lower basin states counter that forcing them to absorb 100% of the active federal shortage management plan violates equitable apportionment principles.</p>
