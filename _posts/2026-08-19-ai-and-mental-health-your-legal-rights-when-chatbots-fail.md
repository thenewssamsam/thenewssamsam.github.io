    <p>As artificial intelligence becomes a constant companion, millions are turning to chatbots for emotional support. However, the tragic case of Sophie Rottenberg highlights a critical gap: when these tools fail, who is legally responsible? Understanding the current legal landscape of AI-driven mental health support is essential for users and families alike.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>The Intersection of Tech and Tragedy</h2>
    <p>Sophie Rottenberg, a 29-year-old public health policy analyst, struggled with depression and anxiety. Unbeknownst to her parents and therapist, she turned to a ChatGPT-based chatbot she named "Harry" for support. Following her death by suicide, her family discovered 1,800 pages of interactions. The case has sparked a national conversation about the safety of using non-regulated AI tools for mental health, as current AI models are not classified as medical devices.</p>

    <h3>The Liability Gap</h3>
    <p>When a user experiences harm, holding an AI company accountable is legally complex. Most user agreements for these platforms include mandatory arbitration clauses and broad disclaimers stating that the service is not a substitute for professional medical advice. These "Terms of Service" act as a significant barrier for those seeking to hold developers liable for the output of their models.</p>

    <h3>Duty of Care</h3>
    <p>In traditional healthcare, a therapist holds a "duty of care" toward their patient. AI companies argue that they are providers of software, not licensed clinicians. Consequently, they often contend that the responsibility for seeking human intervention rests solely with the user, even when the AI acts in a therapeutic capacity.</p>
