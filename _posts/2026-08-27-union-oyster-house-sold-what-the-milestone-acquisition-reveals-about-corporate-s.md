    <h1 class="ncg-title">Union Oyster House Sold: What the Milestone Acquisition Reveals About Corporate Scale and Historic Legacy</h1>
    <p class="ncg-subtitle">When America's oldest continuously operating restaurant changes hands, it forces a larger conversation about how local institutions survive in an era of corporate consolidation.</p>
  </header>

  <section class="ncg-section" id="news-hook">
    <p>For two centuries, the <a href="https://www.boston.com/food/food-news/2026/08/26/union-oyster-house-owner-shares-staff-internal-letter-changes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Union Oyster House</a> has stood as a culinary anchor on Boston's Union Street, serving everyone from Daniel Webster to generations of local families. But even national historic landmarks are not immune to the shifting tides of the modern economy. Houston billionaire Tilman Fertitta and his hospitality empire, Landry's, are moving to acquire the legendary establishment, pending final licensing board approvals.</p>
    <p>For the Milano family, who have owned the restaurant since 1970, this marks the end of a remarkable half-century chapter. For the restaurant's 143-person staff and legions of devoted patrons, the transition has sparked anxious questions about what happens when a local institution is absorbed into a multibillion-dollar portfolio that already spans hundreds of properties, casinos, and sports franchises.</p>
  </section>

    <h2>What Happened at the Union Oyster House?</h2>
    <p>The acquisition details emerged as reports trickled out in late August 2026, confirmed by 82-year-old president Joseph Milano in statements and an internal letter to his staff. If approved by the Boston Licensing Board and the state Alcoholic Beverages Control Commission, the transaction is expected to close in September, making the Fertitta family only the fifth owners since the restaurant first opened its doors in 1826 as Atwood & Bacon's.</p>
