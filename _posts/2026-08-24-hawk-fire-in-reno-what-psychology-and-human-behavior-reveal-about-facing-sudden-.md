  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The Rapid Ascent of the Hawk Fire</h2>
    <p>It started innocently enough on a Saturday morning as a small bushfire, an occurrence familiar to those living in the drought-weary American West. But fueled by bone-dry conditions, a record lack of winter snow, and sudden, severe wind shifts, the Hawk Fire exploded across the Peavine Peak area of the Humboldt-Toiyabe National Forest.</p>
    <p>Within hours, the blaze surged over 23 square miles (60 square kilometers), jumping fire trucks and threatening the edges of Reno, a city of over 280,000 residents. Authorities issued mandatory "GO NOW" evacuation orders for roughly 42,000 residents, urging another 45,000 to prepare. Governor Joe Lombardo swiftly declared a state of emergency in Washoe County, mobilizing the Nevada National Guard and hundreds of firefighters to combat the uncontained blaze. Amid the smoke, charred landscapes, and displaced families, the community was left grappling with a staggering physical and emotional toll.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="psychology-perspective" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The Psychology of Emergency: Normalcy Bias and Warning Apps</h2>
    <p>When disaster strikes with breathtaking speed, the human brain rarely pivots to panic immediately. Instead, it often stumbles through a protective fog known as <strong>normalcy bias</strong>—the psychological tendency to underestimate the likelihood of danger and assume that things will function normally. Several campers and residents interviewed by reporters admitted they initially dismissed the fire on Saturday morning because it looked small and distant.</p>
