  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The 11th-Hour Negotiations</h2>
    <p>Negotiators from the United States and Canada are locked in intense, last-minute talks in Washington to prevent a sweeping trade conflict. At stake is a midnight deadline set by U.S. President Donald Trump, who has threatened to slap aggressive 50% tariffs on approximately  billion worth of Canadian imports. The potential levies span a surprisingly wide array of everyday goods, ranging from industrial softwood lumber down to specific consumer items like hockey sticks and tongue depressors.</p>
    <p>Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney and Trump have held multiple phone conversations in recent days, underscoring the urgency of finding an off-ramp. While Canadian trade officials have kept details private, economic analysts point out that neither nation truly benefits from an escalating trade war. However, navigating a diplomatic exit is complicated by mounting public frustration north of the border over aggressive U.S. rhetoric, as well as Washington's broader push to rewrite the rules of continental trade.</p>
  </section>

    <h2>The Financial Mechanics: Who Really Pays for Tariffs?</h2>

When a 50% tariff is levied on  billion of Canadian products, the tax is paid directly at the U.S. border by American importers, businesses, and wholesalers.</p>

    <h2>A Ghost from the Great Depression</h2>
    <p>To enact these steep levies, the Trump administration bypassed traditional investigative tools like Section 301, instead reaching back nearly a century to invoke Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930. Better known as the Smoot-Hawley tariffs, the 1930 legislation is widely cited by economic historians as a cautionary tale.</p>

    <p>During the collapse of the global economy in the 1930s, Smoot-Hawley aimed to protect domestic industries by imposing heavy taxes on international imports. Instead, it triggered immediate retaliatory trade barriers from trading partners, choked off international commerce, and deepened the misery of the Great Depression. The fact that Section 338 has remained dormant for nearly a century highlights just how extraordinary—and legally untested—the current administration's strategy is.</p>

    <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Who actually pays for international tariffs?</summary>
      <p>Tariffs are paid directly to customs authorities by the domestic importer or business bringing the goods across the border, not by the foreign government. Those costs are often passed on to consumers through higher retail prices.</p>
    </details>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>What is Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930?</summary>
      <p>Section 338 is a provision from the Smoot-Hawley era that allows the U.S. president to impose tariffs of up to 50% on imports from countries deemed to discriminate against American commerce, without requiring lengthy investigations.</p>
