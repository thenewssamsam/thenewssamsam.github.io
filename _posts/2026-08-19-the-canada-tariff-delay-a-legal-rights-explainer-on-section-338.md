  <section id="legal-rights-explainer">
    <h2>The Legal Basis: Understanding Section 338</h2>
    <p>The Trump administration's decision to invoke Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 is an extraordinary legal move. This statute, born from the Great Depression era, grants the President broad, nearly unchecked authority to impose tariffs of up to 50% on goods from nations deemed to be "discriminating" against U.S. commerce.</p>

    <h3>Key Legal Distinctions</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>No Investigation Required:</strong> Unlike other trade statutes that require lengthy fact-finding missions by the International Trade Commission, Section 338 allows for immediate action.</li>
      <li><strong>No Duration Limits:</strong> There is no statutory sunset clause for these tariffs, meaning they can remain in effect indefinitely unless rescinded by the executive branch.</li>
      <li><strong>The "Discriminatory" Clause:</strong> The law hinges on the definition of discrimination, a term that provides significant latitude to the executive office to interpret trade practices as hostile to U.S. interests.</li>
    </ul>
