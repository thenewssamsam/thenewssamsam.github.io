<h3>1. Authority Bias and the Shortcut of Trust</h3>
<p class="ncg-product-inline">Human beings are cognitive misers. Evaluating the complex legislative records, economic philosophies, and foreign policy stances of multiple candidates requires an immense amount of mental energy. To cope with this cognitive overload, our brains rely on heuristics—mental shortcuts. <strong>Authority bias</strong> leads voters to defer to figures they already trust or view as powerful leaders. When Trump declared, <em>"Pretend, please, that I’m on the ballot,"</em> he was actively short-circuiting the analytical process for weary voters, offering his own credibility as a substitute for a detailed candidate evaluation.</p>

<h3>2. The Power of In-Group Identity</h3>
<p>Political preference is rarely just about policy; it is deeply tied to identity. In South Carolina, Ralph Norman’s alignment with former U.S. Ambassador Nikki Haley placed him, in the eyes of many passionate primary voters, slightly outside the core populist movement. When an election becomes framed as an existential test of loyalty to an in-group leader, nuanced debates over qualifications or foreign policy experience recede into the background. Defending the tribe becomes more important than vetting the individual.</p>

<h3>3. Normalcy Bias and Resistance to Disruption</h3>
<p>Voters often experience cognitive dissonance when their preferred leader endorses a candidate who lacks traditional credentials. However, normalcy bias—the tendency to underestimate the likelihood of a crisis or failure—allows supporters to minimize red flags. When Darline Graham openly admitted that national security was "not my thing" during a debate, supporters brushed it off, trusting that larger institutional structures would compensate for any individual shortcomings.</p>
