<header>
    <h1>Amazon Is Destroying Rare Books for AI Training: What Psychology Reveals About Our Urge to Consume the Past</h1>
    <div class="ncg-meta">
        <time datetime="2026-08-17">Published: August 17, 2026</time> &bull; 6 min read
    </div>
</header>

<p>When Amazon started three decades ago, it proudly billed itself as Earth's biggest bookstore, a digital sanctuary built to celebrate the printed word. Today, a peculiar industrial facility in Las Vegas known as VGT3—identified by a quirky logo of a dinosaur holding a book in its claws—is doing something entirely different to literature: buying rare, out-of-print texts, slicing off their bindings, and grinding them down to feed hungry artificial intelligence models.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Actually Happened at VGT3?</h2>

<p>Recent investigative reporting by 404 Media uncovered a striking physical supply chain for digital intelligence. By placing a tracking device inside a rare book, journalists tracked the volume straight to an Amazon-linked facility in Las Vegas designated as VGT3.</p>

<p>The operational reality is brutal in its efficiency: physical artifacts of human history are purchased through commercial channels, their spines are mechanically sheared off, and the pages are scanned at high speed. According to statements provided to 404 Media by Amazon, the company simply "purchases books through commercial channels to improve the products and services customers use."</p>

<p>While tech companies have already scraped the accessible internet clean of public forums, social media posts, and digitized public domain texts, they have hit a severe resource wall. Large Language Models (LLMs) require unfathomable quantities of fresh text to keep evolving. Worse still, the web is increasingly awash in synthetic content. When AI trains on AI-generated text over and over, researchers warn of "model collapse"—a digital form of inbreeding where output quality degrades as models feast on their own digital echoes.</p>

<p>To break this loop, tech firms are turning to pre-2022 human-authored materials. And out-of-print, rare physical books offer a pristine, unpolluted reservoir of language, culture, and context.</p>

<h2 id="the-psychology">The Psychology of Digital Consumption and Model Collapse</h2>

<p>From a psychological standpoint, this industrial destruction of rare books is a striking manifestation of <strong>loss aversion</strong> combined with <strong>technological urgency</strong>. Modern tech development operates under a relentless anxiety: the fear of falling behind in a zero-sum race toward artificial general intelligence.</p>

<p>When institutions or algorithms become desperate for growth, human behavioral patterns often shift toward short-term extraction at the expense of long-term preservation. Rare books represent the physical embodiment of deep, slow human thought—crafted over centuries by authors who never imagined their life's work would one day be fed into a paper shredder so a machine could learn how to write a corporate email.</p>

<div class="ncg-quote-box">
    <p>We are witnessing a historical irony: the company that built its empire on selling books to human readers is now dismantling physical books to teach machines how to replace them.</p>
</div>

<p>Psychologists often talk about normalcy bias—the refusal to plan for, or even accept, catastrophe until it is already underway. For bibliophiles, collectors, and historians, watching physical repositories of human thought get fed into high-speed scanners feels like an irreversible cultural loss. Yet within Silicon Valley's corporate psychology, the book is no longer viewed as a vessel of wisdom or a sacred cultural object; it is merely raw, uncompressed data waiting to be mined.</p>

<h2 id="responses">How Society is Grappling with the Industrialization of History</h2>

<p>How should we process the reality that our cultural heritage is being physically dismantled to power server farms? The reaction cannot simply be luddite outrage, nor can it be passive resignation to corporate efficiency.</p>

<p>First, readers and researchers can place a renewed value on physical community archives, independent local libraries, and private collections that prioritize preservation over rapid monetization. Second, society must demand greater transparency regarding how training data is sourced. If the bedrock of human literature is consumed to build tomorrow's tools, we must ask hard questions about ownership, consent, and cultural stewardship.</p>

<details class="ncg-faq-details">
    <summary class="ncg-faq-summary">Why are tech companies destroying rare books instead of scanning them intact?</summary>
    <p>High-speed document scanners used in industrial data processing often require slicing the spines off books to feed pages rapidly through automatic document feeders without damaging delicate historical pages by hand-turning.</p>
</details>

<details class="ncg-faq-details">
    <summary class="ncg-faq-summary">What is model collapse?</summary>
    <p>Model collapse is a degenerative process where artificial intelligence models degrade over time after being trained repeatedly on synthetic text generated by other AI models rather than authentic human communication.</p>
</details>

<div class="ncg-closing-box">
    <h3>One Last Thought</h3>
    <p>Books have always served as mirrors of the human soul, capturing our frailties, brilliance, and history in ink on paper. Grinding them up to teach artificial intelligence how to mimic us highlights a profound modern paradox: we are willing to consume our physical past entirely to manufacture an automated future. True progress should elevate human wisdom, not treat its physical artifacts as disposable fuel.</p>
