  <section id="introduction">
    <p>President Donald Trump recently turned the South Lawn into a construction tour, showcasing a new granite helipad and an ongoing ballroom project. While the spectacle of a president acting as a site manager is unique, the desire to physically reshape the Executive Mansion is a tradition as old as the building itself.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What is Happening on the South Lawn?</h2>
    <p>President Trump highlighted the installation of heavy-duty granite for a new helipad, praising its durability and asserting that it would leave foreign leaders "very impressed." The project, which includes a new walkway and driveway upgrades, is part of a broader,  million effort to construct a new ballroom. The president frames these additions as essential infrastructure and security upgrades, despite facing ongoing legal challenges regarding their funding and approval.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="historical-parallel">
    <h2>The Architecture of Executive Legacy</h2>
    <p>Throughout American history, presidents have viewed the White House not just as a residence, but as a canvas for their vision of the nation. When we look at Trump’s focus on these projects, we see echoes of earlier leaders who sought to define their legacy through stone and mortar.</p>

    <h3>The Roosevelt Expansion</h3>
    <p>The most significant physical transformation of the White House occurred under Theodore Roosevelt in 1902. Before then, the building was cramped and often in disrepair. Roosevelt oversaw a massive renovation that added the West Wing, effectively separating the president’s office from the residence. Like Trump’s current projects, this was a statement of modernization and necessity, though it fundamentally altered the character of the building.</p>

    <h3>The Truman Reconstruction</h3>
    <p>Perhaps the most extreme example of presidential building projects was Harry Truman’s complete gutting of the White House in the late 1940s. After discovering the structure was dangerously unstable—famously noting that the floors were sagging—Truman oversaw a total reconstruction. While Truman’s project was driven by safety, he, like Trump, was deeply involved in the minutiae of the construction, often frustrated by the pace and the technical challenges of maintaining the building’s historical integrity.</p>
