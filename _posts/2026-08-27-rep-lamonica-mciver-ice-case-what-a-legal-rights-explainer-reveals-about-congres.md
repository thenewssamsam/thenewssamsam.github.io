  <h2 id="legal-rights-explainer">A Legal Rights Explainer: Where Congressional Immunity Ends</h2>

  <p>To understand why the appeals court ruled the way it did, we have to look closely at how American constitutional law treats members of Congress. The Speech or Debate Clause (Article I, Section 6) protects federal lawmakers from being questioned, arrested, or prosecuted for typical legislative acts—such as speaking on the House floor, voting, introducing bills, or conducting official committee investigations.</p>

  <p>The purpose of this protection is vital: it ensures the legislative branch can function independently of executive intimidation or pressure. A lawmaker cannot be sued for libel because of a speech given in Congress, nor can they be arrested simply for expressing controversial policy views.</p>

Routine oversight visits give lawmakers the right to inspect federal facilities, but they do not confer the right to physically interfere with law enforcement personnel executing a lawful arrest or secure perimeter control.</p>
