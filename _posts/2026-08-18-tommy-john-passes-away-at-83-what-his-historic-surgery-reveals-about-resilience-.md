  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The Passing of a Baseball Pioneer</h2>
    <p>Long before his surname became a common noun in sports medicine clinics across the globe, Tommy John was simply a fierce, durable left-handed pitcher trying to save his livelihood. TMZ and subsequent tributes confirmed that John passed away at his home in Florida at the age of 83, surrounded by his family, following a recent bout with bladder cancer.</p>
    <p>Tributes poured in swiftly from across Major League Baseball, most notably from the New York Yankees, where John pitched some of his finest years, securing consecutive 20-win seasons. Yet, as the Yankees' statement noted, his staggering longevity and post-surgery triumphs often overshadowed his raw excellence on the mound. He wasn't just a great pitcher; he was the inadvertent godfather of modern sports orthopedics.</p>




  </section>

  <section id="the-100-to-1-wager" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The 100-to-1 Wager That Defied the Odds</h2>
    <p>In 1974, at age 31, John tore the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) in his pitching elbow while playing for the Los Angeles Dodgers. At the time, that diagnosis was a definitive death sentence for a pitcher's career. Ligaments did not heal themselves under the immense torque of major league pitching, and medical science offered no alternatives.</p>
    <p>Enter Dr. Frank Jobe, the Dodgers team physician. Jobe proposed an audacious, unproven procedure: replacing the torn ligament with a tendon harvested from John's right forearm. The odds of success were pegged at a daunting 100-to-1.</p>
    <p>John's response entered baseball lore: <em>“Dr. Jobe, if you do your job, I’ll do mine.”</em></p>
    <p>He missed the entire 1975 season, returned in 1976, and pitched 14 more productive seasons, retiring at age 46 with 288 career wins. Today, according to the American Medical Association, roughly 35% of active major league pitchers have undergone the procedure that now bears his name.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="historical-parallel" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>A Historical Parallel: Medical Innovations That Created New Eras</h2>
    <p>History is filled with accidental pioneers—individuals who stepped into uncharted waters and inadvertently created frameworks that changed society. Tommy John's surgery shares a fascinating parallel with the early days of penicillin adoption during World War II, or the introduction of Jonas Salk's polio vaccine.</p>
    <p>In each case, a desperate medical frontier faced a seemingly insurmountable barrier. Just as penicillin transformed a fatal bacterial infection into a manageable footnote, Jobe's tendon graft transformed a career-ending elbow snap into a temporary detour. But every historical parallel comes with a nuance: medical breakthroughs often scale faster than our understanding of their long-term consequences.</p>
    <p>Just as antibiotics eventually gave rise to concerns over resistant strains, the widespread adoption of Tommy John surgery has inadvertently altered how youth sports approach pitching development. John himself expressed visible discomfort in his later years over how commonplace the procedure had become among teenage athletes throwing with maximum velocity year-round.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="lessons-resilience" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Tommy John's Journey Teaches Us About Endurance</h2>
    <p>Beyond the statistics, the ERA figures, and the orthopedic breakthroughs, Tommy John's legacy offers a profound lesson in human resilience. When faced with a permanent roadblock—a torn ligament when no blueprint for repair existed—most would have accepted retirement with grace.</p>
    <p>Instead, John leaned into uncertainty. He embraced a process that required patience, physical therapy long before modern training science existed, and the courage to trust an experimental surgeon. His 26-year career stands as a testament to the idea that setbacks, even catastrophic ones, can sometimes become the foundation for an entirely unexpected second act.</p>
  </section>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Did Tommy John make it to the Baseball Hall of Fame?</summary>
      <p>No, despite his 288 career wins, four All-Star selections, and immense historical impact, John is not currently inducted into Cooperstown.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>How many pitchers currently have Tommy John surgery?</summary>
      <p>According to the American Medical Association, approximately 35% of active Major League Baseball pitchers have undergone the ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>What was Tommy John's stance on youth baseball injuries?</summary>
      <p>In his later years, John voiced concern over the surgery being performed on younger and younger athletes, noting that modern training regimens often encourage kids to throw too hard, too often.</p>
    </details>

  <section class="ncg-closing-section">
    <h2>One Last Thought</h2>
    <p>Tommy John's life reminds us that true impact often extends far beyond the arena in which we initially labor. He wanted to be remembered as a fierce competitor on the mound and a bedrock of his teams. Instead, history remembered him as the man who turned a dead end into a bridge for thousands of athletes who followed. When our own plans break down, his story stands as a quiet reminder: recovery often begins the moment we trust someone to help rebuild what was broken.</p>
    <p class="ncg-closing-line">Legacy isn't always what we achieve for ourselves; sometimes, it's the path we clear so others can keep running.</p>
