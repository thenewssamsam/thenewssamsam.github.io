  <p style="font-size: 1.15rem; font-weight: 500; color: #444444; margin-bottom: 25px;">
    When the water tower of Portland's historic Centennial Mills came down in a controlled blast, it didn't just clear a hazard—it erased a physical monument to the Pacific Northwest's foundational 20th-century grain economy. After decades of failed redevelopment plans, squatter fires, and structural decay, the waterfront landmark met its end, offering a sobering window into how rapidly cities forget the very industries that built them.
  </p>

  <h2 id="what-happened" style="font-size: 1.6rem; color: #1a1a1a; margin-top: 35px; margin-bottom: 15px;">What Happened at Centennial Mills?</h2>
  <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">
    The recent demolition of Centennial Mills marked the final chapter for a sprawling, five-acre riverfront property that once anchored Portland's bustling industrial waterfront. Built in 1910 as Crown Mills, the facility was a cornerstone supplier for the legendary "Wheat Fleet"—ships that loaded up in Portland to carry regional flour and grain across the globe. By the late 20th century, the mill was operating under Archer-Daniels-Midland (ADM) before finally shutting its doors in 2000 and passing into the hands of the city's economic development agency.
  </p>
  <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">
    For twenty-six years, the complex sat in administrative limbo. Despite various grand proposals—ranging from public parks to trendy nightclubs and residential spaces—no developer managed to rescue the deteriorating structures. The property suffered from what architectural writer Brian Libby aptly termed "demolition by neglect." Following years of graffiti, broken windows, and mounting safety concerns, a recent fire dealt the final blow, leading to the explosive demolition that brought down the iconic water tower.
  </p>

    </p>

  <h2 id="historical-parallel" style="font-size: 1.6rem; color: #1a1a1a; margin-top: 35px; margin-bottom: 15px;">The Historical Parallel: Industrial Hubs Across Time</h2>
  <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">
    The slow death and sudden destruction of Centennial Mills is hardly unique to Portland. Throughout modern industrial history, cities worldwide have grappled with the difficult transition from manufacturing powerhouses to post-industrial service economies. When we examine the rise and fall of Centennial Mills, clear historical parallels emerge with other monumental waterfront transformations, such as the regeneration of London's docklands or the rust-belt reclamation projects in American cities like Pittsburgh and Buffalo.
  </p>
  <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">
    In each of these historical instances, a familiar cycle plays out. First comes the era of raw utility—massive investments in infrastructure like grain elevators, rail spurs, and deep-water docks designed to extract maximum economic value from geography. Crown Mills and its successors operated on a relentless 24-hour schedule, driven by the sweat of union millers who managed combustible grain dust and aging machinery until the spigots were quite literally run dry.
  </p>
  <p style="margin-bottom: 20px;">
    The second phase is obsolescence. As shipping methods evolved and containerization took over, older multi-story vertical mills became inefficient compared to sprawling suburban facilities. Cities found themselves holding massive historical white elephants. Trapped between the high costs of environmental remediation and the political friction of historic preservation, local agencies often fell into a trap of paralysis. Without a clear economic mandate, properties like Centennial Mills drifted into the third phase: urban neglect, where vandalism and fires do the work that planners were too hesitant to undertake.
  </p>

Former mill superintendent Roger Farnen spoke of the complex almost like an animal he had cared for, noting the relief that came with ending its long, neglected suffering. When we neglect our physical and cultural heritage, we slowly sever the invisible ties that connect us to the generations of laborers who built our cities with calloused hands and steady persistence.
