  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened</h2>
    <p>Following the passage of Hurricane Lala, the Big Island of Hawaiʻi is deep into recovery mode. During a press conference in Hilo, Hawaiʻi Gov. Josh Green announced that all public schools, as well as state and county offices, would remain closed to allow public works and utility crews uninterrupted access to clear roadways and restore critical infrastructure.</p>
    <p>While airports and commercial harbors have successfully reopened to ensure supply chains keep moving, thousands of residents are still grappling with downed power lines, damaged infrastructure, and strict water conservation mandates. The storm left a significant footprint, particularly in rural and windward communities.</p>

  <section id="closures-and-infrastructure">
    <h2>Closures and Community Impact</h2>
    <p>The daily routine for families across the Big Island has been abruptly paused. Hawaiian Electric reported that tens of thousands of customers remained without power as assessment teams surveyed remote areas by air and ground. In response, roughly 100 mainland utility crew members are scheduled to arrive to assist local workers.</p>
    <p>For parents, the disruption extends beyond a single day. While most Hawaii State Department of Education schools face temporary closures for safety assessments, specific campuses like Nāʻālehu Elementary School will remain closed through Thursday, August 20, due to severe storm damage and blocked access roads. Meanwhile, public libraries and select 3rd Circuit Court facilities are maintaining staggered closures as safety conditions dictate.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="essential-water-and-safety-guidelines">
    <h2>Essential Water and Safety Guidelines</h2>
    <p>Perhaps the most critical update for households is the mandatory essential-needs-only water notice issued by the Hawaiʻi County Department of Water Supply. Power outages have disrupted water pumping and distribution facilities, prompting officials to restrict usage strictly to drinking, cooking, and personal hygiene.</p>
    <p>Potable water tankers have been strategically deployed to communities including Pāpaʻikou, Pepeʻekeo, North Kohala, and Nāʻālehu. In addition, the Department of Health issued a brown water advisory due to potential pollutant runoff, strongly urging residents to avoid swimming or wading in freshwater streams, ponds, or coastal brown water for up to 72 hours to prevent bacterial infections like leptospirosis.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="parenting-perspective">
    <h2>A Family Perspective: Helping Children Cope After a Storm</h2>
    <div class="ncg-lens-box">
      <p>Natural disasters can be deeply unsettling for children, who thrive on predictability and routine. When power goes out, schools close, and everyday comforts disappear, kids often absorb the ambient stress of the adults around them. Approaching these disruptions with calm, age-appropriate communication can help transform a stressful weather event into a lesson in resilience.</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>For Younger Children:</strong> Keep explanations simple and visual. Reassure them that helpers—like utility crews, firefighters, and neighbors—are working hard to fix things. Focus on the tangible safety steps your family is taking together.</li>
        <li><strong>For Pre-Teens and Teens:</strong> Involve them in household problem-solving, such as conserving water or helping manage flashlight batteries. Acknowledge their disappointment over missed activities while framing the recovery period as a shared community effort.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>"Disasters disrupt our routines, but they also give us a powerful opportunity to show children how a community pulls together when things get tough."</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section id="how-families-can-respond">
    <h2>How Families Can Respond Safely</h2>
    <p>Navigating the post-storm days requires patience and proactive safety measures. Here are practical ways households can manage the recovery period:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Conserve Water Mindfully:</strong> Strictly adhere to the Department of Water Supply essential needs notice. Save laundry and non-essential cleaning until normal system pressure returns.</li>
      <li><strong>Stay Clear of Hazards:</strong> Treat all downed power lines as live and dangerous. Report them immediately using official utility hotlines.</li>
      <li><strong>Keep Kids Away from Runoff:</strong> Respect brown water advisories. Keep children and pets away from swollen streams, standing floodwater, and mud.</li>
      <li><strong>Check on Vulnerable Neighbors:</strong> Reach out to elderly neighbors or families with infants who may need assistance securing water or emergency supplies.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section id="frequently-asked-questions">
    <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Are all public schools closed on the Big Island?</summary>
      <p>Most Department of Education and public charter schools closed temporarily for safety assessments. However, Nāʻālehu Elementary is closed through Thursday, August 20, and other campuses like Kaʻū High & Pāhala Elementary face extended evaluations based on local road and facility conditions.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Where can I get emergency drinking water?</summary>
      <p>The County has deployed potable water tankers to designated locations including Pāpaʻikou, Pepeʻekeo, North Kohala, and Nāʻālehu. Permanent water spigot locations can also be verified on the Hawaiʻi County Department of Water Supply website.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>When will power be fully restored?</summary>
      <p>Hawaiian Electric crews and incoming mainland support teams are working around the clock, but residents in heavily damaged rural areas should prepare for extended outages as major transmission lines and local circuits are repaired.</p>
    </details>
  </section>

    <h2>One Last Thought</h2>
    <p>Weather emergencies test the limits of our infrastructure and our patience, but they also highlight the quiet strength of island communities. By taking practical precautions, respecting safety advisories, and looking out for one another, families can weather both the storm and its aftermath together. Recovery takes time, but steady, cooperative effort always wins the day.</p>
    <p class="ncg-closing-quote">"In times of uncertainty, our greatest resource isn't electricity or running water—it's the care we show for our neighbors."</p>
