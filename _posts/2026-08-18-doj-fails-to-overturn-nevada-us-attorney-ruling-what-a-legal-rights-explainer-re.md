    <h1>DOJ Fails to Overturn Nevada US Attorney Ruling: What a Legal Rights Explainer Reveals About Vacancy Laws</h1>
    <div class="ncg-meta-bar">
      <time datetime="2026-03-03">Published: March 3, 2026</time>
      <span class="ncg-dot">•</span>
      <span class="ncg-read-time">6 min read</span>
    </div>
  </header>

  <p class="ncg-lead">When a federal appeals court unanimously upheld the disqualification of Sigal Chattah as Nevada's top prosecutor, it sent a sharp warning to the Department of Justice: executive convenience cannot bypass the constitutional requirement of Senate confirmation. For everyday citizens watching the mechanics of government, the ruling offers a rare, clear-cut look at the legal boundaries governing federal appointments.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Ninth Circuit Ruling?</h2>
  <p>A three-judge panel for the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled Monday that Attorney General Pam Bondi violated the Federal Vacancies Reform Act (FVRA) when she designated Sigal Chattah as the office's first assistant specifically to install her as acting U.S. Attorney for Nevada. The decision affirms a lower court's finding that the DOJ's procedure "was never intended by Congress."</p>
  <p>The panel—consisting of two judges appointed during the first Trump administration and one appointed by Bill Clinton—dismissed cross-appeals from criminal defendants who sought to throw out their indictments based on Chattah's invalid status. However, the core takeaway remained intact: the administration's attempt to install interim prosecutors without Senate oversight ran afoul of federal vacancy laws.</p>

  <p>During oral arguments in February, judges expressed skepticism over the government's expansive interpretation of vacancy statutes. Judge Stanley Blumenfeld noted that accepting the DOJ's argument would create "an easy workaround" allowing the attorney general to keep a favored individual in an acting role indefinitely without a Senate vote.</p>

  <h2 id="legal-rights-perspective">A Legal Rights Perspective: The Boundaries of Executive Appointments</h2>
  <div class="ncg-quote-box">
    <p>“This ruling protects not just the Senate's constitutional role in confirmations, but the American people's confidence that criminal prosecutions are being directed by someone with the proper legal authority.” — James I. Pearce, Washington Litigation Group</p>
  </div>
  <p>From a legal rights perspective, the FVRA and the Appointments Clause of the Constitution are designed as structural checks and balances. The Founders intentionally divided appointment powers between the executive and legislative branches to prevent the unchecked concentration of authority.</p>
  <p>When high-profile prosecutorial offices are filled via administrative workarounds—such as hiring an outside individual into a first-assistant position solely to elevate them to an acting leadership role—it tests the very limits of statutory language. Federal courts exist precisely to evaluate whether administrative agencies have stretched their statutory authority beyond what Congress authorized.</p>
  <p>As noted by defense counsel Jeremy Baron during oral arguments, the government has legitimate, established avenues to fill temporary vacancies, but it cannot "backfill" personnel to circumvent the Senate's constitutional confirmation mandate.</p>

  <h2 id="why-it-matters">Why Federal Vacancy Limits Matter to Ordinary Citizens</h2>
  <p>It is easy to view appointment disputes as bureaucratic inside baseball, but the legal authority behind a prosecutor's desk touches the foundation of the justice system. Every indictment, every plea agreement, and every policy decision carried out by a U.S. Attorney's office relies on the lawful delegation of power.</p>
  <p>When courts step in to enforce vacancy rules, they reinforce a fundamental democratic principle: no official, regardless of administration or political alignment, operates above the governing statutes enacted by Congress. Public confidence in the impartiality of criminal prosecutions depends entirely on the transparency and legality of who holds the office.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happens-next">What Happens Next in Nevada and Beyond?</h2>
  <p>Following the ruling, the legal landscape surrounding interim federal prosecutor appointments faces renewed scrutiny. While the Ninth Circuit declined to dismiss the criminal indictments challenged in this specific case—ruling it lacked jurisdiction over that narrow question—the disqualification order stands.</p>
  <p>The administration previously nominated George Kelesis to permanently fill the U.S. Attorney position for Nevada, a nomination awaiting Senate consideration. How the DOJ handles temporary leadership in Nevada and similar districts nationwide will likely depend on whether future judicial panels adopt the Ninth Circuit's strict reading of the Federal Vacancies Reform Act.</p>

  <section class="ncg-faq-section" id="faq">
    <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>What is the Federal Vacancies Reform Act?</summary>
      <p>The FVRA is a federal law that governs how executive branch agencies can temporarily fill vacant positions that normally require Senate confirmation, setting strict time limits and eligibility rules.</p>
    </details>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Does this ruling invalidate criminal cases handled by the office?</summary>
      <p>No. The Ninth Circuit dismissed cross-appeals from defendants seeking to overturn their indictments, holding that the court lacked jurisdiction to review that specific portion of the lower court ruling.</p>
    </details>
  </section>

  <section class="ncg-closing-box" id="closing">
    <h2>One Last Thought</h2>
    <p>The system of checks and balances can sometimes appear slow, complicated, and bogged down by procedural technicalities. Yet cases like <em>USA v. Jackson</em> demonstrate why those technicalities exist. By holding the executive branch to the letter of federal vacancy laws, the judiciary ensures that the machinery of justice remains anchored in constitutional order rather than administrative convenience.</p>
    <p class="ncg-closing-tagline">Legality is not an obstacle to effective governance; it is the foundation that makes authority legitimate.</p>
