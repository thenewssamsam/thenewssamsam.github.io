<p>Throughout history, empires that controlled trade chokepoints held immense economic power. Today, the Strait of Hormuz functions as the modern equivalent of these historical bottlenecks, through which a significant portion of the world's petroleum passes. When regional actors negotiate revenue sharing or when tankers face security threats in these waters, they are participating in a very old geopolitical script.</p>

<p>Another recurring theme in modern Middle East analysis is the fluidity of alliances. Historically, the collapse of a regional hegemon or the shifting of borders always creates a scramble for influence among neighboring states. This dynamic closely mirrors the power vacuums left after the fall of historical empires, where adjacent states quickly moved to project power and secure their borders.</p>
