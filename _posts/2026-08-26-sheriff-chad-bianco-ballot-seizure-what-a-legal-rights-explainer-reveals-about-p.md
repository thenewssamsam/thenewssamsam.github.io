  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p>When Riverside County Sheriff Chad Bianco ordered the seizure of more than 650,000 ballots during a heated political cycle, it instantly sparked an unprecedented legal showdown. Now, as the California Supreme Court reviews the fallout, the central question goes far beyond local politics: Can an elected sheriff bypass established election laws under the guise of an investigation? What happened in California is not merely a localized dispute; it is a critical test of constitutional boundaries, separation of powers, and the fragile chain of custody that underpins American democratic elections.</p>
  </section>

  <div class="ncg-quick-take">
    <h3>Quick Take: Why This Legal Battle Matters</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>The Core Event:</strong> Sheriff Chad Bianco seized over 650,000 ballots following Proposition 50 redistricting races, citing unproven voter fraud theories.</li>
      <li><strong>The Legal Stakes:</strong> The California Supreme Court is weighing two major lawsuits challenging whether a sheriff has warrant authority over election materials.</li>
      <li><strong>The Broader Impact:</strong> A ruling against Bianco could permanently clarify the supervisory authority of the state attorney general over independent county sheriffs.</li>
    </ul>

  <section class="ncg-what-happened">
    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: Inside the Unprecedented Ballot Seizure</h2>
    <p>The controversy stems from actions taken during Riverside County Sheriff Chad Bianco’s unsuccessful run for California governor. Operating on unsubstantiated claims regarding voter counts, Bianco deployed deputies to intercept and seize more than 650,000 ballots from an election. This maneuver bypassed standard election oversight procedures and shattered historical norms regarding the chain of custody for ballots—a process carefully guarded by state and federal laws designed to prevent political interference.</p>

Prompted by these profound risks, the UCLA Voting Rights Project and California Attorney General Rob Bonta filed separate lawsuits to halt the practice and force judicial review.</p>
