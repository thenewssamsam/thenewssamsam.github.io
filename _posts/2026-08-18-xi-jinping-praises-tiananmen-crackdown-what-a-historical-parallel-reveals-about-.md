<p>When Chinese leader Xi Jinping stood before thousands of officials in Beijing’s Great Hall of the People on Monday, his message was unmistakable: stability achieved through absolute authority remains the cornerstone of Communist Party rule. Marking the centenary of former leader Jiang Zemin’s birth, Xi delivered a bullish speech calling for an indomitable fighting spirit in the face of economic headwinds and global friction. Yet the most striking element of the address was a rare, public endorsement of the military crackdown on the 1989 Tiananmen Square democracy protests—a political taboo tightly scrubbed from mainland China's digital history.</p>

<p>As Beijing navigates a complex economic slowdown and tense diplomatic choreography ahead of a scheduled meeting between Xi and US President Donald Trump in late September, looking backward to 1989 offers a revealing window into how modern Chinese leadership frames crisis, control, and national survival.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: A Rare Public Nod to 1989</h2>
<p>Speaking to the politburo standing committee and state delegates, Xi celebrated Jiang Zemin’s legacy, explicitly highlighting Jiang's role in supporting the Central Committee's decision to crush the spring 1989 student demonstrations. The protests, sparked by the April death of deposed reformist leader Hu Yaobang, had swelled into weeks of pro-democracy rallies in Tiananmen Square before military troops and tanks cleared the area on June 4.</p>

<p>Jiang, then the party chief in Shanghai, maintained strict control over the financial hub during the unrest, a loyalty that propelled him to become General Secretary shortly after the crackdown. While state media documentaries and censored internet searches routinely omit the violent reality of the military assault, Xi’s explicit praise underscores how deeply the party ties its legitimacy to the suppression of internal dissent during moments of existential stress.</p>

<h2 id="historical-parallel">A Historical Parallel: The Iron Fist of Continuity</h2>
<p>History rarely repeats itself neatly, but it often rhymes through patterns of institutional survival. To understand why Xi invoked Tiananmen now, it helps to examine how past authoritarian regimes respond when economic promises begin to fray.</p>

<p>In periods of rapid industrial growth—such as the massive economic expansion Jiang Zemin oversaw leading up to China’s 2001 entry into the World Trade Organization—authoritarian states often rely on performance legitimacy. The unwritten social contract is simple: citizens accept political control in exchange for rising living standards and economic opportunity. But when growth slows—as current economic data from China demonstrates—that contract is strained.</p>

<p>History shows that when performance legitimacy falters, regimes frequently pivot back to ideological legitimacy and coercion. By praising the 1989 crackdown, Xi is not merely honoring a predecessor; he is reinforcing a foundational historical doctrine: that internal dissent is an existential threat, and that absolute state control is the only guarantor of national survival against external and internal storms.</p>

<div class="ncg-quote-box">
    <p>"When economic performance slows, regimes often pivot from the promise of prosperity back to the ironclad assurance of control."</p>
</div>

<h2 id="what-this-reveals">What This Reveals About Modern Geopolitics</h2>
<p>The timing of Xi's speech is far from accidental. With bilateral ties between Beijing and Washington marked by trade friction, technological competition over artificial intelligence, and ongoing disputes regarding Taiwan, Xi’s call for an “indomitable fighting spirit” projects strength inward and outward.</p>

<p>Domestically, it serves as a warning to anyone contemplating dissent amid economic uncertainty. Internationally, it signals to foreign powers—including the Trump administration ahead of their upcoming autumn summit—that Beijing will not compromise on core sovereignty issues, regardless of external economic pressure.</p>

<p>Yet this strategy carries inherent risks. Historical parallels also warn that absolute crackdowns stifle innovation, suppress vital feedback loops, and create a brittle political ecosystem where minor economic shocks can trigger disproportionate systemic anxiety.</p>
