    <p>When NASA and startup Katalyst Space Technologies announced they were abandoning a daring robotic mission to rescue the aging Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, it felt like a sharp blow to modern space exploration. The Link satellite, rushed to a July launchpad in just nine months, suffered critical attitude control failures that doomed its rendezvous with Swift. Yet, while the nearly 22-year-old gamma-ray telescope now faces an inevitable fiery reentry later this year, space history suggests that bold, rapid-fire failures are often the painful prerequisite for monumental leaps forward.</p>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened to the Swift Rescue Mission?</h2>
    <p>Launched on July 3, the refrigerator-sized Link satellite represented an aggressive, high-stakes gamble. NASA awarded startup Katalyst Space Technologies a $30 million contract under an unprecedented nine-month development timeline. The objective was straightforward yet technically dizzying: fly up to the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, capture it using three robotic arms, and boost its failing orbit to save it from atmospheric destruction.</p>

    <p>For the first few weeks, everything appeared normal. But in late July, disaster struck. Two of the spacecraft's three reaction wheels—crucial for precise orientation and attitude control—suddenly stopped working. Compounded by cold gas thruster malfunctions, the spacecraft spun out of control while zipping through orbit at nearly 5 miles per second. Although the Link satellite remains operational and may still execute a close navigation demonstration, its primary mission to rescue Swift is officially over.</p>
