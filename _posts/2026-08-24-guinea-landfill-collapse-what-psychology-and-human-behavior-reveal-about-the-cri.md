    <h2 style="font-size: 24px; color: #222222; border-bottom: 1px solid #eaeaea; padding-bottom: 6px; margin-top: 40px;">What Happened: The Conakry Landfill Disaster</h2>
    <p>At least 30 people lost their lives in the dead of night when a towering mound of rubbish collapsed onto nearby homes in Conakry, the capital of Guinea. The tragedy unfolded around 02:00 local time in the Dar-es-Salam neighborhood following intense overnight rainfall that destabilized the massive waste site.</p>
    <p>According to government reports, another 22 individuals sustained injuries, with six listed in serious condition. Emergency response teams, including military personnel equipped with heavy excavators, were deployed immediately to comb through the unstable debris in search of survivors. Prime Minister Mamadou Oury Bah visited the disaster zone to oversee rescue operations, though local leaders and residents fear the death toll may continue to rise as recovery efforts grind forward.</p>
    <p>This catastrophe is not an isolated shock; it echoes a previous fatal landslide at the very same site that claimed 10 lives in 2017. For the families living in the shadow of Conakry's mountains of waste, the recurring danger highlights a brutal urban reality where economic survival forces marginalized communities to settle in the most hazardous zones imaginable.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 20px; color: #444444; margin-top: 25px;">1. Normalcy Bias and Habituation</h3>
    <p>Normalcy bias is a cognitive state that causes people to underestimate the possibility of a disaster and its potential effects. When a danger grows slowly over years—such as a rubbish dump steadily rising inch by inch—the human brain categorizes it as a permanent part of the landscape rather than an active, ticking hazard. Residents adapt to the smell, the sight, and the minor inconveniences until the abnormal slowly becomes normalized. Day after day passes without a catastrophic collapse, training the brain to falsely believe that tomorrow will look exactly like today.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 20px; color: #444444; margin-top: 25px;">2. The Trap of Structural Powerlessness</h3>
    <p>Psychological research into poverty and environmental stress consistently shows that perceived lack of control breeds profound fatalism. For many living in impoverished urban peripheries, relocating away from hazardous sites is an economic impossibility. When financial survival demands proximity to urban centers where informal work can be found, individuals engage in psychological discounting—suppressing awareness of long-term risks to manage the immediate, crushing anxiety of daily survival. It is not that people are blind to danger; rather, they are trapped in a system where every available alternative feels even worse.</p>
