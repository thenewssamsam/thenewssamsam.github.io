The victims spanned four generations, ranging from a great-grandmother in her 90s to a 7-year-old child.</p>

    <h3>1. Normalcy Bias and the Dismissal of Red Flags</h3>
    <p>Family members described Alan Smith as a reclusive "hermit" who spent his days isolated in his room, occasionally displaying verbal hostility but no documented history of physical violence. In domestic settings, families frequently fall victim to <em>normalcy bias</em>—the psychological tendency to underestimate the likelihood of a disaster or to downplay persistent warning signs because the alternative (believing a loved one is capable of extreme harm) is too psychologically painful to accept.</p>

    <h3>2. Isolation, Resentment, and the Envy Trap</h3>
    <p>Psychologically, chronic social withdrawal combined with perceived failure—such as Smith's inability to maintain steady employment while living with aging parents—fosters deep-seated grievances. When parents finally set healthy boundaries by asking an adult child to move out, the perceived rejection can trigger a severe narcissistic injury. For isolated individuals who lack healthy emotional outlets, internal grievances morph into externalized blame, directing fury toward the very family members attempting to establish independence.</p>
