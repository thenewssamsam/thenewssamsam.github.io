  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What the New Forecast Projects</h2>
    <p>
      As winter planning begins across the West, long-range meteorological data indicates a shifting climate pattern for North America. Published on August 16, 2026, OpenSnow’s Winter Forecast Preview points to a strengthening El Niño phase that will reshape storm tracks across the United States. <strong>[Confirmed — OpenSnow]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      While northern border states and western Canada face a warmer, drier outlook with below-normal snowfall, the Southwest and California are positioned to become premier winter destinations for the upcoming season. Meteorologists note that strong El Niño events typically pull the Pacific storm track southward, directing moisture-laden systems straight toward California and the southern tier of the country. <strong>[Confirmed — Alan Smith / OpenSnow]</strong>
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="regional-breakdown">
    <h2>Where the Snow Will Fall: Mammoth, Tahoe, and SoCal</h2>
    <p>
      Not all mountain ranges experience El Niño winter storms equally. According to forecast modeling, different regions across California and Nevada face distinct snowfall probabilities for the months ahead:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Central Sierra:</strong> Holds the highest statistical probability for well-above-average snow accumulation, with resorts like Mammoth Mountain and China Peak sitting directly in the strongest forecast bullseye. <strong>[Confirmed — OpenSnow]</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Northern California and Tahoe:</strong> Projected to see active storm cycles, though forecasters note that temperatures may lean slightly warmer than average. This could occasionally push snow levels higher during potent atmospheric river events. <strong>[Confirmed — OpenSnow]</strong></li>
      <li><strong>Southern California:</strong> Resorts such as Mountain High and Snow Summit frequently see disproportionately higher snow totals during strong El Niño cycles compared to neutral or La Niña years. <strong>[Confirmed — OpenSnow]</strong></li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section id="the-el-nino-factor">
    <h2>The El Niño Factor and Historical Caveats</h2>
    <p>
      Understanding how El Niño operates requires looking past simple generalizations. An El Niño pattern involves warmer-than-average sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean, which alters atmospheric pressure and redirects the jet stream. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      However, forecasters emphasize that a strong El Niño label does not guarantee a uniform record-breaking season everywhere. A prime historical example is the powerful 2015–2016 super El Niño winter. Despite massive early hype, that season ultimately delivered near-normal totals in the Tahoe region and below-average accumulation in Southern California. <strong>[Confirmed — OpenSnow]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      This historical variability is why modern forecasting models incorporate oceanic temperatures alongside atmospheric river tracking and historical analogs rather than relying on a single indicator. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
    </p>
  </section>

  <section class="na-faq">
    <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    <details>
      <summary>When does the 2026–2027 winter ski season typically start in California?</summary>
      <p>Major California ski resorts like Mammoth Mountain typically aim to open in November, depending on early-season natural snowfall and snowmaking temperatures, with peak storm activity expected later in the season.</p>
    </details>
    <details>
      <summary>Does El Niño guarantee a record-breaking snow year?</summary>
      <p>No. While strong El Niños favor active southern storm tracks and above-average precipitation in California, historical precedent shows that individual seasons can vary widely, as seen during the 2015–2016 winter.</p>
