<h2>What Happened: The Anatomy of the Nepal Disaster</h2>
<p>According to reports from geophysicists at Columbia University's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, the catastrophe began with an immense landslide in a mountain pass in the Himalayas. The slide swept away large portions of a glacier, generating so much kinetic and thermal energy that the friction melted vast amounts of ice instantly.</p>
<p>The resulting torrent of ice, debris, and water rushed down into the valley at terrifying speeds, reaching floodwaters estimated at 130 to 160 feet high moving at roughly 44 miles per hour. The initial event was so violent that it registered as a 5.2 magnitude earthquake on seismographs around the globe. Local authorities confirmed that at least 157 people have been killed, with hundreds more missing—including dozens of American tourists visiting the region.</p>

<h3>Normalcy Bias and the Shock of the Unexpected</h3>
<p>One of the most powerful mental mechanisms operating during sudden catastrophes is <strong>normalcy bias</strong>. This cognitive state causes people to underestimate the possibility of disaster even when warning signs are present. Hikers, tourists, and local residents operating in the majestic shadow of the Himalayas view the mountains as timeless monuments of stability. When an entire glacier gives way in a fraction of a minute, the human brain experiences severe cognitive dissonance. The speed—water moving at 44 miles per hour—leaves virtually no window for standard decision-making processes.</p>
