    </h2>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">

    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      The E-3 Sentry—instantly recognizable by the rotating radar dome mounted atop its fuselage—serves as the airborne nervous system for modern air campaigns. It coordinates tactical fighters, tracks incoming threats, and provides commanders with a panoramic view of the battlespace. To lose such a high-value, billion-dollar asset is not merely a loss of expensive hardware; it strikes at the very core of network-centric warfare doctrine that modern air forces have relied upon for decades.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      As analysts parse through the implications, the incident has instantly vaulted to the forefront of global defense discussions. Observers are left asking how a platform designed to detect and evade threats was overcome, and what this signals for the future of air superiority in increasingly contested airspace.

      A Historical Parallel: When Invulnerability Proves Illusionary
    </h2>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      History is replete with moments where a dominant military technology was suddenly humbled, forcing a complete doctrine overhaul. To understand the gravity of the reported AWACS loss, we can look back at past turning points where the "unsinkable" or "unreachable" was shattered.
    </p>
    
    <h3 style="font-size: 1.2rem; font-weight: 600; color: #374151; margin-top: 20px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
      The Battleship Era and the Wake of Pearl Harbor
    </h3>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      For the first half of the 20th century, the battleship was the undisputed monarch of naval power—floating fortresses embodying national might. Yet, the attack on Pearl Harbor and subsequent sinking of capital ships like the HMS Prince of Wales by land-based torpedo bombers demonstrated that massive, expensive platforms were intensely vulnerable to concentrated, innovative attacks. The battleship era did not end overnight, but its psychological invulnerability evaporated instantly, giving permanent precedence to naval aviation and aircraft carriers.
    </p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.2rem; font-weight: 600; color: #374151; margin-top: 20px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
      The U-2 Incident and the Limits of High-Altitude Sanctuary
    </h3>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      In 1960, Francis Gary Powers was shot down flying a U-2 spy plane over the Soviet Union. Until that moment, American planners operated under the confident assumption that flying at extreme altitudes provided an absolute sanctuary beyond the reach of Soviet surface-to-air missiles. That single shoot-down shattered a pillar of Cold War intelligence strategy, forcing the rapid development of satellite reconnaissance and stealth technology.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      The reported destruction of an AWACS E-3 bears a striking resemblance to these historical shocks. It reminds us that whenever military doctrine relies heavily on a single class of high-value platforms functioning within an assumed sanctuary, adversaries will inevitably invest disproportionate resources into finding and exploiting the cracks in that armor.
    </p>
  </section>
