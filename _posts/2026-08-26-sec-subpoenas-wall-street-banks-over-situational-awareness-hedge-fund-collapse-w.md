<p>When the Securities and Exchange Commission steps in to subpoena major Wall Street lenders, financial markets pay attention. That is precisely what happened when federal regulators turned their gaze toward the near-collapse of Situational Awareness, an artificial intelligence-focused hedge fund led by former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner. The fund experienced a staggering plunge from roughly $45 billion down to $10 billion during a July tech sell-off, driven by reported leverage as high as 400%. Beyond the immediate financial shockwaves, this dramatic event offers a profound window into human behavior, the psychology of risk, and the powerful illusions that emerge during technological revolutions.</p>

<section id="ns-what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Fall of Situational Awareness</h2>
<p>The core narrative centers on rapid expansion followed by an equally rapid unwinding. Situational Awareness had built massive, highly concentrated, levered positions in publicly listed equities tied directly to the artificial intelligence infrastructure boom, including major holdings in SK Hynix and CoreWeave. When the broader technology sector experienced a severe sell-off in late July, those heavily levered positions triggered a wave of margin calls from prime brokers.</p>

<p>To prevent a disorderly collapse, Ken Griffin's multi-strategy giant Citadel stepped in to purchase the distressed portfolio at a steep discount, estimated to be around 10%. Griffin reported in a recent investor letter that Citadel has since successfully offloaded roughly 80% of that risk, while underlying assets like SK Hynix and CoreWeave have managed to recover some ground. Nevertheless, the incident prompted the SEC to issue subpoenas to major Wall Street institutions—including Goldman Sachs, JP Morgan, Citigroup, and Bank of America—seeking detailed information regarding trades, leverage terms, and communications.</p>

<p>To understand how a fund can plummet from $45 billion to $10 billion in a matter of weeks, we must look past spreadsheets and examine established psychological patterns. Human decision-making under conditions of technological transformation is heavily influenced by cognitive biases that often override traditional risk management.</p>

<h3>Normalcy Bias and Overconfidence</h3>
<p>During the early and middle stages of a booming market trend, participants frequently succumb to normalcy bias—the mental state that leads people to underestimate the possibility of a disaster because "it has never happened before." When a technological wave like artificial intelligence promises to reshape the entire global economy, brilliant analysts and investors can easily convince themselves that standard historical volatility no longer applies. This overconfidence acts as a powerful intoxicant, masking the silent accumulation of risk.</p>

<h3>Loss Aversion and the Doubling-Down Trap</h3>
<p>Psychologically, human beings feel the pain of a loss roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of an equivalent gain. When high-conviction bets face early downward pressure, loss aversion can trigger frantic attempts to recover ground rather than gracefully accepting a smaller loss. By layering on leverage—in this case, reported at up to 400%—investors attempt to engineer outsized returns, effectively betting that the future will unfold exactly according to their bullish model. When reality diverges from the model, margin calls force rapid liquidation at the worst possible moment.</p>
