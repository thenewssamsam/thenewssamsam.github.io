  <section id="ncg-what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Legal Showdown at the Supreme Court</h2>
    <p>When a group of Texas public school families—representing Christian, Jewish, Hindu, and nonreligious backgrounds—filed a formal petition with the U.S. Supreme Court, they ignited a profound constitutional debate over the intersection of faith, state authority, and public education. At the center of the controversy is Texas Senate Bill 10, a 2025 statute requiring every public elementary and secondary classroom in the state to prominently display a copy of the Ten Commandments using text drawn specifically from the King James Bible.</p>
    <p>The families argue that the mandatory display violates both the Establishment Clause and their fundamental rights to direct the religious upbringing of their children. However, the legal road to the nation's highest court has been fiercely contested. In April, the full U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit upheld the law by a narrow 9-8 vote, concluding that the measure does not resemble a "founding-era religious establishment." Now, the Supreme Court must decide whether to step back into a church-state arena it has largely avoided for decades, raising critical questions not only for constitutional lawyers, but for people of deep faith who care deeply about how sacred truths are honored in public life.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="ncg-legal-background">
    <h2>From Stone v. Graham to Kennedy: The Changing Legal Landscape</h2>
    <p>To understand the gravity of the current petition, we have to look back at how judicial interpretations have shifted over the last forty-six years. In 1980, the Supreme Court struck down a similar Kentucky statute in <em>Stone v. Graham</em>. In that unsigned ruling, the justices reasoned that posting the Ten Commandments in classrooms was designed to induce schoolchildren to read, meditate upon, and venerate religious text—an objective they deemed impermissible for the state under the Establishment Clause.</p>

    <p>For decades, courts relied heavily on the "Lemon test" established in 1971 to evaluate such laws. But the judicial framework changed dramatically in 2022 with <em>Kennedy v. Bremerton School District</em>, where the high court protected a high school football coach's right to pray on the fifty-yard line. Writing for the majority, Justice Neil Gorsuch indicated that the old Lemon test had been "long ago abandoned" in favor of an approach rooted in historical practices and understandings from the nation's founding. Seizing on this shift, the 5th Circuit majority argued that because the Lemon test was "jettisoned," the precedent in <em>Stone v. Graham</em> no longer held binding weight. The petitioning families, however, are urging the justices to clarify that lower courts cannot simply bypass landmark precedents without explicit instruction from the Supreme Court itself.</p>
