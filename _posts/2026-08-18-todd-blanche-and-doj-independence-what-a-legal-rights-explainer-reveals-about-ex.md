  <p>Attorney General Todd Blanche recently made headlines by explicitly declining to pledge Department of Justice independence from the White House, stating he would consider President Donald Trump's views on prosecutions. For decades, the boundary between executive political leadership and prosecutorial discretion has stood as a core pillar of modern administrative governance. When a newly confirmed attorney general openly questions that traditional separation, it raises vital questions for citizens attempting to understand how executive power operates under constitutional frameworks.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened</h2>
  <p>In an August 16 interview on NBC News' "Meet the Press," Attorney General Todd Blanche was directly asked whether he would act independently from the White House. His response was unambiguous: "No, I'm not going to pledge that." He further stated that he would consider the president's views on specific prosecutions.</p>

  <p>The interview followed Blanche's confirmation by the Senate after serving as acting attorney general since April. Throughout his confirmation process and prior congressional testimony, Blanche emphasized that the Department of Justice is part of the executive branch and takes policy direction from the president.</p>
  <p>The discussions surrounding prosecutorial independence have intensified alongside high-profile cases and social media commentary from the White House concerning matters such as the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool vandalism case and prosecutions involving figures like former FBI Director James Comey and New York Attorney General Letitia James.</p>

  <h2 id="legal-rights-perspective">A Legal Rights Perspective on DOJ Independence</h2>
  <p>To understand the weight of Blanche's statements, it helps to examine how the American legal system structures executive authority and prosecutorial discretion. Under Article II of the Constitution, the president heads the executive branch, within which the Department of Justice is housed. However, following the Watergate scandal and the resignation of President Richard Nixon, a post-Watergate norm developed to insulate federal criminal investigations from direct partisan or political interference by the White House.</p>
  <p>This traditional firewall is not explicitly written as an absolute statutory prohibition in every corner of federal law, but rather as an institutional norm designed to protect due process rights. The principle of prosecutorial discretion grants prosecutors broad authority to decide whether to bring charges, what charges to pursue, or when to drop a case. From a legal rights perspective, the core tension lies between the president's constitutional authority to direct executive branch priorities and the individual's right to an impartial, fair judicial process free from arbitrary or politically motivated targeting.</p>
