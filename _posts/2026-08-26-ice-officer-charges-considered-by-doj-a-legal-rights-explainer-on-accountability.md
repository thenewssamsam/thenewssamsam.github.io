<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in Minneapolis?</h2>
    <p>The Justice Department is actively weighing whether to bring criminal charges against Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agent Christian Castro following a winter shooting in Minneapolis that left a Venezuelan immigrant wounded. The case, which has drawn intense national scrutiny, stems from a chaotic January 14 incident during a high-tension immigration enforcement operation.</p>
    
    <p>Initially, Department of Homeland Security officials claimed the agent fired in self-defense after a struggle involving a shovel or broomstick. However, subsequent video evidence and internal reviews unraveled that narrative. The DHS and federal prosecutors later admitted that false statements had been made under oath, prompting the government to drop criminal charges against the two men originally targeted in the raid, Julio Sosa-Celis and Alfredo Alejandro Aljorna.</p>

    <p>High-profile cases involving federal law enforcement officers often plunge into a labyrinth of overlapping jurisdictions, constitutional protections, and statutory immunities. Understanding how these legal frameworks operate clarifies why a case like Castro's moves so slowly and generates fierce debate.</p>

    <h3>1. Dual Sovereignty and Criminal Jurisdiction</h3>
    <p>Under the principle of dual sovereignty in the United States legal system, both state and federal governments possess the authority to enact and enforce criminal laws. If an individual's conduct violates both state law and federal civil rights statutes, both jurisdictions theoretically have the power to prosecute. However, coordination between state attorneys general and the U.S. Department of Justice requires navigating complex Department policies, particularly when civil rights divisions are involved.</p>

    <h3>2. The Extradition Process Between States</h3>
    <p>Extradition is the formal process where one state surrenders an accused individual to another state for trial. While the U.S. Constitution's Extradition Clause generally requires governors to comply with valid extradition requests, governors occasionally assert executive discretion or resistance, particularly when political or jurisdictional tensions flare between state and federal authorities.</p>
