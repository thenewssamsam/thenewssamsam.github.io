This move comes shortly after the United Arab Emirates suspended financial ties with Iran, citing ballistic missile threats—a claim Tehran vehemently denies as a "false-flag operation."</p>

        <h2>Lessons from the Past: Does Isolation Work?</h2>
        <p>History provides us with several instances of states attempting to use total economic blockades to force regime change or policy shifts. The primary historical parallel here is the 19th-century Continental System or more recent 20th-century embargoes. The central challenge in these historical precedents is the "leaky bucket" effect: the more comprehensive the sanctions, the greater the incentive for illicit trade networks to emerge.</p>

        <h3>The Limits of Economic Coercion</h3>
        <p>Historical analysis of the 1930s sanctions against Italy or the Cold War-era trade restrictions shows that while economic pressure can create significant internal hardship, it often fails to translate into immediate political compliance. Instead, it frequently fosters a "siege mentality" within the target nation, where the government uses the external threat to tighten domestic control and justify economic hardship as a patriotic sacrifice.</p>
