  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Anatomy of a Corporate Clash</h2>
    <p>The core dispute between California and Paramount Skydance centers on antitrust concerns, economic impact, and market competition. Attorney General Rob Bonta, alongside 11 other state attorneys general, filed an antitrust lawsuit last month to halt David Ellison’s sprawling $111-billion takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery. State and local studies have warned that the transaction could erase thousands of jobs in Los Angeles, dealing a severe blow to an already fragile local entertainment economy.</p>
    <p>Yet, while regulators insist the case is strictly about market competition and corporate consolidation, the cultural battle lines have widened dramatically. Actor Mark Ruffalo, an outspoken political progressive and prominent deal critic, helped spearhead an artist-led campaign drawing signatures from nearly 5,000 entertainment workers—including high-profile names like Ben Stiller, Bryan Cranston, and Jane Fonda—voicing fierce opposition to the merger.</p>
    <p>The tension boiled over when Ruffalo criticized the involvement of Oracle Corp., co-founded by Larry Ellison (David Ellison’s father), and recirculated a two-year-old video of former Oracle CEO Safra Catz discussing the company's technology contracts with the Israeli military. Ruffalo characterized the war in Gaza using terms like "genocide" and "apartheid" linked to Oracle's infrastructure. In response, Paramount issued a sharp rebuke, accusing Ruffalo of invoking antisemitic tropes. Leaders from prominent Jewish organizations, including the Simon Wiesenthal Center and the Anti-Defamation League, quickly backed Paramount, asserting that applying such terminology to a corporate transaction crosses a dangerous line. Ruffalo fired back on social media, insisting that his criticism was directed at government policy and corporate military contracts, not Jewish people.</p>

    <h3>The Mechanism of Moral Panic and Accusation</h3>
    <p>Psychologically, invoking powerful historical and moral triggers—such as accusations of antisemitism on one side, or allegations of systemic human rights violations on the other—raises the emotional stakes instantly. When parties feel threatened by impending corporate decisions that could dismantle their livelihoods or business empires, cognitive biases like confirmation bias and threat rigidity take over. Executives and critics alike stop listening to the underlying structural arguments—such as Bonta's concerns over market dominance or Ellison's business concessions—and instead focus entirely on defending their moral and personal reputations.</p>
