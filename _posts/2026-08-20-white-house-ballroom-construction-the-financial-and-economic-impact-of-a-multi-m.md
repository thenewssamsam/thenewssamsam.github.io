<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened? The 90,000-Square-Foot Project at a Glance</h2>
<p>When the Trump administration announced plans in July 2025 to tear down the historic East Wing of the White House to construct a massive 90,000-square-foot ballroom, the debate was immediately framed around executive authority, historic preservation, and national security. But beneath the constitutional arguments playing out before the Supreme Court lies an equally compelling dimension that directly impacts taxpayers and public administration: the pure economics of a multi-million dollar federal construction project racing against the judicial clock.</p>

<p>According to recent filings covered by SCOTUSblog, the project is already reported to be 65% complete, with the administration pushing aggressively toward total completion by November 2026. Legal counsel for the National Trust for Historic Preservation has accused the executive branch of trying to "outrun judicial review" by rushing construction to a point where deconstruction becomes economically and physically impossible. As the legal battle intensifies—with the D.C. Circuit ruling that Congress holds the exclusive purse strings for White House structures—financial analysts and legal observers alike are left looking at the underlying cost mechanics of unauthorized federal spending.</p>
</section>

<h2>The Economic Stakes of Sunk Costs and "Outrunning" Judicial Review</h2>
<p>In project economics, the concept of the "sunk cost fallacy" is often weaponized by project proponents to justify completion regardless of legal or structural propriety. By rushing the East Wing ballroom to be "substantially finished by November 2026," the administration's strategy relies on physical fait accompli: once a building is 90% or 100% complete, the cost of tearing it down or modifying it becomes economically prohibitive for any court to practically order.</p>
