  <section id="introduction">
    <p>As the Lindsay Clancy murder trial enters its fourth week, the legal proceedings have moved beyond the undisputed facts of the tragedy to the much more contentious issue of her state of mind. With the prosecution resting its case after 71 witnesses, the courtroom now shifts its focus to the defense’s argument that Clancy was suffering from an undiagnosed psychotic break. This case forces us to look not just at the evidence presented, but at the long, evolving history of how the legal system attempts to define criminal responsibility in the shadow of severe mental illness.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="the-case-facts">
    <h2>The Current State of the Trial</h2>
    <p>The prosecution maintains that Clancy’s actions were a deliberate, premeditated act rather than a result of psychosis. They have pointed to digital evidence, such as drive-time calculations on Apple Maps, to suggest planning. Conversely, the defense argues that a series of misdiagnoses and over-medication by psychiatric providers left Clancy in a state of cognitive collapse. The testimony has highlighted a fragmented mental healthcare system where providers often lacked a holistic view of her treatment.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="historical-parallels">
    <h2>Legal History and the Insanity Plea</h2>
    <p>Throughout history, the insanity defense has remained one of the most polarizing aspects of the judicial process. Much like the famous 19th-century M'Naghten rules, which established the standard for proving insanity in British law, modern trials involving severe postpartum mental health crises grapple with the same fundamental question: Did the defendant understand the nature and quality of their actions?</p>

    <p>Historical cases of "diminished capacity" often turned on whether the defendant was capable of premeditation. In the past, the law was often slow to recognize hormonal or postpartum factors as legitimate contributors to mental instability. Today, the Lindsay Clancy trial serves as a modern touchstone, highlighting how our understanding of biology and neurology has forced the legal system to evolve from a purely moralistic view of crime toward a more clinical one.</p>
