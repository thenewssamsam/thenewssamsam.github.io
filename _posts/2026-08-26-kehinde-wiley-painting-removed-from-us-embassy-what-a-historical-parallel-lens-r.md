  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened at the U.S. Embassy?</h2>
    <p>Earlier this month, a canvas by acclaimed artist Kehinde Wiley—famous for his vibrant portraits and his official depiction of former President Barack Obama—was unceremoniously taken down from the permanent collection at the U.S. Embassy in Santo Domingo. The artwork, titled <em>“Young Artists After Siamesas 1960,”</em> was originally commissioned by the State Department’s Art in Embassies program in 2013 and featured four local Dominican art students against a rich floral backdrop.</p>

    <p>Ambassador Campos documented the removal on social media, accompanied by the defiant strains of Twisted Sister’s “We’re Not Gonna Take It.” In her accompanying statement, Campos made the administration's stance explicit: the embassy is pivoting away from what officials characterize as "globalist and woke ideologies" and toward traditional American patriotism. Meanwhile, the State Department confirmed it is evaluating whether it can legally sell the publicly funded painting.</p>
    
    <p>This action is far from an isolated incident. Under the direction of Art in Embassies director Erin Scavino, the federal government has increasingly prioritized patriotic imagery, replacing perceived ideological departures with themes celebrating American heritage ahead of the country's 250th anniversary. Critics, however, view these sweeping changes—alongside proposed budget cuts to the National Endowment for the Arts and the National Endowment for the Humanities—as a concerted push toward politically motivated cultural curation.</p>
