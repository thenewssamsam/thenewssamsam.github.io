    <h1>Shark Attack Survivor Leah Stewart Speaks Out: What Psychology Reveals About the Will to Survive</h1>
    <p class="ncg-subtitle">When Sydney mother Leah Stewart came face-to-face with a great white shark at Coogee Beach, her mind locked onto a single thought: her 18-month-old daughter. Her emotional first interview offers a profound look into the human survival mindset.</p>
  </header>

  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p>Trauma has a way of stripping away the trivial layers of modern life until only the rawest instincts remain. For 34-year-old Sydney mother Leah Stewart, that moment of absolute clarity arrived in the cold, blood-stained waters of Coogee Beach on the morning of June 13, 2026. Bitten by a great white shark in what she described as a "monster" attack, Stewart spent two weeks in a coma, endured multiple surgeries, and ultimately lost part of her arm.</p>
    <p>Now, months after the horrific ordeal, Stewart is speaking publicly for the first time in an emotional interview set to air on the Nine Network's <i>60 Minutes</i>. Beyond the heartbreaking details of the attack and her ongoing recovery, her story offers a rare window into a profound psychological phenomenon: how the human mind focuses, endures, and fights to live when faced with unthinkable danger.</p>
  </section>

But psychologists look deeper into the cognitive machinery that activates during a life-or-death crisis. How does the human brain process sheer terror, and what dictates whether a person succumbs to shock or fights through it?</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">Tunnel Vision and the Maternal Anchor</h3>
    <p>In her interview, Stewart recalled that as the shark latched onto her leg, her mind did not race through panic or confusion. Instead, it locked onto an unyielding anchor: her 18-month-old daughter, August. <i>“All I was thinking of was August and I thought, she’s not going to have a mum,”</i> Stewart shared.</p>

    <p>From a psychological standpoint, extreme stress triggers narrow attentional focus, often called "weapon focus" or tunnel concentration. Under massive adrenaline surges, the brain discards non-essential data to prioritize a singular, overriding goal. For parents, that protective instinct often overrides self-preservation. The psychological weight of responsibility toward a dependent child acts as a powerful deterrent against giving up.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">Crystal-Clear Trauma Memory vs. Dissociation</h3>
    <p>Trauma survivors often experience fragmented memories. Yet, Stewart noted that her memory of the shark's face—which she called a "monster"—remains crystal clear. Psychologists explain that high-stress encounters stimulate the amygdala, etching emotional snapshots into long-term memory with vivid intensity, even while other surrounding details blur away. This hyper-salient encoding is the brain's evolutionary mechanism for recognizing mortal threats in the future.</p>
