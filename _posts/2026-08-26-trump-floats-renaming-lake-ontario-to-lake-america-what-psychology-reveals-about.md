<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: Lake Ontario and Trade Tensions</h2>
<p>President Donald Trump announced Tuesday morning that he is considering renaming Lake Ontario to "Lake America," following a similar move early in his second term regarding the Gulf of Mexico. Trump shared an altered map on social media showing the official "Lake Ontario" label crossed out and replaced with "Lake America" in a gold-colored font alongside an American flag graphic.</p>

<p>While a U.S. president possesses the executive authority to alter geographic names for official domestic federal use, Lake Ontario is a shared international waterway governed jointly by the United States and Canada through the International Joint Commission and the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement. Neither the U.S. nor any single government can unilaterally dictate what other sovereign nations call international bodies of water.</p>

<section id="the-psychology-of-naming">
<h2>The Psychology of Geographic Naming and Power</h2>
<p>From a behavioral standpoint, renaming geographic features is an ancient political tool designed to assert dominance and shape collective perception. When leaders rename spaces, they tap into fundamental human instincts regarding territory, belonging, and identity.</p>

<p>Psychologists often point to the concept of symbolic ownership. Naming is not merely descriptive; it is prescriptive. By labeling a shared international resource with a national descriptor, leaders invoke what social psychologists call in-group favoritism and out-group differentiation. It creates an immediate psychological boundary, signaling to domestic audiences that their identity and preeminence are being actively championed.</p>
