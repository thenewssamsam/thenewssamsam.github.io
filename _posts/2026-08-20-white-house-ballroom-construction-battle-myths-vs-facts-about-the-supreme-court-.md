<section>
    <p>The legal battle over a sprawling new 90,000-square-foot ballroom at the White House has officially landed at the Supreme Court, sparking intense national debate over executive authority, historic preservation, and the constitutional limits of presidential remodeling. With the Trump administration pressing forward with rapid construction—claiming the project is now roughly 65 percent complete—lawyers for the National Trust for Historic Preservation have urged the high court to leave lower court orders in place to halt the work.</p>
    
    <p>Whenever high-profile federal properties, legal injunctions, and executive actions collide, public discussion is quickly flooded with talking points, rumors, and sweeping legal claims. To help cut through the noise, let us examine what is actually happening in the Supreme Court docket by separating prevailing legal myths from verified facts.</p>
</section>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the White House Ballroom Dispute?</h2>

<section>
    <p>The controversy began when the Trump administration announced plans in July 2025 to tear down the East Wing of the White House to build a massive new event space, citing a century-and-a-half-long need for a large-scale gathering venue on the complex. In response, the National Trust for Historic Preservation filed a federal lawsuit in December, arguing that the project violates federal oversight laws because the National Capital Planning Commission and the Commission of Fine Arts have not properly approved the plans, and Congress has not authorized funding for the structure.</p>

It explicitly permitted the continuation of underground developments, such as bomb shelters and bunkers, as well as any security or safety-related construction. Furthermore, the injunction has been repeatedly paused or stayed pending appeals, allowing the administration to push the overall project to approximately 65 percent completion.</p>
