<section id="what-happened">
<p>When the thermometer pushes past 100 degrees for days on end, life changes in subtle, exhausting ways. The National Weather Service in Oxnard has issued severe warnings across Southern California, projecting that the coming days will bring the absolute hottest temperatures of 2026. Inland areas, coastal valleys, and even traditionally temperate beach communities are bracing for extreme highs ranging from 90 to 110 degrees.</p>

<p>According to the latest meteorological updates, a high-pressure system aloft combined with a northerly wind shift is creating a severe compressional warming effect. Downtown Los Angeles hit 96 degrees early in the cycle, with a 50-50 chance of reaching 100 degrees, while valleys like the San Fernando region face a 40% probability of touching a scorching 110 degrees. Forecasters note that this is not a brief afternoon spike, but a prolonged, multi-day endurance test that carries high risks of heat-related illness and heightened wildfire dangers.</p>

<h3>The Normalcy Bias and Heat Complacency</h3>
<p>One of the most dangerous psychological phenomena during prolonged heat waves is <em>normalcy bias</em>—our deep-seated tendency to underestimate disaster and assume tomorrow will be just like yesterday. Because warm weather is a normal part of the Southern California experience, residents often brush off initial warnings. People delay turning on air conditioning, skip hydration habits, or venture out into remote desert areas—such as the tragic recent incident in Death Valley—underestimating how rapidly extreme heat can overwhelm the human body.</p>

<h2>Behavioral Shifts: Frustration, Fatigue, and the Heat-Aggression Link</h2>
<p>It is not your imagination: people genuinely act more irritable when it is 110 degrees outside. Decades of criminological and psychological research point to the "heat-aggression hypothesis." As discomfort rises, so do rates of verbal friction, road rage, and interpersonal conflict.</p>
