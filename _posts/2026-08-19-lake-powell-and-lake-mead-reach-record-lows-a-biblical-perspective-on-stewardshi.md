    <h1>Lake Powell and Lake Mead Reach Record Lows: A Biblical Perspective on Stewardship</h1>
  </header>

  <p>The water flowing down the Colorado River has sustained generations of communities across the American Southwest, turning arid deserts into thriving cities and productive farmland. But that vital lifeline is now flashing warning signs. Lake Powell and Lake Mead—the nation's two largest reservoirs, which provide water and power to millions across Arizona, Nevada, and California—have officially hit record-low water levels following decades of drought, heavy use, and changing climate patterns.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Colorado River Basin?</h2>
  <p>According to recent reports from Arizona, Lake Powell has dropped more than 20 feet since the beginning of the year, reaching a troubling new low. The reservoir now sits just 30 feet above the critical threshold where its turbines would be forced to stop producing hydroelectric power. Meanwhile, Lake Mead, located further downstream, hit its own historic low point just a week prior.</p>
  <p>Experts from Arizona State University and the University of Arizona emphasize that while these historic lows were anticipated after decades of persistent overuse and a multi-decade drought, they highlight deep systemic vulnerabilities. Water experts reassure the public that faucets will not immediately run dry and that municipalities have planned for water level declines. However, households should prepare for rising water rates, and regional power grids face mounting pressure as hydroelectric generation wanes.</p>
