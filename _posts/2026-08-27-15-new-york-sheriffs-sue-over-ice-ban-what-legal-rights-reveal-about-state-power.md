<p>When fifteen county sheriffs across New York filed a federal lawsuit on August 25, 2026, they did not just challenge a piece of state legislation; they opened a profound constitutional frontline over the boundaries of local authority, state executive power, and federal immigration enforcement.</p>

<p>The legal challenge targets the newly enacted "Local Cops, Local Crimes Act," signed into law by Governor Kathy Hochul in May. As the statute took effect, it immediately banned local law enforcement agencies from engaging in formal or informal cooperation agreements with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and prohibited local jails from holding individuals on civil immigration detainers.</p>

Spearheaded by figures such as Madison County Sheriff Todd Hood and Oswego County Sheriff Don Hilton, the fifteen plaintiffs argue that state executives overstepped their constitutional boundaries by attempting to nullify federal immigration cooperation programs authorized by the U.S. Congress.</p>

<h2 id="the-legal-battle">The Legal Battle Over Federalism and Local Authority</h2>

<p>From a legal rights perspective, this lawsuit highlights the classic American tension known as federalism—the division of power between national and state governments. The core of the sheriffs' argument rests on the claim that local sheriff departments operate as independently elected constitutional officers whose authority derives from the people and federal statute, rather than direct administrative subordination to the state governor.</p>

<p>Congress has historically established frameworks like Section 287(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, which permits local law enforcement agencies to voluntarily enter into agreements to perform specified immigration enforcement functions. The sheriffs' legal team contends that a state legislature cannot unilaterally criminalize or prohibit participation in voluntary federal programs that Congress itself sanctioned.</p>
