  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened</h2>
    <p>Prominent Chinese artist Gao Zhen, known globally for his provocative and challenging sculptures of former leader Mao Zedong, has been sentenced to three years in a Chinese prison. The 70-year-old artist and US permanent resident was found guilty of slandering China's official "heroes and martyrs." This verdict arrives two years after Gao was initially detained upon returning to China with his family for a visit in August 2024, an ordeal that included raids on his studio near Beijing and severe health struggles while in custody.</p>

    <p>International human rights groups and artistic organizations have condemned the ruling. A central point of contention is the legal framework used against him: the artworks at the center of the case were created more than a decade before China enacted the specific law criminalizing the defamation of heroes and martyrs in 2018. Legal advocates note that applying this statute retrospectively creates a dangerous precedent, turning historical artistic expression into an retroactive crime scene.</p>

    <h3>The Echo of Retroactive Enforcement</h3>
    <p>Throughout history, authoritarian movements have frequently relied on ex post facto laws—laws applied retroactively to acts that were legal when committed. During various periods of ideological purging in the 20th century, cultural creators found themselves prosecuted under newly invented decrees that criminalized earlier satire or critique. By punishing Gao for sculptures crafted over ten years before the 2018 law existed, modern authorities are utilizing an old mechanism: keeping an entire community of creators in a permanent state of precarious uncertainty.</p>

    <h3>Satire as an Existential Threat</h3>
    <p>Gao Zhen's most iconic works—such as sculptures depicting Mao kneeling or facing a firing squad of Mao statues—use surrealism and subversion to puncture the armor of state mythology. Historically, regimes built on the infallibility of supreme leaders have always feared satire far more than direct political opposition. Laughter and artistic subversion expose the human fragility behind authoritarian monuments. When the state feels compelled to jail a 70-year-old artist over satirical sculptures, it reveals not strength, but a profound anxiety about the fragility of its official mythology.</p>
