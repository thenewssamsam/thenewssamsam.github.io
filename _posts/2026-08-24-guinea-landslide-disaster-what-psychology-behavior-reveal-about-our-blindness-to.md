<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in Conakry?</h2>
<p>Early on a Sunday, a mountainous heap of refuse collapsed at the largest landfill in Guinea's capital, Conakry, leaving at least 30 people dead according to local authorities. Heavy seasonal rains triggered the catastrophic failure of the unstable waste mountain, burying parts of the surrounding area and sending shockwaves through a community that had long lived in the shadow of the sprawling dump site. While emergency crews and local volunteers mobilized search-and-rescue operations in the treacherous muck, the disaster immediately raised urgent questions about urban planning, waste management, and the terrifying speed at which chronic environmental neglect turns acute.</p>

<div class="ncg-quick-take">
<strong>Quick Take:</strong> When a massive garbage mountain collapsed in Conakry, Guinea, following heavy rains, it claimed dozens of lives. Beyond the immediate tragedy, the event exposes a universal human tendency to normalize dangerous conditions until they erupt into unavoidable catastrophe.
</div>

<p>According to reports, the Conakry landfill had grown over many years into an enormous, unregulated artificial mountain of refuse, towering over nearby neighborhoods. Residents and environmental advocates had long voiced concerns regarding the structural integrity of the waste pile, especially during the heavy West African rainy season. Yet, like countless other overloaded municipal systems across the developing and developed world alike, daily life simply adapted to the looming hazard. People farmed near it, scavenged from it, and built homes beneath it. Until the earth gave way.</p>

<h2>The Psychology of Ignored Dangers</h2>
<p>Disasters like the Conakry landfill collapse rarely arrive without warning. More often, they are preceded by years of slow degradation, minor slips, and escalating hazards that everyone notices but few know how to fix. From a psychological standpoint, this collective inaction is driven by powerful cognitive mechanisms that help humans cope with everyday stress but tragically fail us when dealing with systemic threats.</p>

<p>Chief among these is <strong>normalcy bias</strong>—the psychological state of denial people enter when facing a disaster they have never experienced before. Everyone assumes someone else will address the hazard, resulting in a paralysis of action until a sudden downpour converts complacency into a mass-casualty event.</p>
