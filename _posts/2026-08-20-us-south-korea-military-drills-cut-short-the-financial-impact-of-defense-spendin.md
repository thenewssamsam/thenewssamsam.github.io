<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: Drills Halved</h2>
<p>The annual joint military exercises between the United States and South Korea, known as the Ulchi Freedom Shield (UFS), have been cut in half at the direct request of Washington. Originally scheduled for a 10-day run involving at least 18,000 South Korean troops and comprehensive live-training events, the exercise will now wrap up early on Friday. Pentagon officials confirmed that associated live-training events have been scaled back, with certain field components canceled or converted into digital simulations based on direction from President Donald Trump and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth.</p>

<p>The decision follows President Trump's public statements citing his personal relationship with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, alongside complaints regarding the high financial cost of maintaining these large-scale deployments. At the same time, South Korean President Lee Jae Myung expressed support for the diplomatic overture, while agreeing that Seoul must shoulder a greater share of its own defense responsibilities and contribute more actively to international maritime security efforts, such as restoring free passage at the Strait of Hormuz. For taxpayers, policymakers, and market watchers, this sudden shift lays bare the intricate financial machinery behind the seven-decade-long U.S.-South Korean security alliance.</p>
</section>

When President Trump lamented the cost of these exercises on social media, he touched on a fundamental economic tension in modern global defense: who pays for regional deterrence, and how do changing geopolitical postures impact national balance sheets?</p>
