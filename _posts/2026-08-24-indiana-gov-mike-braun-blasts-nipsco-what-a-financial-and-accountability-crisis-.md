    <h2>What Happened: The 110-MPH Derecho and the Two-Week Fallout</h2>
    <p>On August 11, 2026, a historic derecho tore through the Midwest, leaving a trail of catastrophic infrastructure destruction in its wake. For Northern Indiana Public Service Company (NIPSCO), the storm dealt a crippling blow, knocking out power to more than 60% of its total customer base—over 350,000 households and businesses.</p>
    
    <p>While NIPSCO crews have labored around the clock to rebuild shattered grids, progress in key municipalities like Gary, Portage, and Munster has dragged on far past what citizens consider acceptable. As of Sunday, more than 21,000 customers were still waiting for electricity, with full restoration estimates stretching dangerously close to the two-week mark.</p>

    <h2>The Financial Mechanics of Utility Monopolies</h2>
    <p>To truly understand Governor Braun’s frustration, we have to look past the political rhetoric and examine the unique economic structure of investor-owned utility monopolies. NIPSCO operates as a regulated monopoly within its service territory. Under this business model, consumers have zero market choice. You cannot simply fire NIPSCO and switch to a competing power provider down the street if their service falters.</p>

    <p>This market structure creates a profound economic imbalance. A utility monopoly, however, faces a different set of financial incentives. Their revenue streams are guaranteed by regional ratepayer contracts approved by state utility commissions, insulating them from traditional capitalist competition.</p>
