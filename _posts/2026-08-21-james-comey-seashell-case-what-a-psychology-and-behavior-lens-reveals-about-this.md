    <h2>What Happened inere in the Comey Seashell Case?</h2>
    <p>In a legal filing that reads more like a screenplay than a traditional federal brief, prosecutors in North Carolina have taken an unprecedented step in their criminal case against former FBI Director James Comey. They are using Comey’s very own fiction against him. Comey was indicted earlier this year on felony counts alleging he made a "true threat" against President Donald Trump by posting an Instagram photo of seashells arranged to spell out "8647"—with 47 referring to the 47th president.</p>
    
    <p>According to court documents filed by prosecutors, Comey's crime novel <em>FDR Drive</em>—released just days after his controversial social media post—mirrors the real-life allegations. The book features a prosecutor pursuing a political antagonist whose followers engage in targeted violence after being signaled online. Prosecutors argue this narrative demonstrates a pattern of communication, attempting to counter Comey’s defense that the post was an innocent beach walk photo and that the slang term "86" is widely used without murderous intent.</p>

    <h2>A Psychology &amp; Behavior Perspective</h2>
    <p>Whenever a high-profile public figure becomes the center of a legal storm, our minds naturally look for patterns. We want villains, heroes, and clear-cut motivations. But a closer examination through the lens of psychology reveals how cognitive mechanisms like confirmation bias, narrative transportation, and the ambiguity of digital communication heavily influence both sides of this courtroom drama.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-subhead">The Trap of Narrative Transportation</h3>
    <p>One of the most fascinating psychological elements in this filing is the prosecution's use of a novel. Psychologists use the term "narrative transportation" to describe how deeply people immerse themselves in stories, often blurring the lines between fiction and reality. Prosecutors are leveraging this very phenomenon: because Comey wrote a book about a fictional character who uses coded messages to incite followers, the government is inviting the judge and jury to view Comey's real-life actions through the prism of his own fictional creation.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-subhead">Ambiguity, Slang, and Confirmation Bias</h3>
    <p>The entire seashell dispute rests on a linguistic ambiguity: what does "86" actually mean? In restaurant culture, it means running out of an item or removing something from the menu. In colloquial speech, it can mean discarding or getting rid of something. In one obscure slang dictionary cited by prosecutors, it carries a darker definition.</p>

Both groups are looking at the exact same data—four digits arranged in seashells—yet their brains interpret it entirely through the lens of what they already believe about the actors involved. Psychology teaches us that when meaning is unclear, our minds project our deepest assumptions onto the canvas.</p>
