<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in the 2026 Primaries?</h2>
<p>The 2026 primary election cycle delivered a series of sharp political jolts across Florida, Wyoming, Alaska, and South Carolina. In the race to succeed term-limited Florida Republican Governor Ron DeSantis, high-profile Rep. Byron Donalds, backed by former President Donald Trump, secured a spot to face former Republican congressman and prominent Trump critic David Jolly. Meanwhile, political landscapes shifted dramatically elsewhere: democratic socialist Angie Nixon scored a major upset over former National Security Council aide Alex Vindman in the Florida Democratic Senate primary, and several Trump-backed incumbent or favored candidates—including Rep. Cory Mills in Florida and state Superintendent Megan Degenfelder in Wyoming—suffered surprising defeats.</p>

<p>Political pundits immediately scrambled to decode what these results mean for the shifting balance of power in Congress and state capitals. Yet beneath the horse-race statistics and strategist post-mortems lies a deeper behavioral story. Why do voters suddenly break away from established power structures? What drives communities to reject high-profile endorsements in favor of ideological underdogs or political outsiders? To understand these tectonic shifts, we have to look past political strategy and examine the underlying mechanisms of human psychology.</p>

<section id="psychology-of-the-upset">
<h2>The Psychology Behind Primary Upsets</h2>
<p>When voters flood the ballot boxes to overthrow heavily backed incumbents or reject anointed frontrunners, they are often acting on collective psychological impulses that behavioral scientists understand well. Chief among these is <strong>normalcy bias</strong> colliding with pervasive systemic fatigue. For years, voters have grown accustomed to predictable political friction, standardized talking points, and heavily financed institutional campaigns. When the perceived friction becomes too heavy, a threshold is reached.</p>

<section id="the-cost-of-cognitive-dissonance">
<h2>The Cost of Cognitive Dissonance in Politics</h2>
<p>Voters constantly navigate <strong>cognitive dissonance</strong>—the mental discomfort that arises when holding two conflicting beliefs, such as supporting a political party while deeply distrusting its specific candidates. In the case of Alex Vindman's defeat in Florida, Democratic strategists openly acknowledged a pragmatic reality: pouring millions into a deeply red state would have wasted donor resources. Local voters, processing this dissonance, opted for a candidate whose ideological clarity matched their grassroots convictions rather than leaning into a costly "resistance" narrative.</p>
