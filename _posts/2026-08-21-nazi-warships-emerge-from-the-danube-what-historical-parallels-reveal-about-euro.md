<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Relics of 1944</h2>
<p>The recent BBC report details how record-low water levels across Europe have laid bare the skeletal remains of a Nazi Black Sea fleet. In September 1944, as Soviet forces advanced on the Eastern Front, German Rear Admiral Paul-Willy Zieb ordered more than 200 vessels scuttled near the treacherous Iron Gates bottleneck on the Danube. The goal was twofold: prevent the ships from falling into enemy hands and obstruct the Red Army's advance.</p>

<p>Most of these iron hulls have stayed hidden beneath the riverbed ever since, partly because many still pack unexploded ordnance and hazardous materials. While Serbia has embarked on an ambitious 30 million euro removal program, progress is painstakingly slow. Today, however, an exceptional climate event has done what human engineers struggled to do quickly: nature has lowered the water, bringing rusted masts and explosive-laden decks back into the open air.</p>

<h2>Historical Parallels: When Rivers and Empires Recede</h2>
<p>From a historical parallel perspective, the reemergence of wartime armaments during a climate-induced drought carries a profound symbolic weight. Throughout history, major geopolitical collapses have often coincided with, or been exacerbated by, severe environmental stress. The scuttling of the Nazi fleet in 1944 was an act of desperation by a collapsing regime running out of geographical and military runway as Allied forces closed the net.</p>

<p>However, every historical comparison has its limits. While ancient droughts were natural climatic fluctuations recorded by chroniclers, today's extreme drying of European rivers is driven by rapid anthropogenic warming. Europe is currently the fastest-warming continent on Earth, heating at more than twice the global average. Thus, while the sunken ships remind us of mid-century fascism and military defeat, the shrinking riverbed acts as a modern-day barometer for contemporary ecological challenges.</p>
