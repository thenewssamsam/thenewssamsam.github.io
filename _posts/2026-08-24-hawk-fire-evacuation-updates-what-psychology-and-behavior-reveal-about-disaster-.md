For residents trapped in bumper-to-bumper traffic trying to flee or return to their properties, the immediate danger was compounded by severe gridlock. As emergency officials urge yellow-zone evacuees to stay away for a few days, the crisis highlights a recurring human pattern in fast-moving disasters: why we react the way we do when the worst happens.</p>

  <h3>1. Normalcy Bias and the Delay in Action</h3>
  <p>Normalcy bias is a cognitive state where people underestimate the possibility of a disaster or its effects on them. When placed in a yellow "get ready" zone, the natural human inclination is to wait and see. People hope the wind will shift back or the fire will miss their street. Unfortunately, this delay compresses thousands of individual decisions into a tiny window, transforming orderly departures into sudden, frantic bottlenecks.</p>

  <h3>2. Loss Aversion and the Urge to Return Home</h3>
  <p>Once people evacuate, a secondary psychological pressure kicks in: loss aversion. The deep emotional attachment to property, pets left behind, or cherished valuables creates intense anxiety. The impulse to drive back into a danger zone to check on a house is rooted in a powerful desire to prevent perceived loss. As Sheriff Balaam noted, however, those attempted returns often create counter-flows that block the very fire trucks needed to save those homes.</p>

  <p>Moments of sudden crisis and community disruption often invite deeper moral and spiritual reflection. When unexpected trials arrive like a windstorm, we are reminded of the fragility of our earthly routines and the deep responsibility we have toward one another.</p>
