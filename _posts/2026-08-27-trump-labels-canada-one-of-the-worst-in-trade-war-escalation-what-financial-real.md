  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 24px;">
    <h2 style="font-size: 24px; color: #111111; margin-top: 24px; margin-bottom: 12px;">What Happened: The Latest Trade Escalation</h2>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
      The verbal and economic clash follows months of friction. In a phone interview with right-wing radio presenter Glenn Beck, President Trump criticized Canada's long-standing tariffs on American dairy products and declared that it was time to "take them on." He argued that Canada takes economic advantage of the United States while erecting high barriers against U.S. agricultural exports.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
      This rhetoric arrived less than twenty-four hours after Ottawa struck back. Canadian Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne announced that Canada would double its existing counter-tariffs on U.S. steel and aluminum products to 50 percent. Furthermore, new tariffs ranging from 15 to 50 percent are slated to hit more than $20 billion worth of American goods—including clothing, furniture, video game consoles, and electronics—taking effect on September 8. To soften the blow for domestic industries, the Canadian federal government introduced a $5.4 billion support package.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="financial-perspective" style="margin-bottom: 24px;">
    <h2 style="font-size: 24px; color: #111111; margin-top: 24px; margin-bottom: 12px;">The Financial Impact: Who Pays and Who Profits?</h2>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
      From a purely financial standpoint, tariffs are not magical taxes paid by foreign governments; they are import taxes levied on domestic importers, which are almost invariably passed along to the end consumer in the form of higher prices. When Canada applies a 50 percent counter-tariff on U.S. steel and aluminum, American exporters in those sectors face an immediate hurdle in maintaining their competitive pricing in Canada—which imports roughly $272 billion in U.S. goods annually.
    </p>
    <div style="background-color: #f9f9f9; border-left: 4px solid #2e7d32; margin: 20px 0; padding: 16px 20px; border-radius: 4px;">
      <p style="font-style: italic; font-size: 16px; margin: 0; color: #222222;">
        "Tariffs are designed to alter trade flows, but their immediate financial mechanism is simple: higher transaction friction inevitably trickles down to higher retail prices for everyday goods."
      </p>
    </div>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
      Conversely, when Trump targets Canadian dairy and manufactured goods, American consumers looking to buy imported dairy or manufactured items may see cost increases. While supporters argue that such measures protect domestic industries from foreign subsidies and unfair dairy regimes, critics point out that retaliatory actions can squeeze specific sectors—such as agriculture and manufacturing—that rely heavily on frictionless cross-border commerce.
    </p>
  </section>
