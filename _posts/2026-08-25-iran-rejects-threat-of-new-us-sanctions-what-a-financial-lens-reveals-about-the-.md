<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in the U.S.-Iran Economic Standoff?</h2>
<p>The latest flashpoint in the U.S.-Iran conflict stems from a weekend exchange of defiant rhetoric and looming economic retaliation. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent signaled aggressive new financial measures following months of intense military engagement that began when the U.S. and Israel launched strikes on February 28. Despite severe damage to Iran's economy, navy, and air force, Tehran has successfully choked off traffic through the Strait of Hormuz—a vital global shipping lane—by refusing to allow unauthorized oil tankers to transit.</p>
<p>In response to the looming Treasury press conference, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi posted a video on Telegram dismissing the threat of new sanctions as repetitive and desperate. Meanwhile, diplomatic channels are quietly shifting. Pakistan's army chief, Asim Munir, is scheduled to visit Tehran to discuss regional security and the escalating economic pressures. At the same time, major energy buyers like China—which purchases over 80% of Iran's shipped oil according to analytics firm Kpler data—face mounting U.S. warnings against providing any financial lifeline to the Islamic Republic.</p>

<h3>The Strait of Hormuz Bottleneck</h3>
<p>The global economy runs on predictable shipping lanes. By bringing unauthorized tanker traffic to a virtual standstill in the Strait of Hormuz, Iran has weaponized its geographic position. Because a significant percentage of the world's petroleum supply passes through this narrow maritime corridor, restricting its flow immediately tightens global oil supply. From a purely economic standpoint, restricted supply coupled with steady global demand inevitably drives up crude oil prices.</p>
