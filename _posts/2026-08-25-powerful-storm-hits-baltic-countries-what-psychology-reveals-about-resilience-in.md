<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Baltic Cyclone Unfolds</h2>
<p>Over the weekend, a powerful storm system swept across the Baltic states, bringing torrential rain and ferocious winds that meteorologists in Latvia described as the most severe since 2005. Wind gusts exceeded 67 mph in the Latvian capital of Riga, while rainfall totals reached half of the monthly average in a single day.</p>
<p>The human and structural toll was immediate. Tragically, two people lost their lives—one in Jelgava, Latvia, when a building structure collapsed, and another in neighboring Lithuania after being struck by a falling tree. Across Latvia and Lithuania, more than half a million residents lost electricity, mobile networks experienced disruptions, and transportation networks ground to a halt. In Estonia, ferry services to western islands were suspended, and rail connections from the south faced severe delays.</p>
<p>While repair crews mobilized quickly to restore order as the system weakened, events like this force us to look past the immediate logistical cleanup and examine how human beings process sudden, overwhelming disruptions.</p>

<h3>1. Normalcy Bias and the Shock of the Unexpected</h3>
<p>Most of us live with an unspoken psychological baseline: the belief that tomorrow will look largely like today. When we check the morning forecast, we mentally filter weather alerts through a lens of routine disruption. A storm is usually just something that requires an umbrella or a slightly slower commute. When winds suddenly exceed 67 mph and buildings begin to collapse, our brains experience a jarring mismatch between expectation and reality. This is known as normalcy bias—our tendency to underestimate the likelihood of disaster and its potential severity until it is directly upon us.</p>

<h3>2. The Loss of Control and Information Vacuum</h3>
<p>Modern infrastructure gives us a comforting sense of absolute mastery over our environment. Flipping a switch brings light; tapping a screen connects us instantly to anyone on the globe. When a quarter-million people in Latvia and another 200,000 in Lithuania simultaneously lose electricity and mobile services, that digital safety net vanishes. The sudden information vacuum breeds anxiety. Human beings are fundamentally meaning-making creatures, and when sensory inputs and communication channels are cut off, the brain works overtime to fill in the gaps with worst-case scenarios.</p>
