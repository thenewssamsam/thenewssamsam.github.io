  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened at Centennial Mills?</h2>
    <p>For more than twenty years, the decaying shell of Centennial Mills stood along Portland's Willamette River as a monument to unrealized potential. Recent events, including a major fire that raised hazardous material concerns regarding asbestos and lead, have finally forced the city's hand, prompting demolition crews to tear down sections of the historic flour mill.</p>

    </div>

    <p>Yet, as the wrecking balls swing, long-standing questions are resurfacing about how public funds were managed. KATU reports that public investment stretches back decades, with a September 2006 city report revealing that "more than $12 million of tax increment and other funding" had already been funneled into acquiring the site and making initial improvements.</p>
    
    <p>Despite pouring millions into the property over a concentrated six-year period, municipal leaders failed to secure a viable redevelopment project. The city ultimately sold Centennial Mills to a private developer in 2024, closing a multi-decade chapter of public ownership characterized by red tape, rising costs, and missed opportunities.</p>

    <h2>Financial Lessons for Urban Development</h2>
    <p>The Centennial Mills saga offers a sobering case study in municipal asset management. Financial stewardship in local government requires recognizing when a project has exceeded its risk threshold. Continuing to sink funds into a distressed asset simply because a large sum has already been spent is a classic behavioral trap known as the sunk cost fallacy.</p>
