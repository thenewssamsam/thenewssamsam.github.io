Yet, beneath the glittering headlines of rising bullion prices and robust mining cash flows lies a quieter, more unsettling reality: the vast majority of retail investors remain profoundly unprotected against systemic market shifts. When former investment banker Felix Prehn recently sat down with Kitco News, he didn't just break down why gold is rallying; he exposed a dangerous paradox at the heart of modern household wealth.</p>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Actually Happened in the Markets</h2>
    <p>Gold has notched five consecutive up weeks, putting the precious metal on pace for its best monthly performance since 1999. Trading around $4,650 an ounce, the metal is enjoying renewed institutional and retail interest despite sitting below its late-January peak. But gold is only one piece of a much larger, highly volatile macroeconomic puzzle.</p>
    <p>Consider the broader financial landscape: The 30-year Treasury yield recently touched 5.34%, hovering near a 20-year high, while total federal debt has surpassed $40 trillion. To manage this mountain of debt, the U.S. Treasury announced plans to double its long-dated bond buybacks—moving from $2 billion per operation to at least $4 billion. While officials frame this as routine liquidity support, critics like Prehn argue it functions as artificial economic stimulus designed to prop up bond prices and lower government borrowing costs.</p>

  <section id="financial-impact">
    <h2>Financial Impact: Understanding Gold, Bonds, and the Dollar</h2>
    <p>Why did gold stumble earlier in the year despite global geopolitical conflicts like the war in Iran? Prehn clarifies a common misconception: gold does not automatically surge simply because a war breaks out. Instead, geopolitical conflict often sparks oil price spikes, which drive inflation and force bond yields higher. When government debt pays over 5%, institutional money frequently flees a non-yielding asset like gold in favor of guaranteed paper returns.</p>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box" style="border-left: 4px solid #2c3e50; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 16px 20px; margin: 24px 0; border-radius: 6px;">
      <p style="font-size: 1.15rem; font-style: italic; color: #2c3e50; margin: 0;">"Gold doesn't actually go up; the dollar goes down. It's your insurance. It's like your car insurance—you fall back on it so you can sleep better at night."</p>
      <p style="text-align: right; margin: 8px 0 0 0; font-size: 0.9rem; font-weight: bold; color: #555;">— Felix Prehn</p>
    </div>

    <p>Furthermore, major mining companies are experiencing a golden age of free cash flow—Newmont posted a record $2.2 billion last quarter, while Agnico Eagle cleared $1.3 billion. Because the capital expenditure cycle to bring a new mine online stretches 15 years or longer, this cash windfall will not easily translate into a sudden surge in mineral supply. This structural supply constraint suggests the precious metals mining sector may have longer legs than traditional market cycles imply.</p>
