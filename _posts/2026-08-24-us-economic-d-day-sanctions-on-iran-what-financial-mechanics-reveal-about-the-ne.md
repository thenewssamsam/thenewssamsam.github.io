<h2>What Happened: The Countdown to Economic D-Day</h2>
<p>The United States is preparing to unveil what Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has termed an <strong>economic D-Day</strong> for Iran. Following a high-profile op-ed in the <em>Financial Times</em> and direct warnings from President Donald Trump, the Treasury Department is set to detail a sweeping financial offensive aimed at crippling Tehran's remaining economic lifelines. This escalation comes amidst a protracted six-month conflict that has strained global shipping through the crucial Strait of Hormuz and left domestic U.S. economic metrics—particularly inflation and gas prices—feeling the strain.</p>

<p>By all accounts, the pressure is mounting on both sides. While Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian recently conceded that the nation "cannot continue with war forever," highlighting growing divisions within Tehran's leadership, security hardliners have vowed to neutralize the economic pressure and threaten further disruptions to regional transit. Yet, as Washington prepares its hardest-hitting financial restrictions yet, economists and geopolitical analysts are asking a fundamental question: Can financial pressure alone force a regime change or a strategic surrender when decades of prior sanctions have failed to do so?</p>
