  <p class="ncg-lead">When a fast-moving wildfire forces tens of thousands to flee their homes with only minutes of warning, the immediate physical danger is obvious—but for families with children, the psychological shock and sudden disruption leave deep, lasting impressions. As the out-of-control Hawk Fire swept across the Peavine Peak area near Reno, Nevada, displacing roughly 90,000 residents and destroying neighborhoods, it brought home a terrifying modern reality for parents: how do you protect, comfort, and guide your children when the ground beneath your feet literalizes uncertainty?</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened-in-reno">What Happened: The Rapid Ascent of the Hawk Fire</h2>
  <p>The Hawk Fire began on Saturday and quickly exploded into a massive, 23-square-mile disaster across the Humboldt-Toiyabe National Forest foothills. Driven by shifting, erratic winds that felt like an oven blast, the fire moved so quickly on Saturday night that it jumped over fire trucks and outpaced mobile tracking apps, catching families completely off guard.</p>

  <p>Washoe County officials issued urgent "GO NOW" evacuation orders affecting some 42,000 residents, with an additional 45,000 people placed under evacuation warnings right at the edge of the University of Nevada. At least six people—including three first responders and three civilians—sustained injuries amid zero containment and extreme fire behavior. For newcomers and long-time residents alike, the suddenness of the disaster served as a jarring reminder of how fragile our daily routines can be when severe weather strikes.</p>

  <h2 id="parenting-perspective">A Parenting &amp; Family Perspective: Guiding Children Through Sudden Trauma</h2>
  <p>For parents like Cari Kieffer, whose home was entirely reduced to ash with only a scorched basketball hoop remaining, the hardest part wasn't just losing belongings—it was explaining that loss to crying children. When a 16-year-old, a 3-year-old, and everyone in between watch their sanctuary vanish in hours, standard routines shatter.</p>

In chaotic moments like the Reno evacuations, parents are forced to balance physical safety with emotional triage.</p>

  <h3 class="ncg-subhead">Age-Appropriate Communication During Wildfires</h3>
  <p>Talking to young children about a wildfire requires brutal honesty tempered with absolute reassurance of safety. For younger kids, keeping explanations simple and focused on physical safety ("We are leaving to go on an adventure to a safe place with our pets") prevents overwhelming terror. For teenagers, acknowledging the genuine unfairness and heartbreak of the loss validates their grief rather than brushing it aside with toxic positivity.</p>
