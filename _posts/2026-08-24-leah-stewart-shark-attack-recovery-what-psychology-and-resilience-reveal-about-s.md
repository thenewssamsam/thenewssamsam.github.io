    When 34-year-old Australian mother Leah Stewart woke from an induced coma after a brutal shark attack at Sydney's Coogee Beach, she faced a reality that few of us can fathom. Months later, as she shares her first public comments about receiving a "second chance at life," her journey offers a profound look into human resilience, the psychology of trauma survival, and the fierce instinct that drives us to fight when the unthinkable happens.
  </p>

  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-top: 2rem;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3436; border-bottom: 2px solid #dfe6e9; padding-bottom: 0.5rem;">What Happened at Coogee Beach</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      The incident occurred earlier this year during a morning swim at Coogee Beach, one of Sydney's most popular coastal spots. Leah Stewart, a 34-year-old mother, was suddenly mauled by a shark in an attack that left her with severe bites to her arms and legs. The encounter unfolded amid a broader, concerning spate of shark attacks across Australia, which has seen heightened incidents in coastal waters.
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      The physical toll was catastrophic. Stewart was rushed to the hospital, placed in an induced coma, and underwent multiple grueling surgeries, including the amputation of one of her arms. Yet, in an online update released on Monday—her first public comments since the attack—she revealed that she has finally begun to build some independence. Over $550,000 AUD has been raised through an online fundraiser, allowing her to recover at home and access ongoing medical care. But more than the physical healing, it is her psychological turnaround that has captivated observers worldwide.
    </p>
  </section>

    <p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic; color: #856404;">
      "I want you to know that I feel like I've been given a second chance at life, and I'm not going to waste it." — Leah Stewart
    </p>
  </div>

  <section id="psychology-perspective" style="margin-top: 2rem;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3436; border-bottom: 2px solid #dfe6e9; padding-bottom: 0.5rem;">A Psychological Perspective on Survival and the Will to Live</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      To understand how someone survives a catastrophic predatory attack and emerges talking about gratitude rather than bitterness, we have to look through the lens of human psychology. When faced with mortal danger, the human brain instantly triggers the sympathetic nervous system—the fight-or-flight response. However, in extreme entrapment scenarios where fleeing is impossible, a different psychological mechanism takes over.
    </p>

    <h3 style="color: #2d3436; margin-top: 1.5rem;">The Power of Altruistic Motivation in Crisis</h3>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      In an upcoming interview with Nine Network's <i>60 Minutes</i>, Stewart recalled seeing the shark's "horrific" face, describing it as a "monster." But what pushed her through the agony was not mere instinct; it was a profound external anchor: her young daughter, August. 
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      Psychologists note that altruistic motivation—fighting for the sake of another when survival for oneself feels bleak—radically alters pain tolerance and endurance. Stewart recalled thinking, <i>"She's gonna grow up without a mum... I didn't want her to not have a mum. I just thought, I've got to fight it, do everything I can to be here."</i> By shifting the psychological burden from personal suffering to parental duty, her mind unlocked an extraordinary reservoir of agency during a moment of utter helplessness.
    </p>

    <h3 style="color: #2d3436; margin-top: 1.5rem;">Post-Traumatic Growth vs. Post-Traumatic Stress</h3>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      Media coverage of trauma often fixates exclusively on post-traumatic stress—the anxiety, flashbacks, and despair that naturally follow a near-death experience. Yet psychology also recognizes a phenomenon known as post-traumatic growth (PTG). Coined by psychologists Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, PTG describes the positive psychological change experienced as a result of struggling with highly challenging life crises. 
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      Stewart's statement—<i>"You've inspired me to get better, not bitter"</i>—is a textbook manifestation of cognitive reframing. Instead of asking "Why me?", survivors who experience growth often pivot to "What now?" By channeling the global outpouring of love from family, friends, and strangers into a catalyst for recovery, she actively restructured her narrative from victimhood to survival.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="moral-dimension" style="margin-top: 2rem;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3436; border-bottom: 2px solid #dfe6e9; padding-bottom: 0.5rem;">Reflections on Grace, Fragility, and Stewardship</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      Stories of sudden trauma inevitably force us to confront the profound fragility of human life. We live in a world governed by unpredictable physical forces, where a routine morning swim can transform into a life-or-death struggle in seconds. This stark reality often prompts a deeper spiritual and moral inventory within us.
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      Reflecting on these moments reminds me that life is an unearned gift—something delicate and fleeting that is easily taken for granted during the mundane rush of daily responsibilities. When Scripture notes in James 4:14, <i>"You do not know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes,"</i> it isn't meant to fill us with morbid dread. Instead, it invites us to cherish the present breath, to repair broken relationships, and to live with intentional purpose.
    </p>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      Leah Stewart’s determination not to waste her "second chance" echoes a timeless moral truth: suffering, while painful and uninvited, can strip away trivial distractions and reveal what truly matters. In the words of Psalm 118:17, <i>"I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the Lord."</i> Whether viewed through a lens of faith or deep humanism, survival from the edge calls us to extend grace to others, lean on community, and embrace each ordinary day as a remarkable gift.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="how-readers-respond" style="margin-top: 2rem;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3436; border-bottom: 2px solid #dfe6e9; padding-bottom: 0.5rem;">How Should Readers Respond?</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      It is easy to read a harrowing headline, feel a momentary shudder of sympathy, and scroll on to the next news item. But stories like Leah Stewart's offer practical lessons for our own daily lives:
    </p>
    <ul style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584; margin-top: 1rem;">
      <li style="margin-bottom: 0.75rem;"><strong>Reframe Daily Stresses:</strong> When minor frustrations threaten to derail your day, pause and contrast them against true adversity. Ask yourself whether a current annoyance will matter a year from now.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 0.75rem;"><strong>Lean on Community:</strong> Stewart emphasized the "army of love" that carried her family through dark weeks. Never isolate yourself when facing personal trials; allow friends and community to support you.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 0.75rem;"><strong>Cultivate Intentional Gratitude:</strong> Like a survivor waking up to a second chance, approach your relationships and health as gifts rather than guarantees. Tell the people you love what they mean to you today.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section style="margin-top: 2.5rem; background: #f1f2f6; padding: 2rem; border-radius: 8px;">
    <h2 style="color: #2d3436; margin-top: 0; font-size: 1.5rem;">One Last Thought</h2>
    <p style="line-height: 1.7; color: #4b6584;">
      We cannot control the unpredictable dangers that wait in the physical world, nor can we shield ourselves from every unexpected storm. But we do have a choice in how we respond when the waters grow rough. Leah Stewart’s courage reminds us that even after surviving the unthinkable, the human spirit doesn't merely endure—it can choose to shine with renewed purpose.
    </p>
    <p style="font-style: italic; color: #2d3436; margin-top: 1rem; font-weight: 500;">
      A second chance at life isn't just about cheating death; it's about choosing how fully we decide to live from this moment forward.
    </p>
