  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Dual Blow to Hawaii</h2>
    <p>According to updates from the NOAA National Hurricane Center, Tropical Storm Moke is passing to the south of the Hawaiian islands, but its outer bands are already impacting the Big Island and eastern Maui. Forecasters warn that this rainfall could produce life-threatening floods and mudslides, particularly in deep terrain and areas recently battered by Hurricane Lala.</p>
    <p>The timing could hardly be worse. Hurricane Lala—which dropped over 30 inches of rain in a short window—did not technically make landfall, yet its torrential downpours destroyed bridges, washed out roads, and swept homes off their foundations. As of Saturday morning, over 8,000 residents remained without power, with local utility provider Hawaiian Electric estimating full restoration might not occur until September 8. Tragically, the storm also claimed at least one life: a 90-year-old woman caught in the floodwaters. In response, Hawaii Governor Josh Green has requested a formal emergency declaration from the White House, while local leaders like Hawaii County Mayor Kimo Alameda urge citizens to prepare for another round of severe disruptions.</p>

    <p>Human beings are remarkably adaptable creatures capable of mustering extraordinary adrenaline and focus during an acute crisis. When Hurricane Lala struck, communities rallied, emergency services mobilized, and residents hunkered down. However, the human brain relies on predictable cycles of stress and recovery to maintain emotional equilibrium.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias Versus Hyper-Vigilance</h3>
    <p>In the wake of back-to-back weather events, people often swing between two psychological extremes. On one hand, normalcy bias whispers that "lightning doesn't strike twice," leading some to underestimate new warnings. On the other hand, repeated trauma can induce hyper-vigilance—an exhausting state of constant alertness where every drop of rain sounds like a rising flood. Understanding these normal psychological reactions helps communities normalize their stress response rather than internalizing it as personal weakness.</p>
