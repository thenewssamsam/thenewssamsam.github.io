<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened</h2>
<p>After nearly nine years behind bars, Turkish activist and philanthropist Osman Kavala has received a definitive, two-part order from the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) Grand Chamber demanding his immediate release and the complete nullification of his criminal conviction. Convicted of attempting to overthrow the government through his peaceful involvement in the 2013 Gezi Park protests, Kavala has consistently denied all charges. The ECHR Grand Chamber, convened under exceptional circumstances, backed Kavala 15-2, declaring that Turkish courts lacked any proof of violence and utilized the legal system for political retaliation to silence dissent.</p>

<p>The ruling represents an unprecedented escalation in a multi-year standoff between Ankara and Europe's top human rights court. Previously, Turkey refused to comply with binding 2019 and 2022 judgments, prompting rare enforcement procedures by the Council of Europe. With the Grand Chamber's final decision now delivered and no further appeals possible, the case places fundamental questions of judicial independence, state power, and the international rule of law squarely in the global spotlight.</p>

<h2>A Legal Rights Perspective</h2>
<p>To understand the gravity of the ECHR Grand Chamber's intervention, we have to look past the political rhetoric and examine the core tenets of legal rights and due process. In any functioning constitutional democracy, criminal liability requires demonstrable proof of illegal conduct. A person cannot be held legally responsible for the violent acts of others unless prosecutors can prove direct participation, incitement, or a clear conspiracy. According to the European judges, Turkish courts bypassed this foundational legal standard, treating peaceful meetings, research, and international contacts as criminal offenses.</p>
