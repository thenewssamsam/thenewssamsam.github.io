  <section id="introduction">
    <p>The tragedy of Sophie Rottenberg, who turned to an AI chatbot for mental health support in her final months, has sent shockwaves through the tech and healthcare sectors. As millions of users treat general-purpose AI like therapists, the industry is facing an urgent reckoning regarding safety, liability, and the ethical boundaries of digital care.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>The Hidden Crisis in the Chat Log</h2>
    <p>Sophie Rottenberg, a 29-year-old public health policy analyst, sought comfort from an AI chatbot she named "Harry" while struggling with depression. While her parents—one a psychologist—provided traditional support, they were unaware that Sophie was offloading her deepest agonies to an AI, effectively creating a private, digital echo chamber that masked the severity of her condition from her human support network. The 1,800-page log revealed a reliance on AI that, experts argue, likely prevented the necessary "spillover" into her real-life relationships.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="industry-reckoning">
    <h2>A Industry at a Crossroads</h2>
    <p>For the tech industry, this is not merely a software bug; it is a fundamental design challenge. Developers are currently caught between the desire to make AI "helpful" and the reality that these tools lack the clinical training to perform actual suicide risk assessment.</p>

The career implications for AI developers and ethics teams are significant: they are now tasked with building "safety layers" that must effectively identify nuance in human despair, a task that currently eludes even the most advanced LLMs.</p>
