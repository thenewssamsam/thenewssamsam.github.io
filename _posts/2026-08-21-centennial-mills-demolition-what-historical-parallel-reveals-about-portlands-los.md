  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section" style="margin-bottom: 40px;">
    <h2 style="font-size: 1.6rem; color: #1e293b; border-bottom: 1px solid #e2e8f0; padding-bottom: 8px; margin-bottom: 20px;">What Happened: The End of Centennial Mills</h2>
    <p>For more than two decades, the sprawling, graffiti-scarred hulk of Centennial Mills stood on the Portland riverfront like a ghost ship moored to the Willamette. On a Monday morning in August 2026, that ghost finally vanished. A controlled explosive blast brought down the iconic water tower—a landmark that once adorned neighborhood street signs—following a mysterious fire that left the historic structures structurally compromised.</p>
    
    <p>For Roger Farnen, a former superintendent who spent his 30s managing the roaring machinery inside, watching the demolition was equal parts closure and grief. ADM, the mill's final operator, shut down the facility in 2000 and sold it to the city's economic development agency. What followed was 26 years of failed redevelopment pitches, political inertia, squatters, and what architecture writer Brian Libby aptly termed "demolition by neglect."</p>

When the machinery finally stopped running in 2000, workers drew the grain tanks low, running the mills dry until the spigot was turned off for good. Now, the rubble is all that remains.</p>

    <p>The fate of Centennial Mills is far from an isolated incident. Across American cities, the transition from heavy industrial waterfronts to post-industrial service and residential hubs has followed a remarkably consistent, often painful script.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.25rem; color: #334155; margin-top: 20px;">1. The Shift from Production to Consumption</h3>
    <p>In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, rivers were the lifeblood of commerce. Cities grew up around mills, stockyards, and shipping docks because moving heavy bulk goods required water transport. Portland was no exception; the grain trade anchored the local economy. However, as trucking, containerization, and modern supply chains evolved, riverfront industrial sites became obsolete.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.25rem; color: #334155; margin-top: 20px;">2. The Purgatory of Bureaucratic Wish Jars</h3>
    <p>When private industry pulls out, municipal agencies often step in with grand visions. In Portland's case, Centennial Mills became a symbol of paralysis—caught between public parks, nightclubs, housing developments, and the competing gravity of neighboring projects like the Pearl District. When a property becomes everything to everyone, it often ends up being nothing to anyone. Decades of debate without capital commitment invite decay.</p>

    <h3 style="font-size: 1.25rem; color: #334155; margin-top: 20px;">3. The Illusion of Progress Without Preservation</h3>
    <p>Historical parallels show that cities frequently struggle to bridge their working-class past with their modern identity. While neighborhoods like the Pearl District thrive with new apartments and gleaming sidewalks, the physical monuments of the labor that built those cities are often quietly bulldozed. Progress does not always require destroying the past, but neglect makes demolition inevitable.</p>
