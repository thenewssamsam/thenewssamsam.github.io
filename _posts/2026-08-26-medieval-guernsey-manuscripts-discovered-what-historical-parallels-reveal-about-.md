    <section id="introduction" style="margin-bottom: 30px;">
        <p style="font-size: 1.15rem; font-weight: 500; color: #2b6cb0; margin-bottom: 20px;">Seventeen fragments of medieval manuscripts, hidden in plain sight for centuries within the Island Archives and His Majesty's Greffier's strongroom in Guernsey, have fundamentally transformed our understanding of the Channel Island's early literary life. Until this unexpected discovery, historians operated under the assumption that medieval Guernsey was virtually devoid of libraries, extensive book collections, or organized literary culture.</p>
        
        <p>Instead, as experts like University of Oxford book historian Professor Tamara Atkin and Dr. David Rundle from the University of Kent have revealed, late medieval Guernsey was home to engaged readers who possessed access to scholastic, liturgical, and literary works—preferring French over English for much of their reading. This remarkable find did not emerge from a grand, undisturbed medieval chest; rather, it came to light through the historical practice of vellum recycling, where precious texts were repurposed as administrative notebook covers and record bindings.</p>
    </section>

    <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 30px;">
        <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: #2d3748; border-left: 4px solid #3182ce; padding-left: 12px; margin-top: 40px; margin-bottom: 20px;">What Happened: Uncovering the Guernsey Archives</h2>
        
        <p>The discovery began with a routine archival search when assistant Callum Tostevin-Hall sought a 16th-century copy of Edward III's 1331 Extente of Guernsey, a document outlining Crown rights on the island. Noticing a shelfmark reference to a vellum cover carrying Latin text that had been removed during an earlier conservation phase, Tostevin-Hall inspected the material and recognized a reused bifolium from a medieval manuscript.</p>
        
        <div style="background-color: #fffaf0; border-left: 4px solid #dd6b20; padding: 15px; margin: 20px 0; border-radius: 0 8px 8px 0;">
            <p style="margin: 0; font-size: 0.95rem; color: #7b341e;"><strong>Key Detail:</strong> Out of the seventeen identified fragments, many are written in Latin, while six are in French. Among them is an exceptionally rare fragment of <em>L'Estoire de Griseldis</em>, a dramatic secular poem thought to have been written by Philippe de Mézières around 1395.</p>
        </div>

        <p>Island archivist Vikki Hart noted that this clever reuse of old parchment demonstrates that resourcefulness is far from a modern invention in Guernsey. By repurposing parchment sheets that had outlived their original utility, scribes and administrators unknowingly acted as preservationists, saving delicate texts from total destruction.</p>

        <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: #2d3748; border-left: 4px solid #3182ce; padding-left: 12px; margin-top: 40px; margin-bottom: 20px;">A Historical Parallel: The Art of Survival Through Recycling</h2>
        
        <p>To understand the magnitude of the Guernsey discovery, we must look at how medieval texts have historically survived. Vellum—made from treated calf, sheep, or goat skin—was an exceptionally durable yet costly writing surface. In medieval Europe, when books became obsolete, damaged, or superseded by newer editions, the parchment was rarely thrown away casually. Instead, it entered a secondary economic and administrative lifecycle.</p>

Monks and local administrators frequently sliced up liturgical texts and poetry to reinforce the spines of tax ledgers, court rolls, and account books. While modern archivists often lament the destructive cutting of medieval books, this exact act of functional recycling is the sole reason these fragments survived centuries of dampness, conflict, and shifting political loyalties.</p>

        <h3 style="font-size: 1.25rem; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 25px; margin-bottom: 15px;">The Linguistic Landscape of Medieval Channel Islands</h3>
        
        <p>The preference for French found in six of the Guernsey fragments highlights the deep cultural and linguistic ties between the Channel Islands and continental Europe. Long before modern geopolitical boundaries hardened, intellectual trade routes flowed freely between England, France, and the islands. Texts traveled across the Channel not as pristine library acquisitions, but through trade, diplomacy, and personal travel.</p>
