<h1>Tropical Storm Lala in Hawaii: What History and Human Resilience Teach Us About Resilience</h1>
<div class="ncg-meta-info">
<time datetime="2026-03-31">Published: March 31, 2026</time> &bull; <span>6 min read</span>
</div>
</header>

<p>When most people picture Hawaii, they think of the bustling beachfronts of Waikiki or the devastating burn scars of recent historic fires in Lahaina. But on Monday, the spotlight shifted abruptly to the rugged, rural southern end of the Big Island. Tropical Storm Lala—having weakened from a hurricane just before making landfall—lashed the Hawaiian Islands with relentless rain, uprooting trees, knocking out power for more than 104,000 customers, and dumping a staggering 43.54 inches of rain in Laupahoehoe. As communities like Naalehu, Pahala, and Punaluu grapple with blocked roads, isolated neighborhoods, and washed-out cemeteries, the disaster forces us to look past the postcard images of paradise and confront the raw, recurring vulnerability of island life.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Hawaii?</h2>
<p>Tropical Storm Lala swept past Hawaii’s main islands over the weekend, packing sustained tropical storm-force winds and torrential downpours. While the wind caused significant structural damage and downed transmission towers, it was the catastrophic flooding that proved most devastating. Gov. Josh Green, who surveyed the damage firsthand, reported that mud, rocks, and raging floodwaters poured through rural southern communities, sweeping roughly 100 homes from their foundations.</p>

<p>The storm's impact was acutely felt in remote areas like the Kau district. State Rep. Jeanné Kapela described her district as being "in shambles," with collapsed roads trapping roughly 100 households in places like Punaluu and washing out two cemeteries in Naalehu, disturbing sacred ancestral skeletal remains known as *iwi kupuna*. Emergency services faced temporary disruptions when 911 lines went down on the Big Island, and officials confirmed one fatality—a 90-year-old woman found dead after being reported missing. Fortunately, as Lala moved westward toward the open ocean and was forecast to regain hurricane strength far from land, officials noted that the overall damage, while severe for localized areas, avoided the absolute worst-case scenarios.</p>

<h2 id="historical-parallel">A Historical Parallel: The Echoes of Past Island Storms</h2>
<p>To understand the gravity of Tropical Storm Lala, we have to look back at how Hawaii and other isolated island communities have historically weathered nature's fury. Island geography creates a unique vulnerability: supply chains are narrow, terrain is steep and prone to landslides, and rural populations often lack the redundant infrastructure found in major mainland metropolitan areas.</p>
<p>Historically, storms like Hurricane Iniki in 1992 or Hurricane Lane in 2018 demonstrated that Hawaii's greatest hazard is rarely just the wind speed—it is stationary or slow-moving rainfall. Lane, for instance, dumped over 50 inches of rain in parts of the Big Island, creating devastating flash floods similar to the muddy torrents that recently tore through Naalehu and Pahala. In each of these historical instances, the immediate aftermath revealed a recurring pattern: while state capitals and tourist hubs recover quickly due to prioritized resources, rural, agricultural, and indigenous communities at the geographic margins bear the heaviest long-term burden of isolation and infrastructural repair.</p>

<blockquote class="ncg-quote-box">
"When infrastructure fails at the edge of the map, recovery is measured not just in restored power lines, but in how quickly a community reconnects with its heritage and each other."
</blockquote>

<h2 id="spiritual-perspective">Reflecting on Grace, Stewardship, and Vulnerability</h2>
<p>Witnessing the sudden fury of a tropical storm and the disruption of sacred resting places naturally brings a sobering reminder of human fragility. Scripture often uses the imagery of violent winds and overwhelming waters to remind humanity of both the power of the natural world and our shared dependence on a higher source of stability. As the Book of Nahum observes, "The Lord has His way in the whirlwind and in the storm, and the clouds are the dust of His feet" (Nahum 1:3). Yet, alongside that raw power runs a persistent theme of divine comfort and community steadfastness amid trial.</p>
<p>When disasters strike, they invite us to look closer at how we care for one another and steward the communities we inhabit. The Apostle Paul reminds believers in Galatians 6:2 to "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ." For the residents of the Big Island—suddenly cut off from basic communication, watching over damaged ancestral grounds, and checking on neighbors—this burden-bearing is not an abstract theological concept. It is the practical reality of neighbors clearing debris, leaders fighting for satellite internet access, and volunteers respecting sacred ground. In moments of disruption, we are reminded that our true security does not rest in concrete foundations or weather-resistant grid systems, but in our enduring commitment to compassion, resilience, and mutual aid.</p>

<h2 id="how-readers-respond">How Should Readers Respond?</h2>
<p>While most of us may watch Tropical Storm Lala unfold from afar on a news screen, disasters of this scale offer concrete lessons for anyone living in a vulnerable region:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Support Verified Relief Efforts:</strong> When rural and indigenous communities are hit, generalized aid can take time to reach them. Support reputable local organizations, mutual aid funds, and community-led recovery drives specifically targeting the Big Island's southern districts.</li>
  <li><strong>Review Your Own Emergency Preparedness:</strong> Lala’s impact on 911 services and power grids highlights the importance of having off-grid communication backups, emergency water supplies, and battery-powered radios.</li>
  <li><strong>Exercise Compassion and Respect:</strong> Heed local warnings—such as staying out of murky, debris-filled ocean waters—and respect sensitive cultural sites like disturbed burial grounds during cleanup efforts.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="one-last-thought">One Last Thought</h2>
<p>Tropical Storm Lala reminds us that nature operates on a scale far beyond human engineering, regularly testing the resilience of the places we call home. Yet, long after the winds die down and the floodwaters recede, the true measure of an island community is found not in the durability of its power poles, but in the steadfastness of its people looking out for one another in the dark.</p>
<p><em>In the wake of every storm, human resilience remains our most reliable shelter.</em></p>
