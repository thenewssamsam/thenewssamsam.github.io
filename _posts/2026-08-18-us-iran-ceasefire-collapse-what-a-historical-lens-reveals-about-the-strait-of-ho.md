<section id="historical-perspective">
<h2 style="font-size:1.5rem; color:#2c3e50; margin-top:2rem; border-bottom:2px solid #e9ecef; padding-bottom:0.5rem;">A Historical Lens: The Eternal Struggle for Chokepoints</h2>
<p>History rarely repeats itself word for word, but it frequently rhymes in the language of geography and leverage. The current standoff over the Strait of Hormuz—a narrow nautical artery through which a massive share of the world's petroleum flows—fits a centuries-old pattern of great powers clashing over strategic geographic bottlenecks.</p>

<p>Consider the historical parallels of maritime chokepoint control:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The Vital Bottleneck Dilemma:</strong> From ancient Athens controlling the grain supply through the Hellespont to the British Empire guarding the Suez Canal and the Strait of Malacca, dominant commercial powers have always viewed secure waterways as existential lifelines. When a regional actor gains the capability to threaten those narrow passages, the friction transforms local disputes into global economic crises instantly.</li>
<li><strong>The Brinkmanship Cycle:</strong> Historical crises between rival powers frequently feature strict ultimatums and ticking deadlines—much like the 60-day ceasefire window that just closed. History shows that these deadlines often force an uncomfortable choice: either back down and risk domestic political fallout, or escalate rhetoric until accidental miscalculation becomes the primary danger.</li>
<li><strong>The Limits of Alliances and Tributary Expectations:</strong> Trump's frustration with allied contributions—comparing American security guarantees in places like the Korean Peninsula with requests for regional cooperation—echoes the ancient Roman and British debates over burden-sharing. Empires and global superpowers have consistently struggled with the friction of asking regional allies to bear a share of maintaining the very global order that protects them.</li>
</ul>

<p>When oil prices jump past  a barrel on news of a diplomatic breakdown, it is not merely a financial statistic; it is a reminder of how deeply interconnected global energy supply chains remain. In previous eras, an interruption in regional shipping meant localized scarcity. In our modern globalized economy, a threat to the Strait of Hormuz transmits inflationary pressure directly to fuel pumps, manufacturing plants, and grocery shelves thousands of miles away.</p>

<p>This reality underpins the high-stakes leverage game currently being played. Tehran utilizes the threat of maritime disruption as an asymmetric counterweight against overwhelming conventional military superiority, while Washington employs economic sanctions, naval positioning, and diplomatic pressure to force compliance. History suggests that asymmetric leverage works effectively up until the moment it crosses the threshold into open conflict—at which point all parties typically find the costs far higher than anticipated.</p>
