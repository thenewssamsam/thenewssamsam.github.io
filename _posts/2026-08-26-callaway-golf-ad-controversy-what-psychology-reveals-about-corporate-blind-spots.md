<h2>What Happened: The Ad That Triggered Immediate Outrage</h2>
<p>When a major brand stumbles publicly, the aftermath usually follows a predictable script: a swift online firestorm, a hurried statement from the top executive, and promises to do better. That cycle played out once again when Callaway Golf CEO Chip Brewer issued a public apology for a promotional video created in collaboration with popular golf influencer group Good Good Golf.</p>
<p>The ad, which was quickly pulled from digital platforms after widespread condemnation, featured Good Good co-founder Garrett Clark telling former Division I golfer Alexis Miestowski in a menacing tone, "Do not touch my new driver," right before physically shoving her to the ground. The physical shove—designed perhaps as edgy humor or casual banter within certain online subcultures—instead struck a deep nerve with consumers, sparking intense backlash over depictions of violence against women.</p>

<h2>The Psychology Behind Corporate Blind Spots</h2>
<p>How does a major, multi-million-dollar corporation with seasoned marketing executives approve a video depicting a man shoving a woman to the ground to promote a sports equipment line? From a psychological perspective, this type of misstep rarely stems from outright malice. Instead, it is usually the result of well-documented cognitive biases and social dynamics.</p>

<p>The first is <strong>groupthink</strong>. When creative teams or brand partners operate in closed feedback loops—where everyone shares similar backgrounds, assumptions, or an eagerness to appeal to a specific "edgy" internet subculture—critical dissenting voices get muted. If one person in the room felt uncomfortable with the physical shove, social conformity bias may have kept them silent, assuming that others must see the humor or the marketing value.</p>

<p>Another psychological phenomenon at play is <strong>normalcy bias combined with insular framing</strong>. Creators immersed in fast-paced social media content creation often become desensitized to boundary-pushing imagery. They chase shock value or meme-worthy reactions without pausing to consider how the content translates to a broader, more diverse audience outside their immediate echo chamber. To them, it was just a staged bit of slapstick; to the wider public, it was an unwelcome reinforcement of real-world aggression.</p>
