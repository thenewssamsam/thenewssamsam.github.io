    <h1 id="top">Missing Long Beach Jet Skier Found Alive: What the Psychology of Survival Reveals</h1>
  </header>

  <p>When 53-year-old Paul Anthony Carbullido Jr. was finally spotted floating in a green life jacket three miles offshore from the Seal Beach Pier, a grueling 12-hour ordeal came to an end. For hours on end, a multi-agency search involving the Coast Guard, Long Beach Fire Department, and local police hunted through the dark Pacific waters. His miraculous rescue after half a day adrift raises a gripping question not just about maritime safety, but about the profound mental mechanics that keep a person alive when every instinct tells them to give up.</p>



  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Waters Off Long Beach</h2>
  
  <p>The dramatic incident began late Sunday night when the Long Beach Fire Department received urgent 911 calls reporting two jet skiers in distress around 10 p.m. Both riders had fallen into the water near the Long Beach Breakwater and found themselves unable to climb back onto their watercraft.</p>

  <p>Within minutes, a good Samaritan aboard a boat heard a woman yelling for help from the breakwater. She had displayed extraordinary physical grit by swimming for an hour through open water to find assistance. Responding lifeguards pulled her aboard a fire boat, and paramedics later transported her to a hospital in stable condition.</p>

  <p>However, her male companion remained missing. While fire boats and Coast Guard vessels located the abandoned jet skis around 1:45 a.m. Monday, there was no sign of Carbullido. Search crews scoured the dark ocean through the early morning hours until a Coast Guard helicopter finally spotted him at 10:48 a.m. floating three miles offshore. A rescue swimmer was lowered, and Carbullido was airlifted to safety, suffering from exposure and signs of hypothermia but ultimately in stable condition.</p>

  <h2 id="psychology-perspective">The Psychology of Survival: What Kept Him Afloat</h2>
  
  <p>When an individual is thrust into a sudden, life-threatening crisis in the open ocean, the human brain instantly transitions from routine cognitive processing into a high-stakes survival state. Psychologists who study extreme endurance events often point to a few core mental frameworks that spell the difference between despair and resilience.</p>

  <p>The first is overcoming <strong>normalcy bias</strong>—the psychological tendency to underestimate the danger or deny that a catastrophe is unfolding. Both riders had to quickly accept that their leisure activity had turned into a critical emergency, shedding panic to conserve energy. Once physical action is no longer possible—such as floating alone in the dark without a craft—survival shifts from physical exertion to mental endurance.</p>

  <p>In prolonged isolation, the brain must actively fight against feelings of learned helplessness. Psychologists note that survivors often compartmentalize their experience, focusing strictly on immediate, micro-goals: regulating breathing, scanning the horizon for the next glimmer of dawn, and maintaining body heat. The presence of a high-visibility green life jacket provided essential physical flotation, but the mental discipline to remain calm through twelve hours of cold, dark isolation is what allowed Carbullido to hold on until the Coast Guard helicopter arrived.</p>

  <h2 id="lessons-learned">What We Can Learn From the Incident</h2>
  
  <p>Stories like this serve as a powerful reminder of how quickly routine recreational outings can turn perilous on the water. While first responders and good Samaritans ultimately saved the day, the incident highlights several practical takeaways for anyone heading out onto the ocean:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>Gear Matters:</strong> Wearing a high-visibility life jacket is not merely a legal suggestion; it is the single most critical factor in search-and-rescue visibility from the air.</li>
    <li><strong>The Power of Endurance:</strong> The female companion’s hour-long swim to the breakwater underscores the extraordinary reserves of strength ordinary people can tap into under extreme pressure.</li>
    <li><strong>Multi-Agency Coordination:</strong> Seamless communication between local fire departments, local police, and the U.S. Coast Guard is essential for covering vast expanses of open water in dynamic conditions.</li>
  </ul>
