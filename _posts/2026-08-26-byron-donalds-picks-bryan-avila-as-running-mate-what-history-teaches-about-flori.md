    <h1 class="ncg-main-title">Byron Donalds Picks Bryan Avila as Running Mate: What History Teaches About Florida's High-Stakes GOP Strategy</h1>
    <p class="ncg-subtitle">By tapping State Sen. Bryan Avila at Miami's historic Freedom Tower, Florida Republican gubernatorial nominee Byron Donalds is leaning on a time-tested political playbook rooted in immigrant identity, regional coalition-building, and ideological contrast.</p>
  </header>

  <section class="ncg-content-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the Florida Gubernatorial Race?</h2>
    <p>Florida GOP gubernatorial nominee Byron Donalds announced his selection of state Sen. Bryan Avila as his running mate, broadcasting the ticket from Miami's iconic Freedom Tower—a landmark that once served as a processing center for Cuban refugees fleeing Fidel Castro's regime. Avila, a 42-year-old lawmaker representing Hialeah, brings deep legislative relationships, a military background as a Florida Army National Guard captain, and strong ties to South Florida's influential Hispanic community.</p>
    
    <p>The announcement underscores the shifting political landscape of Miami-Dade County, which has transitioned from a Democratic stronghold into a red-leaning battleground over the past two election cycles under Gov. Ron DeSantis and President Donald Trump. Donalds faces former U.S. Rep. David Jolly, the Democratic nominee, in a general election race that polling indicates will be closely contested. While Jolly attempts to position himself as a moderate, Republicans are working aggressively to tie the broader Democratic ticket to the party's left wing, utilizing Avila's personal history as the son of Cuban exiles to anchor an assertive anti-communist message.</p>

    <h2>A Historical Parallel: The Power of Identity and Geography in American Politics</h2>
    <p>When political candidates choose a running mate, they are rarely engaging in a purely administrative task. Throughout American history, the vice-presidential or running-mate slot has functioned as a strategic bridge across geographic, cultural, or ideological divides. Donalds' selection of Avila finds clear echoes in past electoral strategies where geography and cultural resonance dictated national or statewide victories.</p>

    <p>Consider the classic 1960 presidential campaign, where John F. Kennedy—a young, Catholic senator from Massachusetts—selected veteran Senate majority leader Lyndon B. Johnson of Texas. Kennedy needed Johnson not only to balance the ticket geographically by securing the conservative American South, but also to reassure voters who harbored doubts about Kennedy's background and governing capacity. In a similar fashion, Donalds—as the Republican nominee seeking to become Florida's first Black governor—chose Avila to consolidate support in South Florida's dense, highly independent Miami-Dade electorate. Avila's identity as a Cuban-American rooted in Hialeah serves as a cultural anchor for a coalition that Republicans have spent years cultivating.</p>

While Avila brings legislative wins regarding property tax relief and data center regulation, his past sponsorship of a 2024 law limiting local government power over older, flood-vulnerable buildings drew sharp criticism from preservationists in Miami Beach. In politics, every geographic and demographic gain often comes with localized trade-offs that opponents will test during the campaign trail.</p>
