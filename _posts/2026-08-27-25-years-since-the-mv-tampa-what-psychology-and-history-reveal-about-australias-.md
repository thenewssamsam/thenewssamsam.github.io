  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Day the Tampa Changed Everything</h2>
    <p>On August 26, 2001, the MV Tampa stepped in to save 433 people—predominantly refugees fleeing Afghanistan—from a stricken vessel in distress. Rather than coordinating a compassionate humanitarian landing, the Howard Government blocked the ship from docking on Australian shores. That single political maneuver birthed the "Pacific Solution," inaugurating an era of offshore processing, mandatory detention, and stringent maritime deterrence.</p>
    
    <p>According to ASRC CEO and Founder Kon Karapanagiotidis, the crisis did much more than reshape immigration statutes; it fundamentally rewired Australia's political culture. Politicians across the spectrum discovered an enduring electoral formula: manufacture existential anxiety around national borders, demonize vulnerable human beings seeking safety, and engage in a high-stakes competition over who could project the harshest stance.</p>

Why does the rhetoric of threat remain so stubbornly effective, even when the human costs—stolen years in detention, fractured families, and lives left in limbo—are painfully clear?</p>

    <h3>Loss Aversion and the Threat Matrix</h3>
    <p>Behavioral psychology teaches us about <em>loss aversion</em>: human beings feel the pain of a potential loss roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of an equivalent gain. In political discourse, politicians leverage this cognitive bias by framing migration not as an opportunity or a humanitarian duty, but as a direct threat to stability, resources, and cultural identity. Once a population perceives an existential threat—real or manufactured—rational deliberation shuts down, replaced by a defensive tribalism.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias and Normalizing Cruelty</h3>
    <p>Another powerful psychological phenomenon is <em>normalcy bias</em>—our tendency to underestimate disaster and refuse to look for things when we haven't experienced them before. Over 25 years, offshore processing and systematic deterrence have shifted from shocking emergency measures into the "normal" background hum of Australian policy. When extreme measures repeat over a generation, society develops a collective moral numbness, accepting institutionalized suffering simply because it has always been there.</p>
