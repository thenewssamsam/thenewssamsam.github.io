<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The $4 Billion Buyback and the $40T Milestone</h2>
    <p>As the U.S. national debt officially breaches the staggering $40 trillion mark, the federal government is scrambling to stabilize jittery bond markets. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent recently announced a plan to at least double the size of liquidity support buyback operations for 10- to 30-year bonds, totaling over $4 billion, scheduled between September and November 2026.</p>
    
    <p>The intervention was designed to cool down surging long-term yields. Just last week, 30-year Treasury yields hit a 19-year ceiling of 5.34% before retreating slightly to around 5.25%. While the surprise announcement successfully nudged yields down temporarily, major financial institutions are questioning whether this remedy is far worse than the disease.</p>

<section id="financial-mechanics">
    <h2>The Financial Mechanics: Trading Long-Term Debt for Short-Term IOUs</h2>
    <p>To understand why Wall Street is raising red eyebrows, we have to look at the mechanics of what the Treasury is actually doing. A bond buyback involves purchasing older, higher-yield long-term bonds off the open market to soothe nervous investors. However, to fund these buybacks, the government must issue shorter-term debt instruments.</p>
