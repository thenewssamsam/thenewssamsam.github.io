  <section id="ncg-what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The Autumn Sanctions Push</h2>
    <p>In a recent interview with German newspaper <em>Die Welt</em>, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas outlined a fresh strategic push to ramp up economic pressure on Moscow. According to Kallas, the upcoming proposals slated for the autumn will represent the most aggressive expansion of restrictions since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.</p>

    <p>The numbers under consideration are substantial. To date, the EU has approved 21 sanctions packages, targeting over 3,000 individuals and corporate entities while freezing more than €28 billion in private Russian assets. Kallas indicated that the upcoming autumn package could swell those lists by an additional 1,000 entities, pushing deeper into Russia's military-industrial complex, export channels, and financial networks.</p>
    <p>However, translating diplomatic ambition into binding European law remains a high-stakes legislative hurdle. Every single sanctions package requires the unanimous consent of all 27 EU member states—a voting structure that grants immense leverage to individual capitals seeking carve-outs for their domestic industries.</p>

  <section id="ncg-financial-perspective" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The Financial Impact: Costs, Leverage, and National Interests</h2>
    <p>From a financial perspective, economic warfare is a game of asymmetric friction. Kallas estimates that the Kremlin has already absorbed over one trillion euros in economic costs due to Western sanctions. Yet, the friction is felt unevenly across Europe, creating profound policy friction within the single market.</p>
    <p>The fragility of unanimous consensus was laid bare during negotiations over the bloc's 21st sanctions package. The measure was nearly derailed when Greece insisted on a special exemption from an EU-wide ban on shipping Russian liquefied natural gas (LNG). Despite sharp diplomatic pushback from other member states, Athens secured its exemption to protect domestic shipping and energy interests.</p>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>"When national balance sheets dictate geopolitical resolve, economic sanctions become a delicate negotiation between collective security and localized survival."</p>
    </div>

    <p>This dynamic illustrates a fundamental economic reality: broad policy strokes often mask narrow domestic exposure. When a country relies heavily on specific maritime transport revenues, energy imports, or manufacturing supply chains, an EU-wide ban threatens localized recession risks that national governments are politically compelled to resist. As Brussels prepares its expanded autumn listings, financial markets and policy analysts are watching closely to see which member states will demand economic concessions or financial buffers before giving their green light.</p>
  </section>
