<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened to the Swift Rescue Mission?</h2>
<p>Launched on July 3, Katalyst's Link satellite was designed to rendezvous with the nearly 22-year-old Swift gamma-ray observatory. Swift has far outlived its original two-year design life, serving astronomers as an indispensable tool for detecting gamma-ray bursts—the most powerful explosions in the known Universe. Because Swift lacks onboard thrusters to maintain its orbit, it faces an inevitable, fiery return to Earth's atmosphere later this year.</p>

<p>The rescue mission always carried immense risk. NASA awarded Katalyst a $30 million contract under an exceptionally tight nine-month schedule. Everything proceeded smoothly until late July, when the Link spacecraft suffered attitude control failures. Two of its three reaction wheels stopped working, alongside issues with cold gas thrusters. Despite switching to plasma thrusters, the spacecraft spun out of control, forcing teams to call off the rendezvous while keeping the door open for close-in navigation demonstrations.</p>

<h2>A Historical Parallel: The Cost of Speed and Innovation</h2>
<p>In the grand timeline of aerospace development, rushing a first-of-its-kind spacecraft from concept to launch pad in under a year is practically unprecedented. When NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman noted that the agency "should be willing to move quickly and take smart risks," he echoed a philosophy that has defined humanity's reach into the cosmos for decades.</p>
