    <h2>What Happened: A Double Blow to the Islands</h2>
    <p>Barely a week after Hurricane Lala battered the Hawaiian islands with over 30 inches of rain, destroyed infrastructure, left thousands without power, and tragically claimed a life, the region is facing another severe test. Tropical Storm Moke is currently advancing across the Pacific, threatening to dump an additional 15 inches of rain on the already saturated Big Island and 3 to 6 inches on eastern Maui.</p>
    
    <p>While Moke's center is expected to pass south of the islands without making direct landfall, National Weather Service forecasters warn that the storm will bring life-threatening floods and mudslides, particularly to windward and southeast regions still reeling from Lala. Hawaii Governor Josh Green has requested a federal emergency declaration, and local authorities are urging residents to prepare for renewed power outages, disrupted communications, and hazardous travel conditions as recovery efforts remain stalled.</p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Weather Fatigue and Compounding Crises</h2>
    <p>When we watch weather reports, we naturally focus on the physical metrics: wind speeds, rainfall totals, power outage counts, and flood heights. Yet behind every statistic is a human nervous system attempting to process trauma and uncertainty. When a second major weather event hits before a community has even begun to clean up from the first, it triggers specific psychological phenomena that psychologists refer to as cumulative stress and normalcy bias disruption.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">1. The Erosion of Normalcy Bias and Psychic Numbing</h3>
    <p>Human beings rely heavily on a cognitive baseline often called normalcy bias—the comforting assumption that tomorrow will look roughly like yesterday. When a severe disaster strikes, that baseline shatters, but the human mind immediately begins working to rebuild it during the cleanup phase. However, when Tropical Storm Moke arrives just days after Hurricane Lala, it denies residents the recovery window necessary to reset their emotional baseline. This creates a state of chronic hypervigilance, where people cannot let down their guard because the threat has not truly passed.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">2. Decision Fatigue in the Face of Constant Threats</h3>
    <p>Preparing for a hurricane or tropical storm requires an exhausting series of micro-decisions: Should we evacuate? Do we have enough bottled water? Will the generator hold out? How long will the food in the fridge last? When individuals are forced to make these high-stakes decisions repeatedly within days of each other, acute decision fatigue sets in. People become mentally exhausted, which can lead to delayed responses or fatalistic resignation ("what happens, happens"), even when clear warnings are issued by local leaders like Mayor Kimo Alameda.</p>
