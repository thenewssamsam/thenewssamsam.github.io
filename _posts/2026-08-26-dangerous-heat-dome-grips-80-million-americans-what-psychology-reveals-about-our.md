<h2>What Happened</h2>
<p>More than 80 million people across the United States are currently enduring an extreme heat warning or heat advisory, with forecasters at the National Weather Service's Weather Prediction Center describing the latest system as a steamy air mass even by late-August standards. Daytime temperatures in the South-Central U.S. and the desert Southwest are climbing well into the triple digits, with some areas pushing past 110 degrees Fahrenheit.</p>

<p>Beyond the brutal daytime highs, the real danger in this current weather pattern lies in the lack of overnight relief. According to federal forecasters, minimum temperatures remain unusually high, denying bodies the vital recovery window needed during prolonged heat events. Scientific consensus, supported by groups like the World Weather Attribution network and insights from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory senior scientist Michael Wehner, indicates that human-caused climate change has added several degrees to the severity of these modern heat waves, making severe events more frequent and intense than they were decades ago.</p>

Why is there often such a wide gap between scientific warnings and human action during extreme weather?</p>

<p>One of the most powerful psychological mechanisms at play during events like this is normalcy bias. Human beings are creatures of habit. Because summer is traditionally a warm season, our brains tend to categorize a heat wave as just another hot week rather than an acute environmental hazard. We downplay risks because acknowledging them requires disrupting our comfortable routines. As climate experts point out, heat is uniquely dangerous precisely because it lacks the dramatic, visible destruction of a hurricane or a tornado. It is a silent hazard, making it much easier for our minds to underestimate.</p>

<p>Another psychological hurdle is understanding the heat index—the way humidity tricks our internal thermostat. When sweat cannot evaporate effectively because the air is saturated with moisture, our cooling system fails. Psychologically, we struggle to process threats that rely on invisible atmospheric metrics rather than simple, visible temperature gauges. When the thermometer says 95 degrees but the heat index feels like 112, our intuition rebels against the data, leading many to push their physical limits outdoors until heat exhaustion sets in.</p>
