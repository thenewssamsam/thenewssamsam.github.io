  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Return to the Palmetto State</h2>
    <p>
      Pete Buttigieg’s recent weekend trip to South Carolina marked his first major visit to the state since leaving the Biden administration. Speaking to party activists, university students, and church congregations, the former mayor of South Bend, Indiana, addressed crowds in areas critical to the Democratic nominating process. The Democratic National Committee recently voted to keep South Carolina as the nation's first primary destination, cementing the heavy political influence of its Black electorate.
    </p>
    <p>
      During his 2020 presidential campaign, Buttigieg struggled significantly with Black voters, winning just 3% support in the state's primary compared to Joe Biden's 61%, which ultimately led to his campaign's exit. This time, however, Buttigieg arrived with federal executive experience from his tenure as transportation secretary, highlighting projects like a $23 million federal grant connecting Orangeburg's HBCUs to downtown. Yet, even as he brushed off official 2028 declarations—insisting he is focused on the upcoming 2026 midterms—informal "PETE 2028" signs and spirited local reactions underscored that the race has already begun.
    </p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Political Redemption and Adaptation</h2>
    <p>
      From a behavioral science standpoint, returning to the exact location of a past failure requires overcoming powerful cognitive barriers. Human beings naturally gravitate toward comfort and avoidance, steering clear of environments associated with rejection or negative feedback. In psychology, this avoidance is tied to loss aversion and the fear of repeating painful experiences.
    </p>
    <p>
      Yet, Buttigieg’s return illustrates a contrasting psychological principle: cognitive adaptation and growth mindset. Rather than abandoning a demographic where he previously underperformed, his strategy relies on updating his social proof. In 2020, voters viewed him largely as an untested municipal leader from a predominantly white Midwestern city. In 2026, he arrives bearing tangible receipts—federal infrastructure investments and visible executive output. 
    </p>
