  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: Record-Setting Rains Across Indiana</h2>
    <p>Days of relentless storms and heavy downpours left at least seven people dead across Indiana, capping a devastating week of severe weather that paralyzed parts of the Midwest. In Indianapolis, floodwaters along the White River peaked before finally beginning to recede, leaving residents to survey waterlogged homes and ruined possessions.</p>
    <p>The numbers behind the event underscore its severity. The White River crested at more than 24 feet in communities like Anderson and Noblesville, surpassing flood records that had stood since 1913. Indianapolis Mayor Joe Hogsett noted that the flooding was the worst the city has seen in over thirty years. Across the state, historic rainfall washed out roads and bridges, forced more than 350 emergency evacuations, and left nearly 130,000 utility customers without power.</p>

  <section id="why-it-matters">
    <h2>Why This Disaster Hits So Hard</h2>
    <p>When waters rise past doorsteps, the immediate crisis is logistical: rescue boats, emergency shelters, and power outages. But disasters of this magnitude disrupt more than just infrastructure. They shatter the quiet assumption of safety that people place in their daily routines.</p>
    <p>For Frank Gounder, whose northside Indianapolis home and furniture workshop flooded just as he turned 37, the transition from shock to acceptance happened in real time. Standing knee-deep in ruined materials, his initial anger gave way to a stark realization: fighting the water changes nothing. That moment of cognitive shift highlights how human beings process sudden, overwhelming loss.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="psychology-perspective" class="ncg-lens-section">
    <h2>A Psychology Perspective: Loss Aversion and Normalcy Bias in Crises</h2>
    <p>When unexpected natural disasters strike, human behavior is heavily influenced by deeply ingrained psychological mechanisms. Understanding these patterns helps explain why people react the way they do when confronted with rising waters and severe weather.</p>
    
    <h3>The Trap of Normalcy Bias</h3>
    <p>Before floodwaters reach a doorstep, many people experience what psychologists call normalcy bias—the tendency to underestimate the likelihood of disaster and its potential effects. People assume that because a river hasn't breached its banks in living memory, or because they have weathered past storms safely, this event will follow the same script. When rivers surpass records set more than a century ago, that mental model breaks down instantly.</p>

    <h3>Coping with Sudden Disruption</h3>
    <p>Loss aversion and the shock of property destruction trigger intense emotional friction. When tangible reminders of a lifetime—handcrafted furniture, family vehicles, cherished homes—are submerged, the brain struggles to reconcile past stability with present chaos. As Gounder observed after the initial anger subsided, survival requires a psychological pivot: letting go of what cannot be recovered in order to focus energy on what can still be done.</p>

    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>"When you realize you can’t do anything about the water, you kind of just have to let go and continue, or else you’re just going to be miserable."</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section id="how-to-respond">
    <h2>How to Respond and Support Recovery</h2>
    <p>Disaster recovery extends far beyond pumping out basements and repairing washed-out bridges. Supporting a community through severe weather trauma requires patience, practical aid, and emotional awareness.</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Respect Safety Barricades:</strong> Many injuries and fatalities during floods occur when individuals attempt to navigate compromised roads or bypass barricades. Patience saves lives.</li>
      <li><strong>Check on Neighbors:</strong> Senior citizens and mobile home residents are frequently among the most vulnerable during prolonged power outages and rapid evacuations.</li>
      <li><strong>Prioritize Mental Health:</strong> Acknowledge that the shock of losing property or routine takes an emotional toll. Give yourself and others grace during the rebuilding phase.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>
