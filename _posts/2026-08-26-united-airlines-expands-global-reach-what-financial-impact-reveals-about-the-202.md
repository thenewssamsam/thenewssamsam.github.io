<p>Why now? According to Kirby, the primary catalyst isn't just surging consumer wanderlust—it is the fact that aircraft manufacturers have finally caught up on production backlogs. For years following the pandemic, airlines sat on cash reserves but faced crippling delivery delays from major builders like Boeing and Airbus. As those assembly lines normalize, carriers are eager to deploy new inventory into high-margin international markets where premium seating demand remains robust.</p>

<p>Furthermore, the heavy reliance on Newark highlights the immense value of airport slots. In commercial aviation, a "slot" is an authorized window to land or take off at capacity-constrained airports. Without FAA slot management, delays cascade across the entire national airspace system, burning expensive jet fuel and destroying operating margins. By working with regulators to streamline Newark's scheduling, United has unlocked hidden operational capacity, turning a historically bottlenecked hub into a profitable launchpad for transatlantic growth.</p>

<p>Recent economic data highlights that airfares remain elevated, with some metrics showing prices up significantly compared to pre-pandemic baselines. However, airline executives offer a different lens on the math. Kirby noted during his interview that while tickets feel expensive, average inflation-adjusted fares are actually hovering below historical inflation peaks when viewed across long economic cycles.</p>
<p>The financial reality is that the travel industry experienced an artificial pricing trough during the COVID-19 collapse, when demand fell off a cliff and airlines slashed prices to generate any scrap of cash flow. What travelers are experiencing today isn't necessarily gouging; it is the bumpy normalization of travel costs against an inflationary backdrop featuring higher labor costs, more expensive jet fuel, and massive capital investments in fleet renewal.</p>
