But when statements blur the line between verified medical consensus and political talking points, the resulting confusion can carry real public health consequences. A recent appearance on CBS's <em>Face The Nation</em> by CMS administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz and Florida gubernatorial candidate Rep. Byron Donalds brought questions about vaccine safety, infant immunization, and measles outbreaks back into the national spotlight.</p>

<p>As debates over public health intensify across the country, sorting accurate data from well-intentioned confusion or political rhetoric has never been more vital. Let us examine what the scientific evidence actually demonstrates regarding these high-profile claims.</p>

<p>Another focal point was the infant Hepatitis B vaccine. Dr. Oz suggested that the vaccine primarily helps adults and argued that infant exposure is extremely rare. However, medical history tells a different story. Before universal birth dose recommendations were established in 1991, more than 20,000 infants contracted Hepatitis B annually in the United States. Because roughly 90% of infants infected at birth develop chronic, lifelong infections that can lead to liver failure or cancer, universal vaccination resulted in a staggering 99% decrease in infections among children and adolescents.</p>

<p>Finally, regarding Rep. Donalds' claim that "rampant illegal immigration" drove recent measles outbreaks, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) data paints a very different picture. Out of nearly 2,800 recent measles cases, only a tiny fraction (16 cases, or less than 0.006%) were linked to international visitors. Over 94% of cases occurred in individuals who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown. The resurgence is driven not by immigration, but by localized drops in vaccination rates falling below the 95% threshold required to maintain community-wide herd immunity.</p>
