  <section id="introduction">
    <p>As Lake Powell and Lake Mead hit historic record lows, millions of residents across Arizona, Nevada, and California are asking a fundamental question: What does this crisis mean for my legal access to water and the cost of my utilities? While the situation is critical, understanding the legal framework governing water rights can help clear up common misconceptions about this unfolding environmental challenge.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>The Current State of the Colorado River</h2>
    <p>The Colorado River basin is currently facing its most significant strain in three decades. Driven by persistent drought and overuse, Lake Powell and Lake Mead—the nation's two largest reservoirs—have reached unprecedented low levels. Lake Powell has dropped over 20 feet since the start of the year, nearing the critical threshold where hydroelectric turbines may cease production. Lake Mead, downstream, has similarly hit a record low.</p>
  </section>

    <h3>Can Utilities Raise Rates?</h3>
    <p>When water scarcity hits, infrastructure costs rise. Utilities often seek regulatory approval to pass these costs to consumers. While you have a right to water service, you do not have a constitutional right to a specific, static price for that water. Rate increases are governed by state public utility commissions, which must review and approve any hikes to ensure they are "just and reasonable."</p>
