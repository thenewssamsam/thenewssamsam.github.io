<h1>Russia Sends Warships Near Japan: What a Historical Parallel Reveals About the Kuril Islands Standoff</h1>
</header>

<p class="hpc-lead">When four Russian missile corvettes cruised just 40 kilometers off the coast of Hokkaido, it wasn't just a routine naval exercise. Coming hot on the heels of Vladimir Putin's visit to the contested Kuril Islands and a sharp warning from Moscow about "monstrous consequences," this maritime maneuver highlights a territorial dispute that has remained frozen since the closing days of World War II—and why history suggests these waters will only grow more turbulent.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened Off Hokkaido</h2>
<p>According to Japan's Joint Staff, four Project 12411M Molniya-1 missile corvettes belonging to Russia's Pacific Fleet were detected off Cape Soya on Hokkaido's northern tip. The vessels—identified by hull numbers 916, 946, 971, and 978—did not enter Japanese territorial waters, but their proximity was unmistakable. A P-3C maritime patrol aircraft from the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force closely monitored their movements as they transited the Soya Strait.</p>
<p>This deployment was no coincidence. It immediately followed a visit by Russian leader Vladimir Putin to Iturup, one of the islands in the chain that Japan calls its Northern Territories and Russia calls the Kurils. Tokyo promptly lodged a formal diplomatic protest, triggering aggressive counter-retoric from Dmitry Medvedev, Deputy Chairman of Russia's Security Council, who warned of severe consequences if Japan pressed its claims.</p>

<h2 id="historical-parallel">A Historical Parallel: Unfinished Peace Treaties</h2>
<p>To understand why four Soviet-designed missile corvettes sailing near Hokkaido matter today, we have to look back to the summer of 1945. In the closing weeks of World War II, Soviet forces seized the entire Kuril Island chain—including Etorofu (Iturup), Kunashiri, Shikotan, and the Habomai islets—under the agreements forged at the Yalta Conference.</p>
<p>Eighty decades later, that territorial seizure casts a long shadow over modern geopolitics. Because Tokyo and Moscow have never resolved who rightfully owns these islands, Japan and Russia have never formally signed a World War II peace treaty. They remain, technically and legally, in a state of suspended diplomacy.</p>
<p>History shows that unresolved territorial disputes rarely age well. Much like the disputed borderlands of Eastern Europe or the maritime friction points in the South China Sea, frozen conflicts act as geopolitical pressure cookers. When domestic political pressures rise in Moscow or Tokyo, leaders often lean into nationalist rhetoric over these contested scraps of land. Putin's visit to Iturup and the subsequent naval show of force fit a classic historical pattern: using outer-ring territories to project strength inward, signaling to domestic audiences that national sovereignty is non-negotiable.</p>

<h2 id="strategic-stakes">The Strategic Value of the Kuril Islands</h2>
<p>Why does Moscow cling so tightly to a windswept chain of volcanic islands? The answer lies in geography and naval strategy. The Kuril Islands act as a natural maritime gateway connecting the Sea of Okhotsk to the open Pacific Ocean.</p>
<p>By controlling the straits between the islands, Russia secures a protected bastion for its strategic nuclear submarines operating in the Sea of Okhotsk. Allowing these islands to slip back into Japanese hands would essentially punch a hole in Russia's eastern maritime defense perimeter. That is why Iturup hosts military infrastructure like the Burevestnik airfield, and why the deployment of Tarantul III-class corvettes—armed with supersonic Moskit anti-ship missiles—serves as a blunt reminder of Moscow's military readiness in the Pacific.</p>
