    <h3>The Reactance Effect</h3>
    <p>Psychological <em>reactance</em> occurs when voters feel that their freedom of choice is being threatened by external forces—in this case, the establishment backing of the DSCC. When a party "recruits" a candidate, it can trigger a defensive response in voters who prioritize local agency. Nixon’s rhetoric, which explicitly framed her campaign as a battle against "establishment politics of DC," successfully tapped into this desire for autonomy.</p>

    <h3>The Authenticity Bias</h3>
    <p>Voters often exhibit an <em>authenticity bias</em>, where they perceive candidates who are perceived as "outsiders" or "grassroots" as more trustworthy than those who appear to be products of a political machine. By focusing on issues like "Medicare for all" and "universal child care," Nixon aligned herself with a specific identity-driven movement, creating a sense of psychological proximity that the more traditional, national-profile campaign of Vindman struggled to replicate.</p>
