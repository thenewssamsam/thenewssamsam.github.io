    <section id="introduction">
        <p>In a move that signals a potential paradigm shift for East Asian security, South Korean President Lee Jae Myung has accelerated plans to regain wartime operational control (Opcon) of his nation's military. This push follows Donald Trump’s recent directive to "substantially reduce" joint military exercises with South Korea, a decision that has prompted Seoul to reconsider its long-term reliance on the US-South Korea security umbrella.</p>
    </section>

    <section id="the-financial-angle">
        <h2>The Economic Cost of Sovereignty</h2>
        <p>When a nation moves toward "military independence," the immediate financial consequence is a significant shift in defense spending. For decades, the US has provided a massive security subsidy to the Korean peninsula, maintaining Camp Humphreys—the largest US military base abroad—and absorbing significant costs associated with annual large-scale drills.</p>
        
        <p>As Seoul seeks to take command of its own forces, the budgetary burden will likely shift squarely onto the South Korean taxpayer. President Lee’s call for nuclear-powered submarines and advanced integrated operations requires an aggressive capital expenditure program. For local markets, this suggests a pivot toward domestic defense manufacturing, potentially bolstering firms like Hanwha Aerospace and LIG Nex1, but it also necessitates a reallocation of government funds that might otherwise support social infrastructure or technology subsidies.</p>
