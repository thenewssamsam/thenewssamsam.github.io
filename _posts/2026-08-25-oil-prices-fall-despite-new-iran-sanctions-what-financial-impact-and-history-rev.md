<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened</h2>
    <p>Global energy markets delivered a surprising counterintuitive reaction on Monday as West Texas Intermediate (WTI) and Brent crude both declined by roughly 2.5%, despite the United States launching what officials are calling the most severe economic offensive in modern history against Iran. WTI futures settled at $84.89 per barrel, while international benchmark Brent crude slipped to $92.06.</p>
    <p>The sweeping sanctions package, dubbed <strong>"Operation Economic Outcast"</strong> by the Trump administration, aims to systematically dismantle Iran's economic ties on a global scale. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent characterized the offensive as an "economic D-Day" and the "single greatest financial offensive ever marshaled against an adversary." The policy explicitly signals that major trading partners, including China, will not receive exemptions from the compliance pressure.</p>
    <p>Yet, rather than sparking an immediate supply panic and driving crude prices higher, oil markets dropped. Financial analysts attribute the slide primarily to profit-taking following a sharp jump late last week when President Donald Trump first threatened the crushing economic operations.</p>

<section id="historical-parallel">
    <h2>A Historical Look at Oil Sanctions and Volatility</h2>
    <p>History shows us that sweeping economic sanctions rarely yield immediate, clean outcomes in energy markets. Past embargoes and maximum-pressure campaigns against major oil producers—such as historical restrictions on Iran, Iraq, or Venezuela—frequently follow a distinct pattern:</p>
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Initial Panic Spike:</strong> Markets react violently to the announcement of new restrictions, pricing in worst-case supply shortages.</li>
        <li><strong>The Adaptation Phase:</strong> Trading routes reroute, intermediaries emerge, and enforcement loopholes are tested.</li>
        <li><strong>Fundamental Realignment:</strong> Prices eventually correct based on real-world inventory levels and actual compliance rates among major importers like China and India.</li>
    </ul>
