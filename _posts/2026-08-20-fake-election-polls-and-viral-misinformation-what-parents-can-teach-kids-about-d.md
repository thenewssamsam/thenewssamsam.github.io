  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p>When a mysterious organization named Median Strategies published fabricated political poll numbers online—tricking even high-profile campaigns and moving prediction markets before vanishing—it exposed a fragile ecosystem of digital trust. For parents, this bizarre political stunt offers an urgent, teachable moment about guiding children and teenagers through a world where seeing is no longer believing.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Anatomy of a Fabricated Poll</h2>
    <p>The story broke when Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass shared social media data from a newly minted entity called Median Strategies, which claimed her campaign was surging ahead in the mayoral race. Within days, the numbers were exposed as entirely bogus. Median Strategies quickly shuttered its website, leaving behind a vague note claiming the stunt was a "social experiment" on how unvetted information spreads.</p>
    
    <p>While the culprits hid behind anonymous online handles, the real-world ripple effects were immediate. Prediction markets shifted, news outlets scrambled, and even experienced political staffers fell for the illusion of legitimacy. If sophisticated political operators can be fooled by sleekly packaged disinformation, it highlights just how vulnerable ordinary internet users—especially developing minds—are to fabricated data.</p>

  <section id="parenting-perspective">
    <h2>A Parenting Perspective: Navigating the Age of Manufactured Truth</h2>
    <p>Raising children in the digital age means accepting that our kids will encounter manipulated data long before they have the fully developed critical thinking skills to instinctively question it. The prefrontal cortex—the area of the brain responsible for impulse control, long-term consequence evaluation, and deep skepticism—continues developing well into a person's twenties. This biological reality makes children and teenagers uniquely susceptible to viral emotional hooks and seemingly official statistics.</p>

    <h3>1. Age-Banded Conversations: From Curiosity to Critical Thinking</h3>
    <p>How we discuss online falsehoods must evolve alongside our children. For younger children (ages 6–10), the conversation should center on basic common sense and the idea that "not everything on a screen is true, just like not everything in a fairy tale actually happened." For tweens and teens (ages 11–18), parents can dive into the mechanics of digital deception—such as how anonymous websites build fake authority, how algorithms reward sensationalism, and why viral momentum does not equal factual accuracy.</p>

    <h3>2. Moving Past Fear-Mongering to Practical Empowerment</h3>
    <p>The goal of media literacy is not to make children paranoid, cynical hermits who trust no one. Instead, the aim is to instill a healthy, curious pause. When our kids see a shocking headline, a dramatic statistic, or a viral social media post, we want their immediate internal reaction to be: <em>"That's interesting. Let's see who published this and how they know."</em></p>
