  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened at Carlisle Regional Airport</h2>
    <p>On Wednesday evening, just before 7 p.m., a catastrophic midair collision occurred at the Carlisle Regional Airport in Pennsylvania's Cumberland Valley. Dr. Paul Sokoloski, a 57-year-old physician and certified flight instructor from Hershey, was flying his Cessna 150 solo when it collided with a Bell 407 Pennsylvania State Police helicopter engaged in a routine training exercise.</p>
    <p>According to National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) investigator Aaron McCarter and Acting State Police Commissioner Col. George Bivens, the helicopter had completed a "hover taxi" close to the ground and was moving toward the side of the runway. Witnesses and CCTV footage indicate that the Cessna approached straight and level before experiencing a sudden, sharp pitch upward, leveling off, and colliding with the rear of the helicopter. The impact stripped the helicopter's rotor blades, causing it to crash and roll onto its side. Two troopers inside—Cpl. Bryce Corman and Trooper Jason Mills—were trapped briefly before managing to escape with minor injuries. Sadly, Dr. Sokoloski did not survive the impact.</p>

Yet aviation psychologists note that accidents rarely stem from one isolated mistake. Instead, they are typically the culmination of complex cognitive vulnerabilities—blind spots that affect even highly experienced pilots, doctors, and professionals.</p>

    <h3>1. The Trap of Normalcy Bias and Uncontrolled Airfield Dynamics</h3>
    <p>Carlisle Regional Airport is an uncontrolled airfield, meaning it lacks a traditional air traffic tower with controllers managing takeoffs and landings. Instead, pilots rely on shared radio frequencies to announce their positions and intentions. While this decentralized system works smoothly millions of times a year, it places an immense cognitive load on the individual pilot to build a mental map of their surroundings entirely through auditory cues.</p>

    <p>Psychologically, the human brain relies heavily on patterns and expectations. If a pilot has landed or trained at an airfield dozens of times without unexpected hazards, the brain begins to anticipate routine. This is known as normalcy bias—the tendency to underestimate the likelihood of a hazard because "nothing bad has happened here before." Even when communication occurs over a shared frequency, visual confirmation in fading evening light can be easily compromised by background distractions or misconstrued spatial cues.</p>

    <p>Investigators noted that Dr. Sokoloski had flown south for several hours earlier that day to conduct training, and had also spent time working on aircraft maintenance the previous day. High cumulative fatigue places stress on the prefrontal cortex, which governs executive function, working memory, and risk assessment.</p>
