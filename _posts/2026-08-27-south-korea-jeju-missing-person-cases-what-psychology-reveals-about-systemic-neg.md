  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened on Jeju Island?</h2>
    <p>In late August 2026, the idyllic resort destination of Jeju Island, South Korea, became the center of a national crisis. Over the span of just three days through Monday, authorities recovered the bodies of four people who had previously been reported missing. While the cases themselves were unrelated, the circumstances surrounding their handling triggered immediate public outrage.</p>

    <p>According to reports from Reuters and Yonhap, at least two of these missing-person files had allegedly been closed prematurely by a single police officer without conducting basic verifications to ensure the individuals were safe. One tragic focal point was the case of 37-year-old Jang Mi-ran. Her disappearance morphed into a nationwide controversy after police allegedly shut down the investigation without ever confirming her safety. Her body was ultimately discovered on a Monday—a staggering 104 days after she vanished.</p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Institutional Neglect</h2>
    <p>Why do systems fail so catastrophically when individuals are counting on them the most? From a behavioral and psychological standpoint, the Jeju Island tragedy exposes a dangerous cocktail of cognitive biases, organizational groupthink, and bureaucratic desensitization. Understanding these mechanisms helps explain how an officer could allegedly close a file on a missing human being without confirming their safety.</p>

    <h3>1. Normalcy Bias and Administrative Routine</h3>
    <p>South Korean police received more than 70,000 reports of missing adults annually since 2022. The vast majority of these cases are successfully resolved within 30 days. When an agency handles tens of thousands of similar reports year after year, human psychology adapts through what is known as normalisation of deviance. Routine transforms high-stakes emergencies into paperwork quotas. The sheer volume creates cognitive fatigue, leading officials to unconsciously assume that the next case will resolve itself just like the thousands before it.</p>

    <h3>2. Diffusion of Responsibility in Expanding Bureaucracies</h3>
    <p>As organizations grow in power and scope—such as the South Korean police inheriting broader investigative authority from prosecutors—individual accountability often dilutes rather than strengthens. When workers operate inside a massive hierarchy without rigorous supervisory oversight, the psychological burden of personal responsibility fades. The officer assumes the system will catch errors; the supervisors assume the frontline officers are doing their jobs. In that structural gap, vulnerable citizens fall through.</p>

    <h3>3. Confirmation Bias and Closure Seeking</h3>
    <p>Human brains possess an innate aversion to open loops and unresolved stress. In high-pressure administrative environments, workers often experience a subconscious drive to "clear the queue." Confirmation bias sets in, where ambiguous signals or superficial paperwork entries are conveniently interpreted as proof that a task is complete. Closing a file brings immediate cognitive relief to the overloaded worker, masking the terrifying reality that a real person remains unprotected in the dark.</p>
