<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in the Strait of Hormuz?</h2>
<p>The diplomatic chess match in the Middle East took a new turn this week when Iranian and Omani officials discussed a proposed framework to establish a joint temporary navigational corridor through the Strait of Hormuz. According to joint statements and reports from regional news outlets, the plan aims to clear naval mines and secure safe passage for commercial vessels.</p>

<div style="margin: 20px 0; padding: 15px; border-left: 4px solid #d97706; background: #fffbeb; border-radius: 4px;">
<strong style="color: #b45309;">Quick Take:</strong> 
<p style="margin: 5px 0 0 0; color: #78350f;">While Iran and Oman reached an initial consensus on sharing administrative revenues, Tehran insists the waterway remains shut until Washington accepts its conditions. Meanwhile, President Donald Trump maintains that the strait remains functional, noting that commercial vessels continue to make trips despite sporadic security disruptions.</p>
</div>

<p>At the same time, the broader economic war continues to simmer. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent recently outlined plans for secondary sanctions against Iran's trading partners—notably China, which purchases roughly 90% of Iranian oil exports. Yet, the White House has temporarily held off on enforcing these aggressive penalties to prevent a shock to the global financial system. Amid these conflicting signals, oil markets have reacted with surprising calm, with international Brent crude pulling back below the $90 per barrel threshold as traders weigh diplomatic openings against ongoing naval bottlenecks.</p>

<h2>A Historical Parallel: The Battle for Global Chokepoints</h2>
<p>To truly understand the friction over the Strait of Hormuz, we do not need to look very far into the history books. Maritime choke points have always been the Achilles' heel of empires. Consider the Strait of Malacca during the colonial era, or the Suez Crisis of 1956, when control over a narrow strip of water brought global commerce to a grinding halt and dramatically redrew the geopolitical map.</p>

<p>When a single maritime artery handles roughly a fifth of global crude oil consumption, as the Strait of Hormuz does, it transforms into an economic pressure valve. Historically, nations that control or border these narrow passages quickly realize their disproportionate leverage. <h2>Market Realities and the Economic Strain</h2>
<p>The economic dimensions of the US-Iran conflict are complex and far-reaching. Daily transits through the Strait of Hormuz have dropped significantly from their pre-conflict averages, with preliminary data indicating only a handful of commodity vessels making the passage on recent days. Despite these sharp declines in physical volume, international oil markets have shown remarkable resilience, extending recent losses as investors bet on eventual diplomatic resolutions and the cautious return of U.S. diplomats to Gulf states.</p>
