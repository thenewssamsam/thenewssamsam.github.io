    <h2>What Happened in Nepal and Tibet?</h2>
    <p>On a quiet Wednesday morning, communities along the borderlands of Nepal and the Tibetan Autonomous Region were struck without warning. What initially registered on seismic instruments as a potential earthquake was quickly re-analyzed by the U.S. Geological Survey and the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development as a massive glacial collapse. An avalanche of ice and debris cascaded down, sending an unprecedented torrent of mud and water crashing into river valleys.</p>
    
    <p>The numbers emerging from the disaster zone are staggering. Nepalese authorities reported that the death toll rose to 165, with over 826 people missing in districts like Nuwakot and Rasuwa. Across the border in Tibet, state media confirmed at least three deaths and 558 missing individuals, a significant portion of whom are foreign tourists and pilgrims traveling to sacred sites like Mount Kailash. Rivers like the Trishuli swelled dramatically, with water levels rising up to 9 meters in half an hour, washing away roads, burying buildings in meters of mud, and stranding travelers.</p>

    <h3>Lessons from Past Megafloods</h3>
    <p>When examining historical records of the Hindu Kush Himalaya region, glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs) have periodically reshaped geography for centuries. In the mid-20th century, similar unmonitored glacial retreats caused catastrophic downstream surges in parts of Nepal and Bhutan. However, what makes the current era uniquely perilous is the rapid infrastructure expansion—hydropower projects, roads, and tourism corridors—woven tightly into these steep, precarious valleys. The Trishuli hydropower project facilities, heavily impacted in this recent event, highlight the vulnerability of modern engineering built in paths carved by ancient ice.</p>
