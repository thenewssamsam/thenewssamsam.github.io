<p>When high-stakes diplomacy collides with years of deep-seated trauma, finding a path forward feels almost miraculous. U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner has delivered a stark ultimatum to Hamas, warning that the group must disarm under a 20-point peace roadmap or face a fully backed Israeli campaign to finish the operation. As negotiators race against a strict 30-to-90-day timeline in Cairo and Jerusalem, the world watches to see whether words of compromise will translate into real action.</p>

<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The 30-to-90-Day Ultimatum</h2>
<p>Speaking in an exclusive interview with Fox News chief foreign correspondent Trey Yingst following intense diplomatic meetings in Egypt and Israel, Jared Kushner outlined a rigid framework designed to test Hamas' sincerity regarding President Donald Trump's Gaza peace plan. Kushner met in Cairo with Hamas representatives alongside the National Committee, the newly established technocratic Palestinian governing body.</p>
<p>Under the U.S.-led "Board of Peace" proposal, Hamas is expected to surrender its weapons and dismantle its network of tunnels within a clear operational window. Kushner indicated that progress could become visible within 30 days, with disarmament and tunnel neutralization slated for the 60-to-90-day mark. However, friction remains high: while Israel demands complete demilitarization before permitting any reconstruction or further troop withdrawals, Hamas has insisted that withdrawal and disarmament must occur simultaneously.</p>

<h2>A Historical Parallel Perspective: The Complex Path of Disarmament</h2>
<p>Demanding that a militant or insurgent group voluntarily surrender its weapons in the middle of an active conflict is one of the most difficult feats in modern statecraft. Throughout history, negotiated demilitarization agreements have rarely succeeded without immense leverage, absolute clarity of enforcement, and a credible alternative future for the rank-and-file combatants.</p>

<p>Consider the complexities of historical peace processes, such as the Good Friday Agreement in Northern Ireland or various post-conflict disarmament programs in the Balkans and Southeast Asia. In each of these cases, the transition from active hostilities to verified disarmament stalled repeatedly over sequencing—who moves first, how verification is managed, and how security guarantees are maintained when trust has been entirely shattered.</p>

History suggests that ultimatums backed by credible, overwhelming force can sometimes compel compliance, but they also risk immediate escalation if the enforcement mechanism lacks absolute airtight verification.</p>
