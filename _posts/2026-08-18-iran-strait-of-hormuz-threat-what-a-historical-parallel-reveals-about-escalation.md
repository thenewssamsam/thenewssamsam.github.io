  <section id="historical-perspective" class="ncg-section">
    <h2 class="ncg-h2">A Historical Parallel: The Fragility of Chokepoints</h2>
    <p>History suggests that whenever a major power attempts to secure an exclusive monopoly over a narrow maritime chokepoint, local resistance and global economic strain inevitably follow. From a historical perspective, the current standoff in the Strait of Hormuz bears striking similarities to past geopolitical bottlenecks where control of trade routes became non-negotiable points of national survival.</p>
    <p>Consider the 1956 Suez Crisis. When Egypt nationalized the Suez Canal—another vital global trade artery—Britain, France, and Israel launched a military intervention to wrest back control. While the military operation achieved its immediate tactical objectives, it triggered a severe global energy shock, exposed the limits of declining imperial power, and fundamentally reshaped Middle Eastern geopolitics under heavy diplomatic pressure from Washington.</p>
    <p>A second historical parallel lies in the Tanker War phase of the 1980–1988 Iran-Iraq War. During that period, both belligerents targeted commercial shipping in the Persian Gulf to choke off each other's oil exports, prompting the United States to launch Operation Earnest Will to protect reflagged Kuwaiti oil tankers. That escalation demonstrated a timeless truth: chokepoint conflicts rarely remain isolated local skirmishes. Instead, they metastasize into tests of global logistics, domestic political endurance, and alliance durability.</p>
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>&ldquo;Control over a narrow maritime passage is never just about military positioning; it is a permanent stress test on the nerves of global trade and political leadership.&rdquo;</p>
    </div>
    <p>However, important differences exist today. Unlike past conflicts fought with conventional state-to-state naval engagements, today's theater involves decentralized command structures, powerful revolutionary guard corps networks, and deeply interconnected global supply chains that feel the pinch of every barrel lost in the Persian Gulf.</p>
  </section>
