    The United States and Canada have stepped back from the brink of a damaging trade war, with President Donald Trump announcing a tentative agreement that postpones threatened 50% tariffs on approximately $20 billion worth of Canadian imports. While political leaders in Washington and Ottawa herald the framework as a crucial breakthrough, the fine print remains unsettled—leaving financial markets, agricultural producers, and cross-border supply chains scrambling to calculate the true economic fallout.
  </p>

  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 40px;">
    <h2 style="font-size: 1.6rem; color: #0f5132; margin-bottom: 15px; border-left: 4px solid #0f5132; padding-left: 10px;">What Happened: The Tariff Pause and Emerging Framework</h2>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      The breakthrough came after intense diplomatic maneuvering in Washington, where Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc and United States Trade Representative Jamieson Greer met to hammer out terms. The retaliatory pressure had reached a boiling point after President Trump invoked Depression-era legal authorities to levy a massive 50% tariff on roughly 5% of Canadian exports, ranging from everyday consumer goods like hockey sticks to vital industrial inputs.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      In response to the imminent deadline, the Trump administration temporarily delayed the tariffs until Saturday morning. During this window, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney briefed provincial premiers—including Nova Scotia Premier Tim Houston and Saskatchewan Premier Scott Moe—urging them to ease retaliatory measures, such as returning U.S. alcohol products to provincial liquor store shelves. While political optics point toward a truce, senior officials on both sides acknowledge that pivotal terms regarding agricultural market access and supply management are still being negotiated.
    </p>
  </section>

    <h2 style="font-size: 1.6rem; color: #0f5132; margin-bottom: 15px; border-left: 4px solid #0f5132; padding-left: 10px;">The Financial Impact: Who Pays and Who Profits?</h2>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      When governments threaten sweeping tariffs, the immediate public conversation centers on political posturing and national pride. However, the underlying financial mechanics are fundamentally about cost distribution. A 50% tax on $20 billion in imports does not extract revenue from foreign governments; rather, it functions as a direct cost increase absorbed by domestic importers, wholesale distributors, and ultimately retail consumers.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">
      President Trump maintained that the emerging deal would provide a major win for U.S. farmers by dismantling Canadian agricultural barriers, claiming that tariffs on American agricultural goods would drop to zero. On the flip side, Canadian officials insist that their fiercely protected dairy supply management system remains intact. This fundamental tension exposes a classic economic puzzle: how to open export doors for American producers without dismantling the regulated quotas that shield Canadian farmers from price volatility.
    </p>

    <p style="margin-bottom: 15px;">

Ontario’s government-run liquor monopoly, the LCBO, previously sold nearly $1 billion Canadian dollars annually in American products before pulling them in retaliation. While restoring these sales provides an immediate revenue recovery for U.S. distillers, consumer habits have shifted, and supply chain recovery rarely happens overnight.
    </p>
