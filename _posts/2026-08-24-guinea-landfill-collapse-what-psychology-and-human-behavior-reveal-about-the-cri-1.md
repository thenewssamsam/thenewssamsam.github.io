<p>When a massive, mountainous open-air landfill collapsed in Conakry, Guinea, burying nearby homes and killing at least 30 people, the immediate tragedy was compounded by a haunting question familiar to urban disasters worldwide: Why were people still living right next to a ticking time bomb?</p>

<p>The disaster at the Dar Es Salam dumpsite on Sunday followed heavy overnight rains that saturated towering piles of refuse, causing a catastrophic structural failure that crushed a three-story building and trapped numerous victims. While authorities had previously issued eviction notices and scheduled the site for closure due to severe safety risks, many residents remained. Understanding this tragedy requires looking past the immediate physical collapse and examining the complex psychological forces that drive people to remain in high-risk environments.</p>

<h2 id="psychology-perspective">The Psychology of Normalcy Bias and Urban Risk</h2>
<p>To understand why families remained in the shadow of a condemned landfill despite eviction notices, behavioral scientists often point to a powerful cognitive defense mechanism known as <em>normalcy bias</em>. This psychological phenomenon causes people to underestimate the possibility of disaster, particularly when a dangerous condition has existed for a long time without catastrophic failure.</p>
