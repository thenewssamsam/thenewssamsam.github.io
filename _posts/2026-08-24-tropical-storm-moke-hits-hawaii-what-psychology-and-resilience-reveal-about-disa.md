    <p>When we watch weather reports tracking systems like Tropical Storm Moke and Hurricane Lala, our attention naturally fixes on physical metrics: wind speeds, rainfall totals, power outage numbers, and inches of mud. But there is an invisible metric that rarely makes the banner headlines yet dictates how well a community survives: emotional bandwidth.</p>

    <p>In psychology, the mental toll of experiencing continuous, compounding crises is often understood through the framework of <strong>disaster fatigue</strong> and cognitive overload. Human beings are remarkably well-equipped for acute, short-term emergencies. Adrenaline spikes, community bonds tighten, and people rise to meet immediate crises with astonishing bravery.</p>

This leads to what researchers call "normalcy bias" colliding with sheer exhaustion: people may downplay new warnings simply because they are too tired to prepare all over again, or they may experience acute anxiety and decision paralysis.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">The Weight of Uncertainty</h3>
    <p>Another psychological phenomenon at play during compounding tropical storms is perceived lack of control. When infrastructure is compromised, power is lost, and roads are washed out, daily predictable routines vanish. For families sheltering in place or wondering if their home will flood a second time, the brain's threat-detection center remains stuck in overdrive.</p>
