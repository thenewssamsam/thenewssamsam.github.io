<p>When a mysterious polling outfit named Median Strategies published numbers showing Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass holding a commanding 12-point lead over challenger Nithya Raman, local voters, news outlets, and political observers took notice. But that trust was shattered when the firm abruptly closed its doors and confessed that the entire survey was a fabricated social experiment designed to test how quickly misinformation spreads.</p>

<section id="what-happened">
<h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; margin-top: 2rem; margin-bottom: 1rem; color: #1a202c;">What Happened: The Rise and Fall of Median Strategies</h2>
<p>In local elections, numbers matter. They dictate campaign donations, dictate media narratives, and help everyday voters decide where to place their attention. That delicate ecosystem was recently upended when Median Strategies released surveys claiming massive leads for Democratic Mayor Karen Bass in Los Angeles and state Representative Francesca Hong in a Wisconsin gubernatorial primary.</p>

<p>The numbers quickly escaped into the wild. Local TV newscasts, social media feeds, and online reporting outlets picked up the data without independent verification. In Los Angeles, the fake poll claimed Bass held a 12-point lead over City Council member Nithya Raman in the primary. In reality, Bass edged Raman by a much tighter five-point margin. Similar manufactured or wildly inaccurate polls surfaced around the same time in Wisconsin and Michigan, creating widespread confusion among voters trying to make sense of crowded primary races.</p>

<ul style="line-height: 1.8; margin-left: 1.5rem; color: #2d3748;">
<li><strong>Check the source pedigree:</strong> Before sharing or reacting to a poll, look for transparent methodology, a known polling history, and independent verification from established local outlets.</li>
<li><strong>Support local reporting:</strong> Invest in local newspapers and independent journalists who do the hard work of verifying claims rather than reposting viral soundbites.</li>
<li><strong>Pause before sharing:</strong> Outrage and surprise are the primary vectors for viral misinformation. Take a moment to ask whether a surprising data point has been confirmed by multiple reliable sources.</li>
<li><strong>Focus on policy over projections:</strong> Polls come and go, but candidate platforms and voting records remain constant. Prioritize what candidates actually propose over what viral surveys claim about their odds.</li>
