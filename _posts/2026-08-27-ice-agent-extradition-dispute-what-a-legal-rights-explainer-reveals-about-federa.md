  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Texas-Minnesota Extradition Standoff</h2>
  <p>The core dispute centers on Christian Castro, a US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agent who stands accused in Minnesota of second-degree assault and falsely reporting a crime. The charges stem from a January incident in Minneapolis where a gunshot fired through a front door struck 24-year-old Julio Cesar Sosa-Celis in the leg. Prosecutors allege that Castro then falsely claimed he and another officer were attacked with a broom handle and a snow shovel.</p>
  
  <p>Following his arrest, Castro was held in Brownsville, Texas, near the US-Mexico border. Under Texas law, detainees awaiting extradition must be released after 90 days, a deadline falling on a Thursday. Minnesota authorities, led by Attorney General Keith Ellison and Hennepin County prosecutor Mary Moriarty, have been pressing for his extradition to face trial. However, Texas Governor Greg Abbott has refused to act on the request, citing ongoing investigations into government social service fraud programs in Minnesota.</p>

  <p>In response, Ellison filed a lawsuit against Abbott to prevent Castro's release. But during a federal hearing in Brownsville, US District Judge Fernando Rodriguez Jr. declined to order Texas to extradite the agent, noting that the governor's office claimed the lawsuit was premature because no official denial had been finalized. Consequently, Castro remained poised to walk out of a Texas jail, leaving prosecutors scrambling as federal authorities weigh separate charges.</p>

  <h2 id="legal-rights-explainer">A Legal Rights Explainer: How Interstate Extradition Actually Works</h2>
  <p>To understand why this case has sparked national attention, it helps to look at the mechanics of interstate extradition. Normally, the Extradition Clause of the US Constitution and federal statutes outline a routine administrative procedure whereby a governor of an asylum state surrenders a fugitive to the demanding state.</p>

  <p>However, legal precedent—most notably the landmark Supreme Court case <em>Puerto Rico v. Yet, as Judge Rodriguez pointed out during the recent hearing, questions of "ripeness" and procedural timing often complicate these disputes. When a governor delays acting under the guise of an internal review or political friction, the legal machinery slows down dramatically.</p>
