  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Indonesian Borneo</h2>
  
  <p>The Ganges shark has long been one of the most elusive creatures on the planet. Believed to survive in only a handful of fragmented populations across South and Southeast Asia, with an estimated 240 adults remaining according to the IUCN Red List, the species is notoriously difficult to track. Biologists usually only confirm its existence when a specimen turns up dead as fishing bycatch.</p>

  <p>However, recent fieldwork has upended what scientists thought they knew about the shark's geographic range. In 2023, researcher Michael Grant of James Cook University led an exploratory expedition to North Kalimantan, Indonesia, alongside university partners. While visiting a village along the Sesayap River—a region previously unassociated with the Ganges shark—Grant encountered a local fisherman who had accidentally caught 17 Ganges sharks in a single week. That astonishing local haul uncovered more individual specimens than had been formally identified worldwide over the prior two decades.</p>
