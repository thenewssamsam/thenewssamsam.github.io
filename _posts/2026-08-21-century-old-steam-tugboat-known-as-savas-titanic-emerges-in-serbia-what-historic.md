  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Resurrection of the Slovenian</h2>
    <p>The rusting, iron skeleton resting near the Serbian town of Sremska Mitrovica is far more than a pile of scrap metal. According to local museum director and historian Andrija Popovic, the vessel—originally named the <em>Vag</em> when constructed in Budapest in 1913 during the Austro-Hungarian Empire—measured roughly 177 feet long and hauled heavy cargo up and down the Danube network.</p>
    
    <p>Its history mirrors the violent turbulence of 20th-century Europe. After surviving World War I, the ship was handed over as war reparations to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes (later Yugoslavia) and renamed the <em>Slovenian</em>. When Nazi Germany occupied the region in World War II, the boat fell under Axis control before being reclaimed by Yugoslav anti-fascist forces in 1944. Tragically, in late April 1945, while transporting wounded fighters, it struck a German mine and sank to the riverbed, where it remained undisturbed for more than eight decades.</p>

    <p>Now, a severe and prolonged European drought has dropped water levels so drastically that locals say the wreckage has never been so fully exposed. Across the continent, shrinking rivers are playing the role of archaeologists, dragging long-buried relics—from explosive-laden Nazi warships to ancient Roman bridge piers and even prehistoric woolly mammoth bones—out of the mud.</p>

    <h3>The Echoes of Conflict in the Danube</h3>
    <p>The <em>Slovenian</em> is not an isolated anomaly. Just downstream along Serbia's eastern border with Romania, the skeletal remains of Nazi Germany's Black Sea fleet—some still packed with wartime munitions—frequently break the surface during dry summers, as they did in 2022 and 2024. These vessels were deliberately scuttled in the waning days of World War II as German forces retreated from advancing Soviet troops. For decades, they rested silently beneath a murky veil, hazards to navigation that were largely forgotten by daily commuters until vanishing water brought them back into view.</p>
