  <section id="historical-parallel">
    <h2>A Historical Parallel: When Chokepoints Shape Empires</h2>
    <p>To understand the current deadlock over the Strait of Hormuz, it helps to look backward. History is filled with moments where great powers clashed over narrow maritime arteries—geographic bottlenecks through which a massive share of global trade and energy must pass.</p>
    <p>Consider the 1956 Suez Crisis, or even the historical precedents of ancient naval blockades where controlling a single narrow strait allowed an empire to dictate terms to rival nations. In each instance, a physical chokepoint transformed a local disagreement into an international economic crisis. When a vital artery like the Strait of Hormuz—through which a significant portion of the world's petroleum flows—becomes a contested zone, the rules of normal trade break down.</p>
    <p>However, history also teaches us the limits of absolute control. While modern naval blockades can effectively restrict state-backed shipping, they also create fertile ground for asymmetric friction, rising insurance premiums, and unpredictable economic blowback. Past maritime standoffs demonstrate that the longer a critical trade corridor remains choked, the greater the pressure on domestic political stability for all nations involved, regardless of relative military strength.</p>
  </section>

    <h2>The Economic Ripple: Oil, Markets, and Everyday Costs</h2>
    <p>The geopolitical stalemate is not merely a diplomatic abstract; it has immediate financial consequences. As news of the expired 60-day window broke, Wall Street equities edged lower while international Brent crude climbed 2.7% to $90.87 a barrel.</p>
    <p>For ordinary consumers, oil price volatility driven by Persian Gulf uncertainty translates directly into higher fuel costs at the pump, increased transportation expenses for goods, and renewed pressure on inflation. When energy markets realize that normal tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz cannot be guaranteed, risk premiums immediately embed themselves into the global economy.</p>
