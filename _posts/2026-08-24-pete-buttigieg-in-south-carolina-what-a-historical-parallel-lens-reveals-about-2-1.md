  <p class="ncg-lead">When former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg took the podium in Florence and Orangeburg, South Carolina, this weekend, he was confronting ghosts from his 2020 campaign trail. As he weighs a potential second run for the White House in 2028, Buttigieg is actively working to mend a historical weakness in his political coalition: connecting with Black voters in the state that serves as the official opening contest of the Democratic primary. While the early reception suggests a changed political dynamic, history shows that winning over an electorate requires far more than a single successful trip.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Return to South Carolina</h2>
  <p>During a weekend swing through South Carolina, Buttigieg addressed the South Carolina Democratic Party's Spratt Issues Conference, spoke at Claflin University—a historically Black university in Orangeburg—and attended an NAACP Freedom Fund Dinner. His itinerary also included fundraising in Charleston and meeting with local leaders.</p>
  
  <p>This trip stands in stark contrast to his 2020 presidential bid, where a disappointing fourth-place finish in South Carolina effectively ended his campaign and laid bare his struggles to secure support among Black voters. This time around, however, Buttigieg received a standing ovation at a local Black church after local leaders credited him with helping direct millions of federal dollars toward downtown revitalization projects during his tenure in President Joe Biden's Cabinet.</p>

  <p>Yet, as potential 2028 contenders like California Governor Gavin Newsom, Kentucky Governor Andy Beshear, and New Jersey Senator Cory Booker also make early inroads into the state, Buttigieg insists he remains entirely focused on the 2026 midterms. Still, the underlying arithmetic of the Democratic primary remains unchanged: with South Carolina anchoring the first official nominating contest, any viable candidate must prove they can build a broad, multi-racial coalition.</p>
