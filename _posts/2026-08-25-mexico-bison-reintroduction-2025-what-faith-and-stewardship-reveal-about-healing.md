    <p>When conservationists released nearly 50 American bison into the gypsum dunes of Mexico&apos;s Cuatrociénegas nature reserve, they hoped to revive a landscape left barren after centuries of human absence. Just months later, this remarkable reintroduction has triggered a cascading ecological revival—offering a profound glimpse into how creation responds when broken rhythms are restored.</p>
  </div>

  <section class="ncg-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in Cuatrociénegas?</h2>
    <p>In August and November of 2025, a coalition including the Fundación Pro Cuatrociénegas and Coahuila&apos;s Museo del Desierto reintroduced 47 bison into El Santuario, a 4,000-hectare protected zone within the broader 84,000-hectare UNESCO-recognized reserve in northern Mexico. Extirpated from the region by human activities in the 19th century, these massive bovines—weighing up to 1,700 pounds—returned to their historic home.</p>

    <p>According to Gerardo Ruiz Smith, director of the Fundación, the results were almost immediate. Far from being mere passive residents, the bison are acting as true ecosystem engineers. Their heavy hooves till the soil to improve water infiltration, their grazing clears dry vegetation to prevent wildfires, and their fur and manure enrich the depleted Chihuahuan Desert soil.</p>
