  <h2 id="what-happened" style="color: #2c5282; margin-top: 32px; font-size: 1.5em;">What Happened: An Unrelenting Dome of Heat</h2>
  
  <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
    Southern California is currently caught in the grip of a powerful weather pattern that has pushed thermometers well past normal seasonal baselines. According to bulletins from the National Weather Service, regional temperatures are tracking between 6 and 12 degrees warmer than average. CBS LA has responded by issuing a Next Weather Alert through Tuesday, followed by a Next Weather Watch stretching through Friday as the intense thermal dome refuses to budge.
  </p>
  
  <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
    While coastal areas are dealing with sticky mid-to-high 90s, inland communities in the Inland Empire and parts of Orange County face an even harsher reality. Meteorologists project peak readings between 107 and 112 degrees by midweek. Perhaps most concerning from a public health perspective is the lack of nighttime relief. Overnight minimum temperatures are also breaking records, depriving homes without robust air conditioning of the vital cool-down window the human body relies on to recover from daytime thermal stress.
  </p>

  <h2 id="health-perspective" style="color: #2c5282; margin-top: 32px; font-size: 1.5em;">A Health and Wellness Perspective: How Extreme Heat Affects the Body</h2>
  
  <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
    When ambient temperatures soar past 100 degrees, our biological systems are forced into overdrive. The human body is a marvel of thermal regulation, primarily relying on sweat evaporation to cool itself down. However, when humidity rises and overnight temperatures stay elevated, that cooling mechanism begins to fail. 
  </p>
  
  <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">
    Health experts emphasize that extreme heat is a cumulative stressor. Day one of a heat wave feels uncomfortable; day four or five of continuous high temperatures leaves the cardiovascular system severely strained as the heart pumps harder to push blood to the skin for cooling. This is why heat-related illnesses—ranging from exhaustion and painful cramps to life-threatening heatstroke—often spike later in a prolonged weather event rather than on day one.
  </p>

  <p style="margin-bottom: 16px;">

    <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Prioritize Hydration Early:</strong> Do not wait until you feel thirsty to drink water. Keep fluids replenished throughout the day, and limit caffeinated or alcoholic beverages that can accelerate dehydration.</li>
    <li style="margin-bottom: 8px;"><strong>Create Micro-Climates at Home:</strong> If central air conditioning is unavailable or too costly to run constantly, identify one cool room in the house—ideally on the lower level away from direct afternoon sun—and use fans to circulate air effectively.</li>

  </details>
