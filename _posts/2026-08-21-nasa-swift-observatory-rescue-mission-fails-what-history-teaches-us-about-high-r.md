  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened to the Swift Observatory Rescue?</h2>
    <p>Launched in 2004, NASA's Swift Observatory has spent more than two decades peering into the deepest corners of the universe, studying gamma-ray bursts—the most powerful explosions known to astrophysics. But time and space weather caught up with the satellite. Increased solar storms since fall 2024 accelerated its orbital decay, pulling the observatory closer to Earth's atmosphere than anticipated.</p>

    <p>Facing the inevitable destruction of a prized scientific asset, NASA took a calculated risk. The agency awarded a $30 million contract to Arizona-based Katalyst Space to build the LINK spacecraft—an 880-pound robotic vehicle launched on July 3 via a Northrop Grumman rocket. The mission objective was historic: grab Swift in low-Earth orbit, boost it to a safer 370-mile altitude, and extend its operational life by several years.</p>
    
    <p>It would have been the first time a commercial robotic spacecraft captured a government satellite never designed for in-space servicing. Instead, less than a month into the mission, LINK experienced severe issues, began spinning out of control, and failed to stabilize despite multiple thruster burns. NASA announced on August 19, 2026, that the rescue is officially off. The Swift Observatory will reenter Earth's atmosphere later this year and burn up safely.</p>
