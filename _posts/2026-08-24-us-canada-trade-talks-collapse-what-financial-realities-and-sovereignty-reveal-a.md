<h2>What Happened in the US-Canada Trade Collapse?</h2>
<p>When the midnight deadline arrived on Saturday, years of integrated cross-border commerce collided with political brinkmanship. The US-Canada trade talks, which Washington had signaled were nearing a historic agreement, disintegrated entirely. President Donald Trump imposed steep 50% tariffs on $20 billion worth of Canadian goods, prompting Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney to recall his negotiating team and promise dollar-for-dollar retaliatory measures. According to reports from the Guardian, the breakdown occurred after last-minute US demands crossed red lines regarding Canadian sovereignty—specifically targeting provincial bans on US alcohol sales.</p>

<p>For decades, the border between the United States and Canada was treated as an administrative formality for integrated supply chains, automotive manufacturing, and energy distribution. Today, that border is hardening into an economic barrier. The sudden collapse serves as a stark reminder that international trade agreements are only as durable as the political goodwill supporting them. When geopolitical priorities shift, economic stability often pays the price.</p>

<h2>The Financial Mechanics of a 50% Tariff War</h2>
<p>From a financial and economic perspective, a 50% tariff is not merely a political statement; it is a massive structural shock to cross-border supply chains. Tariffs are taxes levied on imported goods, paid directly by importing companies within the importing country—not by the exporting nation. In this scenario, American businesses importing Canadian products—ranging from winter anoraks to Christmas ornaments, steel, aluminum, and automobiles—face an immediate 50% cost hike.</p>

<h2>Sovereignty vs. Economic Survival</h2>

The flashpoint involved provincial restrictions on US alcohol sales—a policy enacted by Canadian provinces in response to earlier tariff rounds. When Washington demanded the reversal of these bans as a precondition for lower tariffs on crucial sectors like automotive and metals, Canadian leadership balked.</p>
