  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: Pope Leo's St. Peter's Address</h2>
    <p>After spending months studying the major documents of the Second Vatican Council, Pope Leo XIV used his weekly general audience inside St. Peter’s Basilica to address one of the council's most polemical legacies: the reform of the liturgy. Speaking to pilgrims and visitors, the pope asserted that updating liturgical rites was essential to ensure that the faithful could actively participate in the celebration of the Mass rather than simply observing from the pews.</p>

    <p>The pope's remarks centered on <em>Sacrosanctum Concilium</em>, the council's constitution on the liturgy. This historic document opened the door for celebrating the Mass in the vernacular languages of local communities and altering elements of the traditional Latin rite. Pope Leo recalled how the pre-conciliar era often left ordinary believers attending Mass as passive witnesses. By making biblical texts and prayers more accessible, the church sought to awaken a deeper appreciation for the sacred mysteries.</p>

    <p>This papal teaching arrives at a particularly sensitive moment. The liturgical reform has remained a source of intense debate since 1963, reaching a fever pitch in recent years surrounding restrictions on the pre-Vatican II Latin Mass and recent tensions involving traditionalist communities. By firmly placing the reform within the living tradition of the church, Pope Leo has signaled that the path forward involves embracing the council's vision rather than retreating into polarization.</p>

    <h2>Theological Roots of Active Participation</h2>
    <p>To understand why Pope Leo defends the liturgical changes, we must examine the core theological concept of <em>participatio actuosa</em>—active participation. In Christian tradition, worship is not meant to be a theatrical performance watched by an audience, but a corporate offering where every baptized member plays a vital role. The Body of Christ functions best when all its members are spiritually engaged.</p>

Traditionalist critics often argue that modern adaptations strip away the sense of mystery and sacred reverence that drew generations to prayer.</p>
