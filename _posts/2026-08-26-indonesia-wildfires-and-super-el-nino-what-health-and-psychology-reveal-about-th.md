  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in Kalimantan and Beyond?</h2>
    <p>When Suwadi steps outside his home in Indonesian Borneo, he cannot see five meters ahead. Each breath, he reports, feels uncomfortably like smoking a cigarette. For weeks, massive fires have ravaged tens of thousands of acres of land across Kalimantan, torching ancient rainforests and sparking a severe trans-boundary haze that has drifted across borders to threaten Malaysia and Brunei while putting Singapore on high alert.</p>
    
    <p>Data from forestry ministries indicates that nearly 94,000 hectares (about 232,000 acres) burned nationwide in July alone. While slash-and-burn clearing techniques used for palm oil plantations often spark seasonal blazes, authorities note that a super El Niño weather pattern is supercharging the fires, with peak intensity expected in September and seasonal rains unlikely to arrive before November.</p>

    <h2>Psychology of Crisis: Normalcy Bias and Collective Action</h2>
    <p>When staring down an environmental disaster of this magnitude, human behavior often exhibits classic psychological patterns. One prominent phenomenon is <strong>normalcy bias</strong>—the psychological state where people underestimate the likelihood of a disaster and its potential effects. Day after day, children walk to school and adults commute through a yellow-gray haze because the routine of daily life provides a psychological anchor against overwhelming uncertainty.</p>
