<h1 id="top">College Football AP Top 25 Released: What Psychology Reveals About Why Fans Lose Their Minds Over Preseason Polls</h1>
<p class="ncg-meta-info"><time datetime="2026-08-15">Published: August 15, 2026</time> &bull; 6 min read</p>
</header>

<p>The wait is finally over, and the annual August ritual has officially arrived. The preseason AP Top 25 college football poll is out, which means millions of fans across the country are simultaneously celebrating, fuming, and calling into local sports radio stations to explain why their favorite program was completely robbed. Ohio State sits at the peak, traditional powers like Clemson are lurking outside the list, and a dozen new hopefuls are dreaming of a championship run just two weeks before kickoff. But before everyone starts clearing out their garage rafters to hang championship banners based on an August sheet of paper, it is worth looking back at last season. Twelve teams that appeared in the preseason top 25 poll a year ago—including heavyweights like Penn State, Clemson, and LSU—finished the season completely unranked. The polls are half-guess, half-tradition, and entirely built to generate debate. Yet every single summer, smart people lose their minds over them. Why do we care so much about a ranking that means virtually nothing before a single snap has been played?</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the 2026 Preseason AP Top 25</h2>
<p>The Associated Press released its initial 2026 college football rankings, setting off the final fortnight of anticipation before the regular season begins. Ohio State opens at No. 1, followed by Oregon at No. 2, Georgia at No. 3, Notre Dame at No. 4, and Texas rounding out the top five. Further down the list, programs like Alabama sit at No. 13, USC and BYU tie at No. 14, and traditional powerhouses like Clemson found themselves on the outside looking in, landing as the first team receiving votes just outside the top 25. Analysts have already zeroed in on high-stakes early matchups, such as Texas hosting Ohio State in Week 2, while coaches and athletic departments brace for the intense scrutiny that comes with early-season expectations. Yet, as history proves year after year, preseason lists are notoriously inaccurate predictors of December reality. Last year alone, nearly half of the preseason top 25—twelve teams in total—failed to even make the final poll.</p>

<h2 id="a-psychology-perspective">A Psychology Perspective: Why Preseason Polls Trigger Our Brains</h2>
<p>To understand why a group of sportswriters guessing about September football can turn otherwise rational adults into raging arguers, we have to look past the gridiron and into human cognitive wiring. Psychology offers a few clear explanations for why the preseason AP Top 25 carries such an intense emotional grip:</p>

<p><strong>1. Normalcy Bias and Loss Aversion</strong><br>
Fans treat their team's position in an August poll as an absolute baseline of reality. When a rival team is ranked higher, the brain processes it through loss aversion—we feel the sting of an unfair slight much more acutely than the joy of a fair ranking. We assume yesterday's status quo will stretch indefinitely into the autumn, ignoring how wildly unpredictable a five-month college football season actually is.</p>

<p><strong>2. Groupthink and the Echo Chamber</strong><br>
AP voters are scattered across different regions, often relying on shared narratives, historical reputation, and conference prestige rather than exhaustive firsthand study of all 134 FBS rosters. This creates a feedback loop. Once a few prominent voters slot a team high because of their helmet logo or a famous coach, others follow suit to avoid looking out of touch. Fans then ingest this consensus, mistaking a collective guess for objective fact.</p>

<p><strong>3. The Illusion of Control Through Prediction</strong><br>
Summer in sports is a dead zone. Fans crave football, but there are no games to watch, no scores to check, and no Saturdays to plan around. Debating the validity of a No. 14 ranking gives our brains a sense of active participation. By arguing over snubs and overrates, we feel like we have some stake in the upcoming season before the players even take the field.</p>

<p>&ldquo;Preseason polls are not an assessment of what teams will accomplish in December; they are a mirror reflecting our desperate need for football to return in August.&rdquo;</p>

<h2 id="how-should-readers-respond">How Should Fans Respond to the Rankings?</h2>
<p>You do not need to let an August poll ruin your late summer. Instead of letting a writer in another state dictate your anxiety levels, you can channel that fan energy more productively:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Keep receipts quietly:</strong> If your team was left out or slotted too low, let the players prove it on the field. The poll in August is irrelevant compared to the rankings that drop in November.</li>
  <li><strong>Remember the turnover:</strong> Keep in mind that nearly half of last year's preseason darlings missed the final poll entirely. High expectations in August are no guarantee of winter success.</li>
  <li><strong>Enjoy the buildup:</strong> Treat the rankings for what they are—entertainment and conversation fodder designed to pass the time until real games start.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="conclusion">One Last Thought</h2>
<p>College football is America's ultimate theater precisely because it defies neat arithmetic and preseason projections. The magic of the sport lies in its chaos—in transfer portals, true freshmen turning into superstars, and blue-bloods stumbling out of the gate while unheralded programs make historic runs. So let the voters argue, let sports talk radio roar, and let the debates rage on. In two weeks, the polls will matter a little less, and the actual game will start speaking for itself. Enjoy the kickoff. The wait is almost over.</p>

<div class="ncg-closing-box">
<h3>One Last Thought</h3>
<p>We invest so much emotional capital into preseason projections because we want certainty in a sport defined by glorious chaos. But the beauty of college football is that August opinions expire the moment the ball is kicked off. Save your energy for the Saturdays that actually count.</p>
