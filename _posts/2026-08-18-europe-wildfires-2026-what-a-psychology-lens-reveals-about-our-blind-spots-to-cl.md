    <h2>What Happened: A Weekend of Fire Across Europe</h2>
    <p>Over the weekend, a staggering wave of extreme weather turned parts of Europe into active disaster zones. In Belgium, a massive wildfire scorched roughly 30 square kilometers of the High Fens, a fragile nature reserve near the German border. The blaze easily outpaced the country's previous record-setting fire from 2011, fueled by bone-dry soil and intense heat waves touching 37 degrees Celsius (98.6 Fahrenheit).</p>
    <p>Meanwhile, tragedy struck in Greece, where two people lost their lives in a rapidly spreading wildfire on the island of Salamina, west of Athens. Near-gale-force winds pushed flames toward residential neighborhoods in minutes, forcing coast guard vessels to evacuate hundreds of beachgoers. From France and the United Kingdom to Germany, emergency crews battled parallel flare-ups as persistent drought conditions maintained a high-alert status across the continent.</p>

  <section id="psychology-perspective" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The Psychology Perspective: Why We Look Away Until Smoke Reaches the Doorstep</h2>
    <p>Human behavior in the face of slow-moving, escalating crises is governed by powerful cognitive patterns. When looking at how communities, tourists, and even regional authorities respond to extreme weather, three distinct psychological mechanisms regularly come into play.</p>
    
    <h3>1. Normalcy Bias</h3>
    <p>Normalcy bias is the mental state that causes people to underestimate the possibility of a disaster or its possible effects. When temperatures spike or dry spells stretch into weeks, our brains default to historical baseline patterns. We tell ourselves, &quot;It has always been hot in July,&quot; or &quot;Forest fires happen down south, not here in the Belgian uplands.&quot; This bias explains why evacuation orders in Liège province or Salamina often meet initial hesitation—people struggle to reconcile an unprecedented crisis with their everyday routines until visible smoke forces the issue.</p>

    <h3>2. The Illusion of Green Resilience</h3>
    <p>Regions like the High Fens in Belgium or the pine forests of southwestern France are deeply embedded in regional identity as damp, resilient landscapes. When soil dries out to historic depths, the underlying vulnerability contradicts our deeply ingrained mental image of a lush, temperate Europe. We rely on environmental assumptions built for a climate that no longer exists.</p>

    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>&quot;We tend to plan for the world we remember, while nature reacts to the climate we are actually standing in.&quot;</p>
    </div>

    <h3>3. Cognitive Dissonance and Collective Exhaustion</h3>
    <p>Following consecutive summers of severe heat waves, droughts, and fires across France, the UK, and Germany, citizens face continuous crisis fatigue. When threats become an annual fixture, the human mind often retreats into cognitive dissonance—either minimizing the risk to preserve emotional bandwidth or feeling paralyzed by the sheer scale of systemic change. Understanding these mental roadblocks is the first step toward building genuine, clear-headed community resilience.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="how-to-respond" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>How Should We Respond? Practical Preparedness in an Era of Extremes</h2>
    <p>Recognizing our cognitive blind spots does not mean giving in to panic; rather, it calls for practical, grounded adjustments in how households and communities prepare for volatile weather seasons.</p>
    <ul>
      <div>
        <strong>Upgrade Your Local Risk Awareness:</strong> Do not assume your region is immune simply because a wildfire or severe drought has never happened there in living memory. Check municipal fire-risk ratings during heat waves.
      </div>
      <div>
        <strong>Heed Early Warnings Promptly:</strong> When local authorities in Waimes, Bütgenbach, or Greek coastal villages advise evacuation, do not wait to see flames. Normalcy bias thrives on delay; decisive early action saves lives.
      </div>
      <div>
        <strong>Support Cross-Border and Community Cooperation:</strong> Just as Belgian and German farmers hitched water tankers to help neighbors fight the High Fens blaze, local resilience depends on community readiness and mutual aid.
      </div>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section id="closing-thought" class="ncg-section ncg-closing-box">
    <h2>One Last Thought</h2>
    <p>The scorching fires sweeping from the Belgian moorlands to the Greek islands are more than meteorological anomalies—they are mirrors reflecting how we process danger in real time. By acknowledging our natural inclination toward normalcy bias and complacency, we can replace wishful thinking with clear-eyed readiness. The climate may test our borders and our landscapes, but our response remains our own choice.</p>
    <p class="ncg-closing-line">Clear minds and early action are our best defense when the horizon starts to smoke.</p>
