<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in the GOP Primaries?</h2>
<p>Earlier this spring, political observers watched as President Donald Trump seemed to effortlessly sweep aside Republican critics and primary challengers in a display of raw political power. Figures like Sen. Bill Cassidy of Louisiana and Rep. Thomas Massie of Kentucky fell to candidates boasting the coveted presidential endorsement. To many, it looked like an unbreakable grip on the base.</p>

<p>Yet, the summer has brought a decidedly more complicated landscape. A string of recent losses by Trump-backed candidates across Florida, Wyoming, Minnesota, and other states has begun to chip away at that winning aura. From Florida's 19th Congressional District—where Trump pick Catalina Lauf fell to local media mogul Jim Schwartzel—to gubernatorial races in Wyoming and Minnesota, voters are increasingly demonstrating an independent streak. Rep. Cory Mills in Florida and Rep. Max Miller in Ohio also pushed ahead despite presidential warnings, while Rep. Andy Ogles lost his reelection bid in Tennessee following voter backlash.</p>

<p>While Trump has still secured notable victories in key battlegrounds like Arizona and Michigan, these defeats raise a compelling question: Is the kingmaker effect losing its gravity? To understand this shift, we have to look past the political headlines and examine the underlying mechanics of human behavior and cognitive psychology.</p>
</section>

<h2>The Psychology of Political Endorsements</h2>
<p>Why do voters follow endorsements in the first place? From a psychological standpoint, political choices are cognitively demanding. Voters are constantly bombarded with policy details, candidate backgrounds, and conflicting media narratives. To cope with this overwhelming cognitive load, the human brain relies heavily on mental shortcuts, or heuristics.</p>
