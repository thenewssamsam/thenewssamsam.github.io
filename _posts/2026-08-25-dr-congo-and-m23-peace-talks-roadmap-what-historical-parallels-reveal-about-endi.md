<section id="what-happened" class="drc-section">
<h2>What Happened: The Geneva Roadmap</h2>
<p>The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and an M23-led rebel alliance have agreed on a sequenced roadmap for peace talks following five days of intense negotiations in Switzerland. Mediated with the help of the United States, Qatar, Togo, Switzerland, and the African Union Commission, the agreement builds upon a framework pact signed in Doha late last year. Both sides have committed to a standardized ceasefire violation reporting mechanism, and a verification mission is scheduled to deploy to Minembwe in South Kivu province.</p>

<p>Yet, optimism remains heavily guarded. Even as diplomats shake hands in European conference rooms, the reality on the ground in eastern Congo remains punishingly violent. Clashes persist, accusations fly over drone strikes in populated zones, and a catastrophic humanitarian crisis—compounded by a devastating Ebola outbreak that has claimed over 2,500 lives since May—continues to unfold. To understand whether this paper roadmap can survive contact with a brutal battlefield, we must look backward.</p>

Instead, they follow a predictable, fragile pattern.</p>

<h3>1. The Trap of Sequencing Without Local Trust</h3>
<p>In many historical peace processes, such as early attempts to stabilize the Balkans or parts of West Africa in the late 1990s, diplomats excel at drafting sequenced timelines (like the Doha framework and the new Swiss roadmap). However, paper steps often fail because local command structures lack mutual trust. When the DRC handed over 15 prisoners to the Congo River Alliance (AFC) and M23 via the Red Cross earlier this month, it was a vital confidence-building gesture. Yet, history warns that prisoner exchanges can be instantly derailed by a single localized skirmish or disputed airstrike, such as the recent drone allegations in Minembwe.</p>

<h3>2. The Challenge of Proxy and Regional Entanglements</h3>
<p>The eastern Congo conflict is not merely an internal domestic dispute; it is heavily entangled with cross-border accusations involving regional neighbors. Kigali consistently denies backing M23, while Kinshasa denies supporting Hutu militias like the FDLR. This mirrors historical stalemates in Central Africa and the Horn of Africa, where proxy dynamics often outlive official diplomatic breakthroughs. Past peace deals—such as the Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement in 1999—struggled precisely because external actors retained strategic interests on the ground long after signatures dried on parchment.</p>

<h3>3. Verification Deficits</h3>
<p>A roadmap is only as strong as its verification mechanism. The planned deployment of a ceasefire verification mission to Minembwe is a nod to historical lessons learned: without neutral, on-the-ground observers capable of investigating violations impartially, propaganda quickly replaces truth. In past African and global conflicts, the absence of robust monitoring allowed minor ceasefire breaches to cascade into full-scale military offensives.</p>
