<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened</h2>
    <p>Nearly a quarter-century after the September 11 terrorist attacks, a military judge has officially scheduled the trial for the alleged mastermind of the plot, Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, to begin in June 2028. Air Force Lt. Col. Michael Schrama rejected a proposed January 2027 start date pushed by prosecutors, instead giving the defense and prosecution another 18 months to prepare at the U.S. naval base at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba.</p>
    
    <p>Mohammed will stand trial alongside three other co-defendants: Walid bin Attash, Ali Abdul Aziz Ali, and Mustafa Ahmed al-Hawsawi. All four men have been held at Guantanamo Bay since 2006 following their captures abroad. They face war crimes and murder charges for orchestrating the suicide hijackings that brought down the World Trade Center towers, struck the Pentagon, and crashed a fourth plane in a Pennsylvania field on September 11, 2001, resulting in nearly 3,000 deaths.</p>
    
    <p>The path to this trial date has been fraught with unprecedented legal hurdles. The military commissions system, established during the Bush administration and heavily tested under Obama, has faced years of appeals, procedural challenges, and intense debates over government transparency. Defense attorneys have repeatedly argued that the staggering delays stem from government efforts to shield details of the "enhanced interrogation techniques"—including waterboarding Mohammed 183 times—used against detainees at CIA black sites before their transfer to Cuba. Last summer, a split federal appeals court threw out a plea agreement that would have spared Mohammed the death penalty in exchange for a guilty plea, forcing the case back onto a grueling trial trajectory.</p>

    <p>When news breaks that a trial for crimes committed in 2001 will not take place until 2028—27 years after the fact—the immediate public reaction is often a mix of frustration, disbelief, and profound exhaustion. How can a nation built on the rule of law take nearly three decades to bring primary defendants to trial? Yet, when we step back and view this through the lens of history, we find that protracted justice is a recurring, albeit painful, hallmark of international and war crimes tribunals.</p>
