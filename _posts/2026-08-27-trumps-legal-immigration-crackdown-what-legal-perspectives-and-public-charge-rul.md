  <p class="ncg-lead">The Trump administration is expanding its sweeping immigration enforcement efforts beyond border crossings to target individuals navigating the U.S. immigration system legally. With a temporary pause on immigrant visa processing worldwide and a planned revocation of hundreds of thousands of tourist and business visas, legal experts and applicants alike are trying to make sense of shifting federal policies.</p>

  <p>For years, public debates surrounding immigration policy have focused heavily on unauthorized border crossings, deportations, and asylum seekers arriving at the southern border. However, recent moves by the State Department and the Department of Homeland Security signal a broad pivot toward restricting legal pathways as well. By tightening public charge guidelines and reexamining how temporary visas interact with asylum claims, the administration is reshaping the landscape for families, workers, and international travelers.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The New Visa and Asylum Policies</h2>

  <p>According to reports by The Associated Press, the State Department recently paused all immigrant visa applications globally while briefing consular officials on stricter "public charge" rules. This temporary pause, which began in early August and is expected to run through early to mid-September, affects individuals seeking to immigrate through family-sponsored channels—such as parents, spouses, and children of U.S. citizens—whose interviews are being rescheduled.</p>

  <p>Simultaneously, the administration is preparing to revoke the business and tourism (B1 and B2) visas of up to 200,000 foreigners who entered the country legally on tourist permits and subsequently applied for asylum. While immigration attorneys note that this revocation will not directly remove individuals already inside the United States with pending asylum cases, it serves as a powerful deterrent and enforcement signal to future applicants.</p>

The revocation is largely symbolic and strategic—designed to send a clear message that tourist visas should not be utilized as a stepping stone to permanent residency.</p>
