  <p class="ncg-lead">When Tyson Foods announced it was shopping its massive beef processing facility near Pasco, Washington, it sent shockwaves through the regional agricultural community. Handling roughly 2,000 head of cattle a day and employing 1,400 workers, the Wallula plant is the economic heartbeat of Walla Walla County and the largest slaughter facility in the Pacific Northwest—and local farmers are pointing directly at state regulatory costs as the catalyst.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened at the Tyson Pasco Facility?</h2>
  <p>On August 13, Tyson Foods made major waves across the American agricultural sector. Alongside shuttering operations at a beef facility in Joslin, Illinois, and a case-ready plant in Eagle Mountain, Utah, the corporate giant announced it is pursuing a buyer for its Pasco-area beef plant. Tyson pointed to one of the most severe cattle shortages in U.S. history, stating plans to anchor its remaining beef operations around three massive hubs in Nebraska, Kansas, and Texas.</p>
  
  <p>The Pasco facility—technically located in Wallula—has operated under Tyson ownership since 2001. It processes the vast majority of fed cattle in the Northwest. However, industry stakeholders note that the decision to sell rather than close the doors immediately offers a window of opportunity, provided a buyer steps forward before market disruptions take full effect.</p>

  <h2 id="ripple-effects">What Happens If the Plant Goes Dark?</h2>
  <p>The financial math facing Pacific Northwest cattle producers if the Pasco plant fails to find a buyer is stark. Jack Field of the Washington Cattle Feeders Association emphasized that the region requires two viable beef processing plants, not just one. Losing the Pasco facility would instantly slash regional slaughter capacity roughly in half, leaving the AgriBeef plant in Toppenish as the solitary large-scale operation standing.</p>

Ultimately, a healthy food supply depends on recognizing that farming families cannot continually absorb mounting financial burdens without eventually breaking.</p>
