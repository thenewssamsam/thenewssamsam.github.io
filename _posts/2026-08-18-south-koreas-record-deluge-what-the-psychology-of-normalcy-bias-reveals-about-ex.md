It shatters our baseline assumption of how the world is supposed to work. This week, southern South Korea found itself grappling with a historic weather event that compressed months of precipitation into a single weekend, leaving communities reeling and forcing us to examine not just our physical infrastructure, but our psychological readiness for sudden environmental shocks.</p>

<h2 id="nks-what-happened">What Happened in South Korea and the Philippines?</h2>

<p>Torrential downpours that began pounding southern parts of South Korea over the weekend resulted in tragic loss of life and widespread structural damage. According to the interior and safety ministry, the city of Geoje in South Gyeongsang province recorded an astonishing 800mm (31 inches) of rain over the weekend, with cumulative totals reaching 912mm by Monday evening. Neighboring Tongyeong also experienced severe flooding, receiving 671.9mm during the same period.</p>

<h2 id="nks-why-it-matters">Why Sudden Extreme Weather Shocks Us</h2>

<p>To understand why severe weather events cause such profound disruption, we have to look past the meteorological data and examine human behavior. For most of us, day-to-day life operates on an assumption of continuity. We expect tomorrow's weather to roughly resemble yesterday's, or at least fit within historical seasonal norms.</p>

<p>When weather systems begin delivering multi-foot deluges in a matter of hours, they bypass our slow, deliberate cognitive processing. Communities built around drainage capacities designed for decades past suddenly find themselves confronting an entirely new reality. The immediate danger is physical—rising water, falling earth, trapped residents—but the secondary challenge is cognitive: accepting how quickly the familiar environment can become hostile.</p>

<h2 id="nks-psychology">The Psychology of Normalcy Bias in Climate Crises</h2>

<p>When disaster warnings arrive, human beings frequently display what psychologists call <strong>normalcy bias</strong>—a mental state that causes people to underestimate both the possibility of a disaster and its possible effects. Because we have never experienced an entire summer's rain in 48 hours before, our brains tend to treat the warning as an exaggeration rather than an immediate threat.</p>

<p>Coupled with normalcy bias is <em>loss aversion</em> and the comfort of routine. People hesitate to evacuate their homes, leave property behind, or disrupt their daily schedules because the mental friction of taking drastic action feels larger and more certain than the abstract risk of a natural disaster. It is only when water breaches the doorstep or earth moves against foundations that the psychological barrier breaks down.</p>

<p>Furthermore, groups often experience <em>groupthink</em> during unfolding emergencies. If neighbors stay put, individuals look to each other for social cues, convincing themselves that if others aren't panicking, the situation must not be truly critical. Understanding these cognitive traps is the first step toward building genuine resilience.</p>

<div class="nks-quote-box">
  "Normalcy bias convinces us that tomorrow must look like yesterday—until nature forces us to rewrite the rules in real time."

<h2 id="nks-response">How Communities and Individuals Can Adapt</h2>

<p>Adapting to increasingly volatile weather patterns requires shifting how we prepare, both practically and mentally. Governments and municipal leaders must update infrastructure standards, moving away from historical averages that no longer reflect modern climate realities. But individual preparation matters just as much.</p>

<p>First, citizens must practice preemptive acceptance of rapid environmental change. When meteorologists issue unprecedented warnings—such as an entire summer's rain falling in 48 hours—treating the forecast as a worst-case scenario rather than an alarmist exaggeration can save lives. Second, communities benefit from establishing clear, pre-planned evacuation triggers that eliminate the hesitation caused by waiting to "see how bad it gets."</p>
