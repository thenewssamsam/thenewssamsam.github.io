  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The Breaking of a North American Alliance</h2>
    <p>International relations experienced a seismic jolt when Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney announced a sweeping wave of retaliatory tariffs against the United States. Speaking in a televised national address, Carney confirmed that Canada would match Washington's recent 50 percent tariffs on $20 billion worth of Canadian goods "dollar for dollar." Starting September 8, duties will hit vital US sectors including steel, dairy, home appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics.</p>

    <p>The move comes after Ottawa walked away from what Canadian officials termed a "bad deal" on trade, marking a profound deepening of friction between longtime neighbors and allies. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer disparagingly likened Canada's response to tactics used by Beijing, noting that China and Canada are now the primary nations willing to implement reciprocal countermeasures against Washington's trade policies.</p>
    <p>For decades, the idea of a trade war between the United States and Canada—partners sharing a border and an average daily trade volume of $2.5 billion—seemed almost unthinkable. Yet this rapid escalation raises an urgent question for ordinary observers: When two deeply integrated economies begin putting up walls, what does history tell us about where this path leads?</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-sub-heading">The Echo of the 1930s Smoot-Hawley Act</h3>
    <p>The most prominent historical parallel to current protectionist impulses is the infamous Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930. Initially conceived to protect domestic American agriculture and manufacturing during an economic downturn, the legislation raised US tariffs to historic highs. Rather than securing domestic jobs, it triggered immediate retaliatory measures from America's largest trading partners.</p>
