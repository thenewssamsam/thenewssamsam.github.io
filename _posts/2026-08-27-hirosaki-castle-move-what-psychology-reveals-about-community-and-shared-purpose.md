  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The Massive Return of Hikiya</h2>
    <p>To the rhythmic chants of <em>so-re, so-re</em>, nearly 1,800 people lined up over a weekend in northern Japan to manually pull a 360-tonne castle keep back toward its original foundation. Located in Aomori prefecture, the historic Hirosaki Castle keep had previously been shifted away to allow workers to repair a stone wall damaged during a major earthquake. With the wall completely restored—featuring all 2,185 stones meticulously restacked in their precise original positions—officials faced a choice: use heavy industrial machinery to slide the structure home, or invite the public to participate.</p>
    
    <p>They chose the people.</p>

    <p>The event was heavily oversubscribed, drawing roughly 8,000 applications for just 1,800 slots. Participants traveled from across Japan and around the globe, spanning Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America. Utilizing an ancient Japanese building-relocation technique known as <em>hikiya</em>, where structures are placed on rollers rather than dismantled, the rotating teams of 80 volunteers heaved on 30-metre ropes to advance the castle a little over two metres across two days. While machinery will eventually complete the final stretch of the 73-metre journey, the manual phase remains an intentional, community-building tradition.</p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Shared Effort and Belonging</h2>

When 80 strangers lock their hands onto a thick hemp rope, synchronize their breathing, and pull in unison to the cadence of a traditional chant, individual identity temporarily dissolves into a powerful collective flow state.</p>

    <h3>The Power of Tangible Contribution</h3>
    <p>In an increasingly digital economy where many workers push pixels, manage spreadsheets, or write code that feels entirely abstract, physical labor offers a rare psychological reward: immediate, visible cause and effect. Participants like Motoharu Nakata, a 61-year-old who traveled across the country after participating eleven years prior, experienced a deep sense of legacy and continuity. Moving the castle with their own muscles gives people a tangible stake in heritage preservation that no financial donation or digital tourist ticket could ever replicate.</p>

    <h3>Overcoming Isolation Through Ritual</h3>
    <p>Modern life often breeds profound social isolation, even in densely populated urban centers. Events rooted in ancient tradition provide a structured, safe social framework where strangers can cooperate intensely without awkwardness. By placing people side-by-side on a rope, the <em>hikiya</em> process cuts through social stratification. Wealthy executives, local students, and international tourists all pull with the exact same weight distribution. That radical leveling effect creates an immediate, profound psychological bond.</p>
