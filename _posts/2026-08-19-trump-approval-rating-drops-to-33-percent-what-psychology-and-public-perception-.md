  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: A Historic Low in普 the Polls</h2>
    <p>United States President Donald Trump has seen his approval rating slide to 33 percent—matching the lowest point of his entire political career, according to a newly released Reuters/Ipsos poll. Conducted over four days, the survey reveals that an overwhelming 64 percent of Americans disapprove of his job performance, driven largely by public anxiety over the ongoing US-Israel war on Iran and its heavy economic toll.</p>
    <p>The conflict, which began in February with promises of a swift resolution to prevent Iran from developing a nuclear weapon, has instead settled into a resilient stalemate. While the administration initially framed the intervention as a short-term necessity, consumers nationwide are feeling the pinch at the pump. According to the American Automobile Association (AAA), gasoline prices are up nearly a third compared to this time last year. Even within his core political base—including self-identified Make America Great Again (MAGA) voters—polling indicates that only about a third believe the economic sacrifices of the war are worth the cost.</p>

  <section id="psychology-perspective" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The Psychology of Wartime Discontent</h2>
    <p>To understand why a president's approval rating can drop so sharply over a protracted conflict, we have to look past the political headlines and examine how human beings process prolonged stress, economic pain, and broken expectations. Behavioral psychology offers several clear concepts that explain the current wave of public dissatisfaction.</p>
    
    <h3>The Toll of Normalcy Bias and Broken Expectations</h3>
    <p>When the conflict with Iran began, many citizens relied on normalcy bias—the mental assumption that things will quickly return to baseline. The administration's initial projection that the war would take only a few weeks reinforced this expectation. When reality outpaces those early promises—with 80 percent of Americans now anticipating an extended conflict—the psychological friction is severe. People experience cognitive dissonance: the uncomfortable tension of holding two conflicting realities (supporting a national security goal versus watching household expenses spiral out of control).</p>

    <h3>Loss Aversion and the Salience of Pain</h3>
    <p>Behavioral economics teaches us about loss aversion: human beings feel the psychological pain of a loss roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of an equivalent gain. While abstract geopolitical victories—such as stalling an adversary's nuclear program—are difficult to quantify in daily life, paying nearly 30 percent more at the gasoline pump is immediate, constant, and visceral. Every trip to fill up the tank acts as a repetitive reminder of the conflict's cost.</p>

    <div class="ncg-quote-box" role="region" aria-label="Shareable Quote">
      <p>"Abstract national security goals feel distant, but a thirty percent hike in gasoline prices is felt at every single fill-up. When expectations of a quick victory collide with persistent economic pain, political approval inevitably erodes."</p>
    </div>

    <h3>Groupthink and Base Fracturing</h3>
    <p>We are also witnessing the limits of group cohesion under sustained economic pressure. Historically, intense political bases remain fiercely loyal during external crises due to in-group solidarity. However, when the cost-of-benefit ratio becomes too high for individual households—as evidenced by the Politico poll showing only a third of MAGA voters comfortable with the economic toll—loyalty begins to fracture under the weight of pragmatic reality.</p>
  </section>

    <h2>A Scripture Perspective: Wisdom, Cost, and Leadership</h2>
    <p>Throughout history, the intersection of national conflict, economic strain, and leadership has invited profound moral reflection. Scripture often speaks directly to the heavy burden of governance and the importance of counting the cost before entering into enduring strife.</p>
    <p>In the Gospel of Luke, Jesus poses a practical question that applies just as easily to nations as it does to builders: <em>"Suppose one of you wants to build a tower. Won't you first sit down and estimate the cost to see if you have enough money to complete it?"</em> (Luke 14:28, NIV). This principle of foresight reminds us that actions taken without a clear accounting of their long-term endurance frequently lead to distress and division.</p>
    <p>Furthermore, Proverbs reminds us of the value of quiet counsel and peace over reckless entanglements: <em>"A prudent person foresees danger and takes precautions, but the simple go blindly on and suffer the consequences"</em> (Proverbs 22:3, NLT). This event reminds us to look beyond immediate political justifications and consider the very real human and economic suffering experienced by everyday families. Scripture invites leaders and citizens alike to value peace, practice transparent wisdom, and remember that earthly power and stability are fleeting compared to the enduring welfare of communities.</p>
