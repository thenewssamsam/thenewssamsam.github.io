    <h2>What Happened: The Pivot to Economic D-Day</h2>
    <p>Nearly six months into a grinding conflict that has strained military munitions stocks and depleted crucial interceptor supplies, the United States government announced a sweeping new wave of measures against Iran. Dubbed officially "Operation Economic Outcast" and billed by some officials as an economic D-Day, the initiative targets roughly 60 global individuals and entities engaged in business with Tehran. The move follows persistent demands from President Donald Trump that Iranian leadership relinquish control claims over the Strait of Hormuz and return to nuclear negotiations.</p>

    <p>Yet, analysts note that the announcement fell significantly short of a total blockade. Crucially, major Chinese banks facilitating Iranian oil trades were left untouched. When reporters questioned Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent about why Beijing's financial pipelines were spared, his response was blunt: "Why would I want to blow up the global financial system?" Instead of crippling sovereign trade overnight, experts describe the new package as a psychological operation designed to induce market uncertainty while masking the stark exhaustion of Washington's military runway in the Persian Gulf.</p>

    <h2>A Historical Parallel Perspective</h2>
    <p>History rarely repeats itself identically, but it certainly rhymes with stubborn consistency. Whenever a dominant military power discovers that raw kinetic force cannot secure a rapid, clean political victory, it pivots instinctively toward economic strangulation. We saw a stark template for this during the interwar period and throughout the Cold War, where comprehensive embargoes and financial blockades were heralded as bloodless substitutes for war.</p>
