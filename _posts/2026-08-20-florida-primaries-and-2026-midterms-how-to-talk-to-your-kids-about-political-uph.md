  <p class="ncg-intro">As election night dust settles across Florida, Alaska, and Wyoming, the 2026 primaries have delivered major political upsets, high-profile defeats, and fierce matchups that dominate the evening news. For parents watching the returns with children nearby, these moments of intense political theater raise a recurring question: how do we help our kids make sense of a polarized and unpredictable world without passing on our own anxieties?</p>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the 2026 Primaries?</h2>
    <p>The late-August primary contests set the stage for crucial November general elections across the country. In Florida, the Republican gubernatorial race to succeed term-limited Gov. Ron DeSantis is officially set, with Rep. Byron Donalds facing former Rep. David Jolly. Meanwhile, the Democratic Senate primary saw an unexpected upset as progressive democratic socialist Angie Nixon defeated former National Security Council aide Alex Vindman.</p>
    <p>Across the aisle and across the map, several Trump-backed candidates faced surprising defeats, including Rep. Cory Mills in Florida's 7th District, who fell to former television journalist Ryan Elijah amid lingering committee investigations. Similar primary shake-ups occurred in Wyoming and other states, proving that even heavy endorsements cannot completely insulate incumbent figures from voter frustration and shifting local tides.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="parenting-perspective">
    <h2>A Parenting Perspective: Navigating Political Noise at Home</h2>
    <p>When headlines are filled with dramatic defeats, sharp rhetoric, and sudden political upsets, children absorb more than we realize. Even if they are not watching the live blogs or cable news chyrons, they pick up on the tone of our voices, the tension in the room, and the anxieties swirling around the dinner table. Approaching these moments through a parenting lens means shifting our focus from who won or lost to how we model emotional resilience for the next generation.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-subhead">Age-Banded Communication Tips</h3>
    <p>How we talk about elections must match our children's developmental stages. Younger children do not need the intricate details of primary polling or congressional ethics investigations; they simply need reassurance that their immediate world is safe and predictable.</p>

    <ul>
      <li><strong>Young Children (Ages 5–9):</strong> Keep explanations simple and rooted in civic participation. Frame elections as how communities make decisions together, much like picking a class representative or choosing a game to play on family night. Limit exposure to intense broadcast imagery.</li>
      <li><strong>Pre-Teens and Teens (Ages 10–16):</strong> Invite their questions and validate their curiosity. If they ask about surprise upsets or harsh campaign rhetoric, use it as a teachable moment about how democracy involves diverse opinions, disagreements, and ongoing civic debate.</li>

It invites parents to model a quiet confidence that transcends political outcomes, teaching children that our ultimate security rests not in government power, but in enduring values like kindness, integrity, and grace.</p>
