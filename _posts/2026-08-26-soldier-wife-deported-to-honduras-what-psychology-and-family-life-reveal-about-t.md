  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: A Soldier's Family Split Apart</h2>
    <p>On a Monday morning in late August 2026, Cristy Maryori Villafranca-Trejo was deported to her native Honduras. Her husband, Army Sergeant Hedar Leonel Turcios Juarez, was left behind at Fort Bliss, Texas, scrambling to figure out how to balance his military duties with caring for their six-year-old daughter alone. Villafranca-Trejo's removal marks at least the seventh instance of a spouse or parent of an active-duty U.S. service member being deported under the immigration policy rollbacks enacted during Donald Trump's second presidency.</p>
    
    <p>According to reports by the Associated Press, more than 50 immediate family members of U.S. troops have found themselves placed in federal immigration detention since the administration pulled back long-standing protections that historically shielded military families from sudden deportation. For Sgt. Turcios Juarez—who immigrated from Honduras, enlisted in the Army, and earned his U.S. citizenship in 2024—the reality of this policy shift hit home on July 11 when his wife was detained outside a Walmart near their base. Despite active appeals, an ongoing legal process, and a pending application for military parole-in-place, the deportation order was executed, leaving a soldier to choose between his sworn duty to the nation and his foundational duty as a father.</p>

When individuals experience sudden, uncontrollable disruptions to their primary attachment figures—such as a spouse being abruptly detained and deported—the psychological fallout mirrors acute trauma. For a service member, this creates a profound state of cognitive dissonance.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias and Institutional Betrayal</h3>
    <p>Prior to the current administration's rollbacks, Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) practice generally allowed immediate family members of U.S. troops to adjust their legal status, even if old, overlooked removal orders existed. Families relied on this precedent—a classic example of normalcy bias, where people assume that past protections will hold true. When that system changes overnight, the psychological shock is compounded by feelings of institutional betrayal. As El Paso Representative Veronica Escobar noted after visiting Villafranca-Trejo in detention, a soldier naturally feels a profound sense of abandonment when the system targets the very people standing beside them.</p>
