    <h2>What Happened: The Ross Fire Explodes</h2>
    <p>In rural Palo Pinto County, Texas, weather is always a central preoccupation, but few recent days have tested local endurance quite like this week. Sparking on Monday, the Ross fire ballooned to a staggering 80,000 acres by Wednesday morning, tearing across dry ranchland in the historic Cross Timbers region. Fueled by persistent drought and shifting winds, thick gray plumes of smoke rose high into the Texas sky, sending ash and embers floating as far away as the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex.</p>

First responders from local volunteer departments and state agencies rushed to establish fire lines, prioritize human safety, and protect generations of hard work. Yet, long before official disaster relief resources fully mobilized, local residents were already taking matters into their own hands.</p>

  <section id="the-cowboy-spirit">
    <h2>The Real Meaning of Neighborly Action</h2>
    <p>When 71-year-old cattle rancher Bart Simmons and his son Trent heard that a fellow Palo Pinto County rancher's property was directly in the path of the flames, they did not hesitate. Maneuvering around road closures and thick, choking haze, they hauled six trailers of cattle to safety on their own 2S Ranch. They are now tending to animals suffering from burns and smoke inhalation, treating strangers' livestock with the same care they would give their own.</p>
