<h2>What Happened: The Border Reopens</h2>
<p>The resumption of cattle trade between the United States and Mexico is setting off waves of speculation across the livestock industry. Following border closures that left ranchers and feedlots wondering about the future of cross-border supply chains, trade is finally beginning to flow again. According to Oklahoma State University Extension Livestock Marketing Specialist Derrell S. Peel, the big questions on both sides of the border are straightforward yet complex: How many cattle will cross? What types will arrive? And how fast can ports return to normal operations?</p>

<p style="margin: 0; font-style: italic; font-size: 1.1em; color: #1b5e20;">"Mexican cattle imports averaged 1.17 million head in the 20 years from 2004-23, proving that historical trade patterns remain our most reliable guide through market uncertainty."</p>
</div>

<p>While the world has undoubtedly evolved since the border disruptions began, Peel suggests that history remains the most dependable compass for navigating what comes next. Between 2004 and 2023, annual Mexican cattle imports averaged 1.17 million head, swinging from a low of roughly 702,600 head in 2008 to a high of 1.47 million head in 2012.</p>

<p>From a financial perspective, understanding the mechanics of these livestock imports requires looking past the headlines and examining the actual supply numbers. Historical data shows that Mexican cattle imports correlate heavily with severe drought conditions south of the border, which trigger temporary spikes in trade that eventually average out over time. In fact, a five-year moving average reveals only about a modest 222,000-head variation across years.</p>

If New Mexico ports like Santa Teresa and Columbus open a month later, another 125,000 to 175,000 head could follow. Yet, because very few spayed heifers are likely to be imported initially, and ports will take weeks to ramp back to full capacity, Peel adjusts the realistic expectations downward. For the remainder of 2026, an influx of up to 250,000 head is technically possible, but 150,000 head is a much sounder financial baseline.</p>

<p>The composition of these imports matters immensely for domestic feedlots facing tight U.S. feeder cattle supplies. In 2023, feeder cattle under 700 pounds made up a staggering 98.7% of total Mexican imports, with 29% weighing under 440 pounds. Many of these lightweight animals will head into stocker programs first, while others facing tight domestic supplies may enter feedlots immediately. Once on feed for six to eight months, these cattle could produce beef available for slaughter starting around March 2027, offering a much-needed volume relief valve for the U.S. market.</p>
