<h2>A Psychological Perspective: Why Masks Amplify Terror</h2>
<p>The choice of a Chucky mask in this case is not merely a random costume choice; it taps directly into deep-seated psychological mechanisms. In behavioral science, masks have long been studied for their ability to induce <strong>deindividuation</strong>—a psychological state where an individual loses their sense of self-awareness and personal accountability within a crowd or behind a disguise.</p>

<p>Furthermore, horror icons like Chucky exploit <strong>uncanny valley</strong> effects and childhood fears. By transforming a familiar, innocent object—a child's doll—into a grotesque instrument of menace, perpetrators weaponize absurdity and shock value to paralyze their targets. When victims see an exaggerated, grinning plastic face, their brains struggle to process the surreal threat instantly, delaying the fight-or-flight response. Jameka's instinctive decision to kick her assailant in the chest broke that paralysis, proving that raw human agency remains the ultimate antidote to psychological intimidation.</p>
