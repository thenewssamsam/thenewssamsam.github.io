    <h1 class="ncg-title">US-Iran Standoff in the Strait of Hormuz: What Historical Parallels Reveal About Economic Warfare</h1>
  </header>

  <div class="ncg-intro">
    <p>The high-stakes economic collision between Washington and Tehran in the Strait of Hormuz has turned into a classic test of endurance. As the Trump administration ramps up economic pressure and declares an economic "D-Day" against any nation sustaining Iran, the regime in Tehran refuses to back down despite severe domestic contraction. To understand whether this strategy of economic strangulation will succeed where military force has stalled, we have to look past the immediate headlines and examine how past geopolitical showdowns handled resource choke points and national resilience.</p>
  </div>

  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened in the Strait of Hormuz</h2>
    <p>Months into the conflict, shipping traffic through the crucial Strait of Hormuz remains severely restricted. While pre-war daily transits averaged around 130 vessels, current crossings frequently struggle to reach double digits. According to data from energy consultancy Kpler, crude oil-laden Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs) have dropped from roughly eight daily transits before the conflict down to just two or three.</p>
    
    <p>Yet, the energy markets are not entirely starved. Increasing volumes of oil are bypassing the strait via alternative pipelines, moving through covert ship-to-ship transfers, or navigating the passage "dark"—with their Automatic Identification Systems turned off. While the United States bets that intense economic punishment—including an IMF-projected 5% economic contraction and 80% inflation in Iran—will force the regime to cave, Tehran continues to demonstrate a high threshold for pain, maintaining its leverage over the waterway through the persistent threat of attack.</p>

History, however, tells a far more complicated story.</p>
