<p>The news out of Palm Beach County has left communities reeling. Palm Beach County Clerk of Court and Comptroller Michael Caruso, a 67-year-old former state legislator, was arrested on five felony charges including kidnapping, lewd or lascivious molestation, and child abuse causing mental injury. According to the Florida Department of Law Enforcement affidavit, the allegations involve a young family member who had regular, unsupervised access to the public official. Florida Governor Ron DeSantis immediately suspended Caruso from office as the investigation continues.</p>

<p>For parents, grandparents, and caregivers, headlines like this strike a deep, visceral chord. They force us to confront an uncomfortable and terrifying reality: abuse often happens not behind closed doors with strangers, but within the trusted circles of family and community life. When a prominent public figure holding a position of public trust is accused of such acts, it prompts an urgent, necessary conversation in our own homes about boundaries, supervision, and how we protect the most vulnerable among us.</p>

Upon questioning, the child disclosed that he had been allegedly abused by Michael Caruso during unsupervised outings—such as fishing trips—and notably during a family cruise aboard Royal Caribbean's <em>Icon of the Seas</em> in August 2025.</p>

<h3>1. Dispelling the Myth of the Stranger Danger</h3>
<p>For decades, safety education focused heavily on warning children about strangers in cars or parks. Yet criminological data consistently shows that the vast majority of child abuse is committed by individuals the family knows and trusts—often family members, close friends, or authority figures. Equipping our children means teaching them about body boundaries regardless of who the person is, reinforcing that no adult has the right to ask them to keep secrets from their parents.</p>

<h3>2. The Danger of Isolation Tactics</h3>
<p>The FDLE affidavit notes that Caruso allegedly isolated the young victim by separating him from his parents under everyday pretexts like getting a snack or going fishing. Abusers frequently use grooming tactics designed to create private, unsupervised bubbles. Parents can counter this by maintaining the "rule of open spaces"—ensuring that while trusted relatives can spend time with children, activities should generally remain transparent, observable, and within reasonable boundaries.</p>

<h3>3. Listening to Behavioral Cues</h3>
<p>Children—especially those under the age of 12—may not always have the vocabulary or the emotional safety to immediately report abuse in words. In this case, the investigation was partly initiated after a parent observed behavioral changes and interactions between siblings. Paying close attention to sudden shifts in a child's behavior, regressions, unusual anxieties, or unexplained bodily discomfort can be vital lifelines for early intervention.</p>

<h2 id="how-parents-should-respond">How Families Can Respond Right Now</h2>

<p>It is easy to let news like this curdle into generalized paranoia, but fear is a poor teacher. Instead of panicking, parents can take grounded, practical steps to strengthen family safety:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Establish Open-Door Communication:</strong> Create an environment at home where children feel completely safe telling you anything, even if an adult told them not to. Reinforce that secrets about physical touch are never allowed.</li>
<li><strong>Review Access Regularly:</strong> Know who spends time alone with your children. Even with trusted relatives or community leaders, maintain healthy boundaries and check in frequently with your children after outings.</li>
<li><strong>Listen Without Judgment:</strong> If a child ever expresses discomfort about an interaction with an adult—even a beloved family member—take it seriously, listen calmly, and investigate immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Support Professional Care:</strong> As seen in this case, trauma-informed therapy played a vital role in helping the young victim process distress. Never hesitate to seek professional pediatric counseling if you suspect a child is struggling.</li>
</ul>
