<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the Sewers Near Penn Station</h2>
    <p>Last week at 4 a.m. on Seventh Avenue near 32nd Street, surveillance cameras captured a surreal scene: three individuals wearing overalls and headlamps climbing directly out of a municipal manhole near Penn Station. The New York Police Department has since launched an investigation, with detectives stating that these underground scavengers are primarily migrants searching the city's labyrinthine 7,400-mile sewer system for items of value.</p>
    
    <p>While no immediate arrests were made in this specific incident, police note that similar occurrences have cropped up throughout the summer in Brooklyn and Queens. Inspections by the Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) confirmed no direct threat to critical infrastructure, but city officials have repeatedly emphasized the immense dangers involved. Venturing into nineteenth-century wastewater pipes exposes trespassers to noxious gases, sudden flooding, unstable footing, and confined space hazards. Yet, despite the obvious peril, the phenomenon persists, driven by underground scavenger subcultures documented in places like Venezuela.</p>

    <h3>The Weight of Normalcy Bias</h3>
    <p>Normalcy bias is a cognitive state that causes people to underestimate the likelihood of disaster or refuse to adapt when faced with unprecedented situations. For the average citizen, the idea of entering a New York City sewer pipe feels unimaginable because our daily routines operate under strict societal boundaries. We expect food to come from grocery stores, income from formal employment, and shelter from designated housing. When individuals step outside those boundaries into extreme environments, it highlights a profound divergence in perceived reality.</p>

    <h3>Adaptation, Scarcity, and Loss Aversion</h3>
    <p>Behavioral economists and psychologists frequently study how prolonged scarcity alters decision-making. When individuals arrive in a massive, high-cost metropolis like New York without formal work authorization or established safety nets, standard risk assessments shift dramatically. The psychological mechanism of loss aversion—where avoiding a negative outcome outweighs acquiring a comparable gain—is inverted when survival itself feels precarious. In Venezuela and other regions facing severe economic collapse, informal scavenging in hazardous environments like rivers, dumps, or drainage systems has evolved into a survival strategy of last resort. Transporting those learned behaviors into a North American city creates a jarring culture clash.</p>
