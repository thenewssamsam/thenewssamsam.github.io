  <section id="what-happened" class="spain-ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened at the Border?</h2>
    <p>
      The political pressure on Madrid has reached a boiling point following an unprecedented event in July 2026, when approximately 80,000 people crossed illegally from Morocco into the Spanish autonomous city of Ceuta in a single day. While the vast majority of those individuals have since returned to Morocco, roughly 5,000 people remain inside the enclave, including an estimated 2,000 unaccompanied minors.
    </p>
    <p>
      In response to the ongoing fallout, the Spanish government approved a draft immigration and asylum bill designed to synchronize domestic policy with the broader European Pact on Migration and Asylum, which came into force earlier this summer. Madrid also declared the crisis a matter "of interest to national security," establishing a single command center to coordinate the response after weeks of appeals from local leaders in Ceuta.
    </p>
    <p>
      While the administration of Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez had previously advocated for a more humane and comprehensive approach to EU migration, the new legislative push introduces harsher measures. These include accelerated return procedures and the EU's mandated "screening" protocols, which aim to streamline the rejection of unfounded asylum claims.
    </p>

    <h2>A Legal Rights Perspective on the EU Pact</h2>
    <p>
      From a legal rights framework, the intersection of national sovereignty and international human rights obligations creates a profound tension. The European Pact on Migration and Asylum was built on a dual promise: establishing tighter external borders and faster deportations while maintaining fundamental human rights protections. However, legal experts and human rights organizations have long questioned whether accelerated expulsion procedures can adequately safeguard an individual's right to a fair hearing.
    </p>

    <h3>The Mechanics of the New EU Screening Procedure</h3>
    <p>
      Under the newly implemented EU screening rules, individuals arriving irregularly are subjected to a rapid pre-entry phase. During this window, identity, health, and security checks are conducted before formal asylum eligibility is determined. For frontline states like Spain—affecting both Ceuta and the Canary Islands—the practical effect is a significant compression of administrative timelines, making it considerably harder for applicants to mount a robust legal defense if their initial claims are flagged for fast-tracked rejection.

Under international conventions and Spanish domestic law, minors enjoy heightened protections that distinguish their legal status from that of adult economic migrants.
