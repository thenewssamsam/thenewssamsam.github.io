  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: A Night of Unprecedented Strikes</h2>
    <p>According to reports from Al Jazeera and local officials, the overnight assault on Moscow involved roughly 620 Ukrainian drones, making it the largest such aerial attack on the Russian capital in two years. Moscow Mayor Sergei Sobyanin reported that air defenses intercepted 180 drones over the capital region, while Governor Andrey Vorobyov noted that the town of Pavlovsky Posad and the Bogorodsky district suffered the most serious structural damage, including burned homes and a damaged logistics warehouse belonging to e-commerce giant Wildberries.</p>
    
    <p>Simultaneously, the toll on the Ukrainian side remained devastating. Russian forces deployed two Banderol jet-powered cruise missiles against a residential area in the Pechenihy settlement of the Kharkiv region, resulting in 10 deaths and at least 18 injuries, according to regional Governor Oleh Syniehubov. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy condemned the strike as a brutal attack on civilians, echoing United Nations data showing that July civilian casualties reached their highest monthly levels since the opening months of 2022.</p>

    <p>To understand the strategic logic and the limits of this new wave of long-range drone warfare, we can look back at mid-20th-century aerial strategies, such as the strategic bombing campaigns of World War II. In both historical conflicts and modern asymmetric engagements, military planners have consistently turned to deep-penetration aerial strikes with a specific psychological and economic goal in mind: to disrupt industrial supply lines, strain enemy air defenses, and bring the psychological reality of war directly to the civilian population centers of the opposing nation.</p>
