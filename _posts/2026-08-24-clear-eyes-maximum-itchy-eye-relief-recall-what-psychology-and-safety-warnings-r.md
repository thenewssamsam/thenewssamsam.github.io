<p>When you reach for an over-the-counter medicine to soothe a minor irritation, the last thing you expect is that the remedy itself might carry a hidden risk. A nationwide consumer alert issued by the Food and Drug Administration has put millions of medicine cabinets under sudden scrutiny, involving roughly 40,000 bottles of popular eye drops sold at major nationwide retailers including Walmart, Target, and Kroger. For ordinary shoppers who trust familiar pharmacy brands to be safe and sterile, news like this triggers an immediate wave of unease.</p>

<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The 40,000-Bottle Recall</h2>
    <p>According to reports from federal regulators and news outlets, New York-based Prestige Brand Holdings initiated a voluntary recall of specific batches of <strong>Clear Eyes Maximum Itchy Eye Relief</strong>. The issue centers on a lack of assurance regarding product sterility, which prompted an alert from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).</p>
    
    <p>The recall affects 15-milliliter bottles carrying <strong>Lot Code 2552A</strong> with an expiration date of <strong>September 30, 2027</strong>. Originally announced in July, the recall was formally classified by the FDA on August 14 as a Class II risk level. This classification indicates that the use of or exposure to the affected product may cause temporary or medically reversible adverse health consequences, though the probability of serious harm remains remote.</p>

    <h2>The Psychology of Product Recalls and Everyday Vulnerability</h2>
    <p>Whenever a household product recall hits the news cycle, it creates a subtle ripple in consumer psychology. We rely heavily on cognitive shortcuts—heuristics—to navigate our daily lives. Walking down a grocery or pharmacy aisle, we spot a familiar blue-and-white box of Clear Eyes, a brand trusted for generations, and our brain automatically assigns it a label of absolute safety. We do not inspect the manufacturing sterility reports of every item in our shopping cart.</p>

    <h3>Normalcy Bias and the Medicine Cabinet Shock</h3>
    <p>This reliance on routine exposes us to what psychologists call <em>normalcy bias</em>—the tendency to underestimate the possibility of disaster or manufacturing failure because nothing has ever gone wrong before. When an unexpected recall disrupts that routine, it forces a sudden shift from unconscious trust to hyper-vigilance. Suddenly, every bottle in the bathroom cabinet looks suspicious. People wonder what else might have slipped past quality control.</p>
