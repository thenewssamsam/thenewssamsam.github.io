<section id="what-happened">
  <h2>What Happened: The Accidental Discovery of Life Settlements</h2>
  <p>Most of us view life insurance as a straightforward safety net: you pay monthly or annual premiums, and when you pass away, your beneficiaries receive a lump sum to secure their financial future. For Frank Sierawski, diagnosed with lung cancer at age 35, those policies were meant to protect his wife and three children if the worst happened. But modern medicine intervened, his cancer went into remission, and those policies remained active.</p>
  
  <p>Years later, while browsing a cancer survivor support group online, Sierawski learned about a financial mechanism known as a "life settlement." Instead of letting a policy lapse or waiting for a natural death, policyholders can sell their life insurance policy to third-party investors for a fraction of its face value—typically 20 to 30 cents on the dollar. The investor takes over the premium payments, and when the original policyholder eventually dies, the investor collects the full payout. To Wall Street, human mortality had become an abstract financial asset.</p>

<section id="historical-parallel">
  <h2>A Historical Parallel: From Viatical Settlements to Wall Street Portfolios</h2>
  <p>How did betting on human death become a legal, multibillion-dollar industry? The roots of this market trace back to a foundational legal principle from a 1911 Supreme Court ruling, which established that a life insurance policy is a form of property that owners can sell as they see fit. However, that abstract legal precedent did not spark an immediate commercial frenzy. It took a human tragedy to give the concept a real, beating heart.</p>

That financial crunch led to a handshake deal with a wealthy benefactor who paid the premiums in exchange for the eventual policy payout.</p>

  <p>As word spread through HIV support groups, Page began brokering similar arrangements, helping dying individuals extract immediate value from their insurance policies to fund their final months of care. Over time, these compassionate individual arrangements—known originally as viatical settlements—caught the attention of institutional capital. Wall Street realized that aggregating pools of policies created a predictable statistical model based on life expectancies, turning mortality into just another asset class traded in global financial markets.</p>
