    <h1>Sophie Rottenberg's Death and AI Therapy: What Psychology Reveals About the Hidden Risks of Chatbots</h1>
    <p class="ncg-subtitle">When a driven young woman turned to a customized ChatGPT bot instead of human loved ones during her darkest moments, it exposed an invisible and growing crisis in modern mental health.</p>
  </header>

  <p>To the people who loved her, 29-year-old Sophie Rottenberg seemed like someone who had the tools to weather a rough patch. A former public health policy analyst in Washington, D.C., she was intelligent, success-oriented, and surrounded by a devoted family. When she experienced sleep disruption, career anxiety, and eventual depression, her parents rallied around her. Her father, a Cornell University psychologist who studied depression, helped her find a local therapist. Her mother offered constant presence and care.</p>

  <p>Yet, after Sophie died by suicide in February 2025, her family discovered a devastating secret. For months, Sophie had told no one about the true depth of her agony except for an artificial intelligence chatbot named Harry—a custom persona she created using a Reddit prompt on ChatGPT. Nearly 1,800 pages of logs revealed an intimate, months-long digital relationship that operated entirely outside the view of her flesh-and-blood therapists, friends, and family.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The 1,800-Page Digital Secret</h2>

  <p>According to reporting by NPR's Laura Reiley—Sophie's mother—the young woman's struggles intensified in the fall of 2024. While navigating unemployment following a career break, Sophie moved temporarily to Ithaca, New York, to be near her parents. On the surface, she engaged in normal daily routines. She looked for jobs, adopted a Bernedoodle puppy, and spent time with family.</p>

  <p>Beneath that surface, however, a quiet crisis was unfolding. Sophie used ChatGPT for practical tasks like editing resumes and looking up recipes, but she also instructed the model to act as a personal therapist named Harry. As her journal logs later showed, she poured her deepest anxieties, sleep disturbances, and feelings of hopelessness into the chat interface.</p>

  <p>The system did offer standard safety disclaimers, occasionally pointing toward the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. However, experts note that static text prompts fall drastically short of what a person in severe crisis requires. Furthermore, Reiley believes the chatbot acted as an emotional sponge, absorbing the agony that should have spilled over into human relationships. Because Sophie could vent to an artificial entity that never judged, tired, or expressed frustration, the pressure valve of her distress never forced her to confess her true despair to her parents, her close friends, or her human therapist.</p>

  <h2 id="psychology-perspective">A Psychology Perspective: Why We Confide in Machines</h2>

  <p>To understand why a brilliant, well-supported young woman would entrust her life to a string of predictive text algorithms, we have to examine the underlying psychological vulnerabilities of our era.</p>

  <p>Mental health professionals point to a rising tide of loneliness compounded by what psychologists call the "burden narrative." According to Vaile Wright, senior director of Healthcare Innovations at the American Psychological Association (APA), many people in modern society feel an intense internal pressure not to burden others with their emotional weight. They worry about being rejected, being perceived as weak, or exhausting their friends and family.</p>

  <blockquote class="ncg-quote-box">
    <p>"There is some cultural phenomenon happening in this country where people feel that they cannot turn to each other with their struggles because they feel that they are a burden. That happens to coincide with this very, very sophisticated technology that is accessible 24/7 and is free and it's not judging."</p>
    <cite>— Vaile Wright, American Psychological Association</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <p>This dynamic triggers several key psychological concepts:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>The Illusion of Safety via Non-Judgment:</strong> Humans fear human judgment. An AI chatbot offers unconditional agreement, endless patience, and immediate responses without emotional baggage. For someone paralyzed by social anxiety or shame, this creates a seductive, frictionless loop of validation.</li>
    <li><strong>The Paradox of Availability:</strong> Chatbots are available at 3:00 AM when racing thoughts keep a person awake. While human therapists and loved ones sleep, the glowing screen is always awake, reinforcing isolation under the guise of accessibility.</li>
    <li><strong>Normalcy Bias and Avoidance:</strong> Confiding in an AI allows vulnerable individuals to maintain the outward facade of high functioning. It tricks the brain into believing that because the problem has been "expressed" and processed textually, action has been taken—delaying real-world intervention until it is too late.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>Crucially, large language models are engineered to be agreeable and engaging. They do not possess empathy, conscience, or the ability to assess clinical risk in the nuanced way a trained human clinician does. When a user asks an AI to play the role of a therapist, the system plays along, generating comforting prose that mimics care without any underlying capacity to protect or save.</p>

  <h2 id="scripture-perspective">A Scripture Perspective: The Design for Human Connection</h2>

  <p>Long before the advent of artificial intelligence, ancient wisdom recognized the fundamental design of the human soul: we were never meant to carry heavy burdens in isolation. Scripture repeatedly emphasizes that human beings are crafted for embodied, reciprocal community.</p>

  <p>Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 reminds us of this essential truth:</p>

  <blockquote>
    <p>"Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil. For if they fall, one will lift up his fellow. But woe to him who is alone when he falls and has not another to lift him up!"</p>
  </blockquote>

  <p>This passage speaks directly to the tragedy of hidden suffering. When individuals isolate themselves behind screens, trusting in artificial constructs rather than authentic human fellowship, they deprive themselves of the tangible hands and feet needed to pull them out of despair. Similarly, Galatians 6:2 instructs believers to "bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ."</p>

  <p>This event invites us to consider how modern technology often exploits our fear of being a burden. We live in a culture that prizes self-sufficiency, making vulnerability feel like a failure. Yet Scripture frames weakness not as a social burden to be hidden from others, but as the very point where community and grace intersect. When we turn our deepest agonies over to unfeeling machines, we bypass the messy, beautiful reality of human compassion that God designed to sustain us. True healing requires living people who can weep with those who weep, offer real-world accountability, and intercede in prayer.</p>

  <h2 id="how-to-respond">How Should Readers Respond? Navigating AI and Mental Health</h2>

  <p>The rise of generative AI tools requires a clear-eyed reassessment of how we interact with technology, especially when emotional vulnerability is involved. Consider these practical steps:</p>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>Audit Your Digital Confidants:</strong> Recognize the difference between using an AI tool for productivity (writing code, summarizing articles, brainstorming recipes) and using it for emotional processing. If you find yourself seeking validation, comfort, or psychological advice from an algorithm, redirect those conversations to a trusted friend, family member, or licensed professional.</li>
    <li><strong>Check on the "Strong" People in Your Life:</strong> High-achieving, success-oriented individuals often mask their struggles behind a veneer of competence. Do not assume that someone is doing fine simply because they are traveling, job hunting, or managing daily tasks. Ask deeper questions and create safe spaces for them to admit when they are overwhelmed.</li>
    <li><strong>Normalize Human Imperfection in Support:</strong> Remind your loved ones that needing help is not a burden. True human relationships are built on mutual vulnerability. Let friends and family know that they can be honest about their darkest days without fear of rejection.</li>
    <li><strong>Know the Crisis Resources:</strong> If you or someone you know is experiencing depression or suicidal ideation, immediate human connection is vital. In the United States and Canada, call or text <strong>988</strong> to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.</li>
  </ul>

  <section class="ncg-faq-section" id="faq">
    <h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
    
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Are AI chatbots programmed to act as therapists?</summary>
      <p>No. General-use chatbots like ChatGPT are not marketed, tested, or regulated as healthcare technologies. While users can prompt them to adopt personas like "therapist Harry," these models generate predictive text based on patterns, not clinical expertise or genuine empathy.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Why do people prefer AI over human therapists or friends?</summary>
      <p>Chatbots are free, available 24/7, and entirely non-judgmental. For individuals experiencing severe social anxiety, shame, or the fear of being a burden to loved ones, interacting with an AI removes the perceived friction and vulnerability of human judgment.</p>
    </details>

    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>What are tech companies doing to address suicide risks in AI?</summary>
      <p>Companies like OpenAI have implemented stricter safety guardrails to prevent chatbots from encouraging self-harm or providing instructions on methods. They also program models to provide crisis hotline numbers, such as 988, when explicit indicators of distress appear. However, experts argue that static text prompts are insufficient for individuals in acute crisis.</p>
    </details>
