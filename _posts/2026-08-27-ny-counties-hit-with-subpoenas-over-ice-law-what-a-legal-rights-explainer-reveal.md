<p>When New York Attorney General Letitia James issued subpoenas to several local counties refusing to comply with a new state immigration law, the ongoing friction between state mandates and local enforcement exploded back into the headlines. At the heart of this standoff is a fundamental question of jurisdiction: Can a state forbid local law enforcement from assisting federal immigration authorities, or do counties possess the legal autonomy to chart their own course? As federal courts weigh challenges and local leaders push back, understanding the actual legal framework governing these disputes is more important than ever.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened</h2>
<p>New York Attorney General Letitia James served subpoenas on multiple New York counties—including Rensselaer, Broome, and Steuben—after officials allegedly failed to comply with a newly enacted state immigration statute. Signed into law by Governor Kathy Hochul in May, the statute explicitly bars local jails from detaining individuals solely on Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) detainers and requires local law enforcement agencies to wind down formal 287(g) cooperative agreements with federal immigration officials.</p>

<h2 id="the-legal-battle">The Legal Battle Over ICE Cooperation</h2>
<p>The clash between New York State and resisting counties is not taking place in a legal vacuum. Earlier this month, a federal judge issued a preliminary ruling rejecting a challenge brought by the Trump administration against the state's new immigration restrictions, though the court did strike down a narrow provision attempting to prevent ICE agents from covering their faces while on duty. With the core of the law largely upheld so far, Attorney General James's office is stepping up enforcement against municipal holdouts.</p>

<p>Under the Tenth Amendment and established constitutional doctrines, states possess broad police powers to regulate local municipalities, county governments, and state-funded entities such as local jails. When a state legislature passes a law concerning how local police and county facilities operate, counties—as political subdivisions of the state—generally lack independent sovereignty to override state statutes.</p>

<p>At the same time, immigration law is constitutionally rooted in federal authority. However, the Supreme Court has long established the "anti-commandeering" doctrine, which prevents the federal government from forcing state and local governments to enforce federal regulatory programs. New York's new law effectively flips this dynamic inward: the state is telling its own subordinate local agencies that they are barred from choosing <em>to</em> cooperate with federal immigration detainer requests.</p>

<p>When an attorney general issues a subpoena, it functions as a formal demand for documents, communications, or testimony in an ongoing investigation. Failing to comply with a state subpoena can lead to enforcement actions in court, potentially resulting in judicial orders compelling compliance or financial penalties. For resisting counties, the legal battle will likely turn on whether the state statute unconstitutionally infringes upon local administrative discretion or if the state is simply exercising its unquestioned authority over state-chartered local institutions.</p>
