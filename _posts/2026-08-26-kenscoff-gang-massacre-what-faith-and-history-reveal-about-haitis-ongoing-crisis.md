<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Kenscoff</h2>
<p>The numbers alone are staggering, but the human reality is far worse. Over the weekend, armed men affiliated with powerful criminal factions swept into Kenscoff, a once-peaceful mountain community nestled in the hills overlooking Port-au-Prince. According to United Nations reports, at least 47 people lost their lives—including five children and two government employees—while more than 50 others were taken hostage.</p>

<p>The attackers, linked to the formidable "Viv Ansanm" gang coalition designated by the United States as a foreign terrorist organization, also set fire to 25 homes, sending terrified residents fleeing into the rugged terrain. In a chilling digital threat, a gang leader identifying as Izo 2 warned the director of Haiti's national police, Vladimir Paraison, against deploying drones or tactical forces, declaring that any harm to gang members would result in the execution of all hostages.</p>

<p>Experts like Romain Le Cour of the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime point out that this violent escalation is calculated. By forcing the stretched police force and international security missions to disperse their limited personnel into remote areas like Kenscoff, criminal networks aim to weaken defenses elsewhere. More ominously, the attack serves as a brutal demonstration of power ahead of Haiti's scheduled general elections on December 13—the first in over a decade. It sends an unmistakable message to the government and its citizens: when gangs choose to strike, they can, and the state cannot reliably protect them.</p>
