    <h1 style="font-size: 2.4rem; font-weight: 800; color: #1a202c; line-height: 1.25; margin-bottom: 15px;">Maternal Mental Health Crisis: What the Lindsay Clancy Trial and Hayden Panettiere’s Death Reveal About Our Broken Healthcare System</h1>
    <p style="font-size: 1.2rem; color: #4a5568; font-weight: 400; margin: 0;">High-profile tragedies and ongoing courtroom battles have thrust maternal mental health into the mainstream. For millions of women, this long-overdue conversation exposes a medical system that treats mothers as an afterthought.</p>
  </header>

  <section id="ncg-intro" style="margin-bottom: 40px;">
    <p>When Adrienne Griffen gave birth to her second child back in 2001, she expected the routine exhaustion of early parenthood. Instead, she encountered a terrifying wall of severe postpartum depression that refused to lift. When she finally sought help from her doctor, the response was not compassionate intervention, but an abrupt warning about Child Protective Services and a chilling reference to Andrea Yates, the Texas mother who had recently drowned her five children. It took Griffen another grueling six months to find proper care.</p>
    
    <p>Twenty-five years later, conversations that once languished in hushed, lonely tones have finally broken into public consciousness. Yet, as experts and advocates point out, systemic neglect remains deeply entrenched. The ongoing trial of Lindsay Clancy—a Massachusetts labor and delivery nurse who says she strangled her three young children while experiencing severe postpartum psychosis—alongside the tragic death of actress Hayden Panettiere, has forced society to confront an uncomfortable truth: maternal mental health care in the United States is fundamentally broken, and the cost of this neglect is measured in lives.</p>
  </section>

    <p>To understand the current urgency, we must look at the numbers. While rare conditions like postpartum psychosis affect roughly 2 out of every 1,000 women after childbirth, broader mental health struggles—including debilitating anxiety and clinical depression—impact as many as 1 in 5 women during pregnancy and throughout the year following birth. These conditions are statistically more common than gestational diabetes, a well-monitored complication where standard screening protocols are strictly enforced across hospitals nationwide.</p>

    <h2 style="font-size: 1.8rem; font-weight: 700; color: #2d3748; margin-top: 30px; margin-bottom: 15px; border-bottom: 1px solid #edf2f7; padding-bottom: 8px;">How Should Readers Respond?</h2>
    <p>Awareness without action leaves the underlying crisis intact. If you or someone you know is navigating pregnancy or the postpartum period, consider taking these concrete steps:</p>
    <ul style="padding-left: 20px; color: #4a5568;">
      <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>Build the Village Before Birth:</strong> Establish a concrete postpartum plan that goes beyond nursery decor. Arrange for meal trains, scheduled check-ins, and trusted family members or professionals to assist during the first critical weeks.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>Screen Proactively and Openly:</strong> Ask obstetricians and pediatricians direct questions about mental health screenings at every single postnatal appointment. Do not wait for symptoms to become a crisis.</li>
      <li style="margin-bottom: 10px;"><strong>Combat the Stigma:</strong> Create safe, judgment-free spaces for mothers to express feelings of overwhelm, fatigue, or intrusive thoughts without fear of institutional retaliation or social alienation.</li>
    </ul>
    
    <p style="font-size: 0.9rem; color: #718096; margin-top: 15px; font-style: italic;">Note: This is general information, not medical advice. If you are experiencing a mental health crisis or having thoughts of self-harm, please contact your local emergency services or call/text the Suicide &amp; Crisis Lifeline at 988 immediately.</p>
