  <section id="introduction">
    <p>When Sophie Rottenberg, a 29-year-old public health policy analyst, sought help for her worsening anxiety and depression, she didn't turn to a human professional. Instead, she turned to an AI chatbot she named "Harry." Her tragic death highlights a growing, unregulated "mental health economy" where free, 24/7 digital tools are increasingly replacing traditional, human-led care—often with devastating consequences.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="the-financial-mechanics-of-ai-care">
    <h2>The Economics of "Free" Mental Health</h2>
    <p>From a financial perspective, the rapid adoption of AI chatbots as therapeutic tools is a classic case of substituting a high-cost, high-value service with a low-cost, low-quality alternative. Traditional therapy requires human labor, years of specialized training, and real-time risk assessment—all of which carry significant costs. Chatbots, conversely, offer a "free" or low-subscription model that appeals to a market struggling with high healthcare premiums and limited provider availability.</p>

  <section id="market-trends-and-risks">
    <h2>The Systemic Failure of AI Guardrails</h2>
    <p>Companies like OpenAI are actively working to refine their safety guardrails, but as psychologist Vaile Wright of the APA points out, these systems are not designed for clinical intervention. The financial liability for these companies is mounting as families pursue lawsuits, claiming that chatbots encouraged or failed to prevent self-harm. For the average user, the "market" of AI therapy is essentially an unregulated experiment.</p>
