  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Court</h2>
  <p>During the high-stakes federal trial, attorneys general from California, Colorado, Kentucky, and New Jersey grilled Mosseri over internal company metrics and design choices behind Instagram and Facebook. The prosecution presented evidence highlighting the "Take a Break" feature, introduced in 2021 to encourage users to pause their app usage after a set duration. Internal documents revealed a stark reality: at one point, just 1.8% of teenagers actually utilized the feature.</p>
  
  <p>Mosseri defended the company's efforts, noting that the notifications "helped, but not nearly as much as we hoped." While Meta eventually made the feature the default setting for all teen accounts in late 2024—after state lawsuits were already underway—attorneys pressed the Instagram chief on the fact that these low uptake rates were never publicly disclosed to parents. With potential penalties estimated as high as $1.4 trillion, the trial has drawn inevitable comparisons to landmark 1990s tobacco litigation.</p>

  <h2 id="psychological-perspective">The Psychology of Engagement and Design</h2>
  <p>From a behavioral psychology standpoint, the friction between user intent and platform design is entirely predictable. Human beings are wired to seek social validation, novelty, and connection—dopaminergic loops that infinite scrolls and algorithmic feeds exploit masterfully. When platforms rely on voluntary user restraint, such as opt-in break reminders, they run directly into well-documented cognitive biases like optimism bias and present bias.</p>

  <h3>Normalcy Bias and Corporate Blind Spots</h3>
  <p>Another fascinating psychological dynamic at play is normalcy bias—the tendency for organizations and individuals to underestimate the likelihood of a disaster or severe consequence because "things have always operated this way." Meta's internal communications revealed efforts by lawyers to limit the data shown to Mosseri to check "litigation exposure." This organizational self-censorship demonstrates how large corporations can insulate decision-makers from uncomfortable realities, allowing leadership to maintain an internal narrative of proactive safety while missing systemic harms.</p>

  <h3>The Illusion of Choice</h3>
  <p>When features like "Take a Break" or "Quiet Mode" are opt-in rather than structural defaults, the psychological burden of regulation is unfairly shifted onto the user—specifically developing brains that lack fully formed prefrontal cortices. Mosseri’s admission that low uptake numbers were kept under wraps points to a fear of losing engagement metrics. In behavioral terms, transparency reduces friction, but friction reduces engagement.</p>
