<section id="what-happened">
<p>When military analysts examined recent state media reports regarding North Korea's newest ballistic missile system, a quiet alarm bell went off not just in intelligence agencies, but in engineering offices around the world. According to reports circulating internationally, Pyongyang's latest weapon is credited with two headline-grabbing specifications: a devastating 2.5-ton warhead and a 900-kilometer operational range. To the casual observer, it sounds like a formidable weapon of terror designed to project power across the region. But to aerospace engineers and defense experts, those two numbers share a fundamental problem—they physically cannot both be true at the same time.</p>

<p>Ballistic missile design is governed by the brutal, unyielding laws of rocketry. In simple terms, adding weight to the nose cone of a missile requires exponentially more fuel, a larger airframe, and more powerful propulsion systems to achieve a specific distance. When a state boasts about an unusually heavy payload paired with a substantial range, aerospace specialists immediately start checking the math. As Euromaidan Press and other analytical outlets recently highlighted, experts are pointing out that these publicized figures likely represent worst-case political posturing rather than verified physical achievements. But why do these numbers clash so severely, and what does history tell us about how states exaggerate or misinterpret military hardware?</p>
</section>

<h2>A Historical Parallel: The Rigid Physics of the Cold War Arms Race</h2>
<p>To understand why North Korea's latest claims invite skepticism, we only need to look back at the mid-20th century during the height of the Cold War ballistic missile race between the United States and the Soviet Union. Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, both superpowers engaged in elaborate games of psychological deterrence, often exaggerating the capabilities of their strategic arsenals in military parades and public announcements.</p>

<p>During the development of early intermediate-range ballistic missiles (IRBMs), Soviet engineers faced the exact same thermodynamic and aerodynamic trade-offs that North Korean technicians face today. A heavy payload meant thick shielding, massive explosive mass, and tremendous kinetic inertia upon re-entry. To push a 2.5-ton payload over 900 kilometers, a rocket needs an immense amount of thrust. If the rocket is too small to carry the necessary propellant for that weight, the range drops dramatically. Conversely, if the missile is built light enough to reach 900 kilometers, its payload capacity shrinks to a fraction of a ton.</p>

<p>History shows us that propaganda and deterrence often rely on combining maximum theoretical numbers from two entirely different test configurations. A missile might achieve a 900-kilometer range, but only when carrying a light, standard-sized warhead. Alternatively, it might be able to lift a heavy 2.5-ton payload, but only on a much shorter, depressed trajectory. Presenting both maximums as a single unified capability is a classic psychological warfare tactic designed to create maximum ambiguity and anxiety among neighboring nations.</p>

<h2>The Danger of Misjudging Technical Limitations</h2>
<p>When analysts evaluate these weapons, underestimating a hostile state's technological capability is dangerous, but overestimating it can be equally destabilizing. Governments can inadvertently trigger unnecessary escalations by reacting to inflated performance metrics that do not actually exist in the physical arsenal.</p>
