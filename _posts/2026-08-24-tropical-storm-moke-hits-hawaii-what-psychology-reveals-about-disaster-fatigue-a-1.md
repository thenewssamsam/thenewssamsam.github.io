<section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
<h2>What Happened: The Double Blow to Hawaii</h2>
<p>Communities across Hawaii are bracing once again for severe weather as Tropical Storm Moke moves through the Pacific. According to the National Weather Service, Moke is impacting the Big Island and bringing heavy rain and gusty winds to neighboring islands through Monday. Forecasters warn that up to 15 inches of rain could fall across the Big Island, creating life-threatening flash floods and mudslides in areas already saturated by last week's storms.</p>

<p>This incoming system arrives less than a week after Hurricane Lala battered the region. Although Lala did not make direct landfall, it unleashed catastrophic rainfall exceeding 30 inches in certain spots, destroying infrastructure, washing out bridges, and lifting homes off their foundations. Tragically, at least one person—a 90-year-old woman—lost her life in the floodwaters. More than 8,000 residents remained without power over the weekend, with restoration efforts from local utilities expected to stretch into early September. Governor Josh Green has requested a federal emergency declaration, while local leaders like Hawaii County Mayor Kimo Alameda urge residents to prepare for another round of disruptions.</p>

<h3 id="emotional-depletion">Emotional Depletion and Chronic Crisis</h3>
<p>Psychologists use the term "disaster fatigue" to describe the emotional exhaustion that sets in when individuals experience continuous crises without adequate time to recover. During a single acute crisis, adrenaline surges, enabling people to act quickly, evacuate, or secure their homes. However, when a second disaster strikes before the cleanup of the first is complete, the nervous system remains stuck in a state of hyper-arousal.</p>

<h3 id="normalcy-bias">Navigating Normalcy Bias and Warning Fatigue</h3>
<p>Another psychological phenomenon visible in fast-moving weather events is "warning fatigue." When people are repeatedly warned about severe conditions—some of which may vary in local intensity—they can experience cognitive fatigue that dampens their sense of urgency. Some might unconsciously downplay the threat of Tropical Storm Moke simply because they are tired of being afraid.</p>
