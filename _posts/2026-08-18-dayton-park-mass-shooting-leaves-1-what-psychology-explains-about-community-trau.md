<section class="ncg-lens" id="psychology-perspective">
<h2>A Psychology Perspective: Why Public Violence Stuns Us</h2>
<p>From a psychological standpoint, incidents like the Dayton park shooting trigger specific, predictable human defense mechanisms. Understanding these patterns does not erase the pain, but it can help explain why we feel the way we do in the aftermath.</p>

<p>First, consider the concept of <strong>normalcy bias</strong>. Our brains operate on an assumption of safety; we expect our daily routines—like a walk in the park—to proceed without catastrophic interruption. When that assumption fails, the cognitive dissonance can be overwhelming. It forces our minds to rapidly recalibrate what we consider "safe."</p>

<p>Second, public spaces carry a shared social contract. When violence erupts in a park rather than a private setting, it shatters the illusion of communal sanctuary. People often experience secondary trauma simply by imagining themselves or their loved ones in that exact spot at that exact time. This proximity effect explains why a local tragedy can provoke widespread anxiety well beyond the immediate neighborhood.</p>

<div class="ncg-quote-box">
<p>"When a public park becomes the scene of sudden violence, our collective sense of safety takes a direct hit—reminding us how fragile our daily assumptions really are."</p>
</div>

<p>Psychologists also point to the phenomenon of hypervigilance following such events. Residents may find themselves feeling uneasy visiting routine public spaces, scanning crowds, or feeling an unwarranted sense of vulnerability. Recognizing these responses as a normal neurological reaction to abnormal events is the first step toward emotional recovery.</p>
</section>
