        <h2 style="color: #1a202c; border-bottom: 1px solid #e2e8f0; padding-bottom: 8px; margin-top: 30px;">What Happened: The Aftermath in Memphis</h2>
        <p>Severe storms tore through the Memphis and Shelby County area, leaving a massive trail of destruction in their wake. Memphis Light, Gas and Water (MLGW) President and CEO Doug McGowen reported during a press conference that this weather event ranks as the 7th most damaging storm in MLGW history. At its peak, the storm knocked out power to over 140,000 customers.</p>
        
        <p>While repair crews have made steady progress, tens of thousands of residents remain without power. Collierville, Germantown, and Memphis are all managing blocked roads, downed trees, and inoperable traffic signals. In response, Memphis-Shelby County Schools (MSCS) announced the closure of all schools and district offices, while Collierville Schools implemented a two-hour delay to ensure safety during the morning commute.</p>

        <p>When daily routines vanish in the span of a single storm, the human brain undergoes a predictable sequence of cognitive stress responses. Understanding these patterns can help explain why a prolonged power outage feels so uniquely draining—even when physical safety is maintained.</p>

        <h3 style="color: #2d3748; margin-top: 20px;">1. The Shock of Normative Disruption</h3>
        <p>Human beings rely heavily on predictability. Modern life trains us to expect flowing water, instant lighting, reliable refrigeration, and seamless connectivity. When a storm like the one hitting Memphis instantly strips these conveniences away, it triggers a mild form of cognitive dissonance. Our internal mental map of how the world should operate suddenly clashes with reality.</p>

        <h3 style="color: #2d3748; margin-top: 20px;">2. Normalcy Bias and Adaptation</h3>
        <p>Initially, many people experience normalcy bias—the tendency to underestimate the duration and severity of a crisis, assuming power will return within an hour or two. As hours stretch into days, the psychological weight shifts. Frustration builds as uncertainty lingers. However, human beings possess a remarkable capacity for adaptation. Once we accept a new (temporary) baseline—such as community center visits, ice chests, and altered school schedules—our stress levels often stabilize.</p>

        <h3 style="color: #2d3748; margin-top: 20px;">3. The Power of Shared Hardship</h3>
        <p>One of the fascinating psychological upsides of localized natural disasters is the "rally effect" or shared adversity bond. Neighbors who might normally wave politely across a fence suddenly share generators, clear fallen limbs together, and check on elderly residents. This social cohesion acts as a powerful buffer against anxiety, transforming a solitary annoyance into a shared community endeavor.</p>
