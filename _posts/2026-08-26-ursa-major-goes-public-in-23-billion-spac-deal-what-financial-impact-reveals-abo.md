    <h1 class="ncg-title">Ursa Major Goes Public in $2.3 Billion SPAC Deal: What Financial Impact Reveals About the Defense Industrial Base</h1>
    <p class="ncg-lead">Defense technology firm Ursa Major is planning to enter the public markets through a $2.3 billion SPAC merger, securing at least $350 million in fresh capital to scale up production of hypersonic missiles and solid rocket motors as the Pentagon pushes to overhaul a strained supply chain.</p>
  </header>

  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: A Major Defense Play Enters the Public Markets</h2>
    <p>Colorado-based defense technology company Ursa Major announced plans on August 25 to go public via a merger with Bleichroeder Acquisition Corp. III, a blank-check company. The transaction values the combined business at approximately $2.3 billion, featuring a pre-money equity valuation of roughly $1.6 billion. The deal is slated to close in the first quarter of 2027, subject to customary regulatory and shareholder approvals, after which the company expects to trade on Nasdaq.</p>

    <p>According to the companies involved, the transaction brings at least $350 million in committed financing to the table. This capital injection is designed to accelerate the manufacturing of missile propulsion systems, hypersonic vehicles, and spacecraft technology. About $110 million is being funded immediately upon the signing of the merger agreement, while the remainder is backed through a private investment in public equity (PIPE) led by Inflection Point Asset Management alongside existing investor XN and other institutional players. Additional funds could flow in depending on shareholder redemptions from the SPAC's $345 million July trust account.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="financial-mechanics" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>The Financial Mechanics: Scaling Up Capital-Intensive Manufacturing</h2>
    <p>From a financial perspective, Ursa Major's journey illustrates the heavy capital expenditures required to transition from a niche component supplier into a prime defense manufacturer. Founded in 2015 by former SpaceX and Blue Origin propulsion engineer Joe Laurienti, the company initially focused on supplying liquid rocket engines for commercial launch companies, allowing them to purchase propulsion rather than build it in-house.</p>

    <p>However, the economic realities of modern defense manufacturing demand massive physical infrastructure. Ursa Major plans to channel its fresh funding into turning its 400-acre Galeton, Colorado, solid rocket motor test site into a full-scale production campus. It is also expanding liquid-engine manufacturing, additive manufacturing capabilities, and development facilities for complete missile systems like the HAVOC medium-range hypersonic missile.</p>

    <h2>Market Dynamics: Why the Pentagon Is Pouring Capital into New Suppliers</h2>
    <p>To understand the valuation and the investor appetite behind this $2.3 billion deal, one must look at the structural pressures facing the U.S. defense industrial base. For decades, consolidation left the defense sector with very few prime contractors capable of manufacturing critical components like solid rocket motors and hypersonic propulsion systems. This bottleneck has created severe supply chain vulnerabilities.</p>

    <p>The Pentagon has actively pushed to introduce new sources and expand domestic manufacturing capacity. Ursa Major has directly capitalized on this policy shift. Beyond its liquid engines like the Hadley—which has powered Stratolaunch's Talon-A hypersonic test vehicles through 10 consecutive successful flights—and the Draper engine used in Air Force research demonstrators, the company has entered the highly consolidated solid rocket motor market. It is currently developing a second source for the Navy's MK 104 dual-thrust rocket motor.</p>
