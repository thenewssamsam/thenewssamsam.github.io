  <section id="historical-perspective">
    <h2>A Historical Perspective: The Geography of Chokepoints and Reluctant Allies</h2>
    <p>Throughout modern history, great powers engaged in protracted standoffs have routinely fixated on narrow maritime arteries. The Strait of Hormuz—through which a massive share of global energy supplies traditionally flows—acts today much like the Dardanelles or the English Channel did in past centuries: a geopolitical bottleneck where minor miscalculations cascade into strategic crises.</p>
    <p>When President Trump warns Oman for attempting to navigate or mediate between competing interests, he echoes a recurring historical dilemma. Nations caught geographically between sparring empires—often referred to in diplomatic history as "reluctant buffers"—find themselves squeezed between superpower demands and local survival imperatives. During the nineteenth-century European naval standoffs and twentieth-century Cold War proxy frictions, neutral states that tried to maintain independent diplomatic channels were frequently pressured by dominant maritime powers demanding absolute alignment.</p>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>“History reminds us that when superpowers demand total compliance from strategic neighbors, maritime chokepoints transform from trade routes into tripwires.”</p>
    </div>

    <p>However, historical comparisons also reveal clear limits. Unlike mid-century ideological blocs, contemporary supply chains are deeply integrated, and regional middle powers like Oman or the Kurdistan Regional Government operate with multi-directional diplomatic ties that previous generations of smaller states rarely possessed. Pressuring an intermediary rarely yields compliant silence; more often, it drives regional actors to hedge their bets further, complicating long-term coalition building.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="wider-reverberations">
    <h2>The Wider Reverberations: From the Red Sea to Regional Proxies</h2>
    <p>The expiration of the Iran deadline is not occurring in a vacuum. Shipping data from MarineTraffic highlights that commercial vessel movements through both the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb strait remain severely depressed compared to pre-war averages. CENTCOM forces report dozens of commercial vessels redirected or boarded, while electronic interference such as GPS spoofing continues to plague civilian mariners navigating these contested waters.</p>
    <p>Beyond maritime logistics, the conflict's ripples touch land borders with equal volatility. The drone attack reported by KRG Prime Minister Masrour Barzani in Erbil—and Tehran's swift rejection paired with warnings of potential "false flag" operations—illustrates how decentralized conflicts spawn numerous secondary friction points. When official diplomatic channels narrow, intelligence agencies and local militias fill the vacuum, increasing the risk of unintended escalation that neither Washington nor Tehran originally intended.</p>
  </section>
