<p>When a severe storm system rolls through a region, the immediate aftermath is usually measured in downed branches and flickering lights. But for the residents of Gary, Indiana, the August 11 storms transformed a weather event into a twelve-day endurance test. While surrounding areas saw power restored within hours or days, chunks of this majority-Black rust-belt city remained completely in the dark, exposing deep-seated vulnerabilities that weather reports alone cannot capture.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Gary?</h2>

<p>On August 11, a powerful storm slammed northern Indiana, knocking out power to more than 370,000 customers served by the Northern Indiana Public Service Co. (NIPSCO). While utility crews steadily worked through the grid, the timeline stretched past breaking point for Gary, one of the state's poorest cities. Twelve days later, thousands of residents were still living without electricity, running refrigerators, or dependable water pumps.</p>

<p>The prolonged darkness drew sharp rebukes from local leadership. Gary Mayor Eddie Melton called the pace of restoration "unacceptable," noting the severe toll on families, children, and local businesses trying to recover lost revenue. In response to the crisis, Indiana Governor Mike Braun directed the state consumer advocate to petition for a formal investigation into NIPSCO's emergency preparedness, vegetation management, and the actual deployment of funds meant for grid reliability.</p>

<h2 id="structural-disinvestment">Decades of Disinvestment Meet Extreme Weather</h2>

<p>The crisis in Gary cannot be blamed solely on wind speeds or fallen branches. For decades, the city has wrestled with the fallout of white flight, shrinking industrial jobs, and the steady closure of steel factories. This long-term economic contraction left municipal budgets stretched thin and infrastructure aging without adequate modernization.</p>
