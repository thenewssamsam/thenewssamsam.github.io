<h2>Tariffs, Manufacturing, and the Cost of Doing Business</h2>
<p>At the heart of the current policy debate are aggressive tariffs and stricter immigration enforcement. While proponents argue these measures protect domestic industries, the practical financial mechanics often tell a more complicated story.</p>

<p>In the automotive sector, for instance, high duties on imported steel, aluminum, and vehicle components have driven up manufacturing costs. With the average price of a new car now exceeding $50,000, domestic sales have slipped from pre-pandemic highs of around 17 million vehicles annually to roughly 16 million. Rather than triggering a massive reshoring boom, some manufacturers have adapted by expanding production facilities abroad—such as General Motors doubling down on investments in South Korea to build smaller, more affordable SUVs for the American market.</p>

<h2>Financial Mechanics: Who Pays and Who Profits?</h2>
<p>From a purely financial literacy standpoint, tariffs operate essentially as a tax paid by importing companies and passed along to domestic consumers in the form of higher prices. When duties are levied on intermediate goods—the raw materials and components that American factories need to build finished products—margins shrink, and businesses are forced to choose between absorbing the loss or raising retail prices.</p>
