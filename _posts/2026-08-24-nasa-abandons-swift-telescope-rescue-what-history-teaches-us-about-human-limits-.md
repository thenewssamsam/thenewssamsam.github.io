  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened to the Swift Rescue Mission?</h2>
  
  <p>Launched way back in 2004, NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory was originally slated for a quick two-year mission to study gamma-ray bursts. Instead, it turned into an astronomical workhorse, operating for over two decades as a general-purpose multi-wavelength observatory. But nothing lasts forever in low Earth orbit. Recent surges in solar activity heated up our upper atmosphere, causing it to swell and drag on satellites, dragging Swift's orbit lower and lower.</p>

  <p>Enter Katalyst Space. In September 2025, Katalyst secured a $30 million contract from NASA for a first-of-its-kind rescue mission. They launched the Link spacecraft on July 3rd, 2026, hoping to rendezvous with Swift, latch on, and physically push it up to a safer altitude. Unfortunately, by the end of July, the Link spacecraft hit its own persistent attitude control snags. Without the ability to stabilize and execute the maneuver safely, NASA and Katalyst pulled the plug.</p>

  <p>Instead of a rescue, Link will now perform flyby data collection exercises. Swift itself is expected to re-enter the atmosphere and burn up later this year. "We knew this was a high-risk, high-reward mission," noted NASA astrophysics director Shawn Domagal-Goldman, acknowledging that while the outcome stings, the pioneering attempt still generated valuable engineering data for future orbital operations.</p>
