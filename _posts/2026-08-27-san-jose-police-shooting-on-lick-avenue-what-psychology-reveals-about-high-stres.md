<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened on Lick Avenue</h2>
<p>According to San Jose Police Chief Paul Joseph, the incident unfolded after officers tracked an armed robbery suspect—later identified as Anthony Chavez of Campbell—who had fled police, crashed a stolen vehicle, and ducked into an occupied home near Lick Avenue and Goodyear Street on Tuesday afternoon.</p>
<p>Fearing for the safety of any residents who might be trapped inside with a violent, armed individual, members of the department&apos;s specialized MERGE tactical unit executed a crisis entry. Unbeknownst to the officers, the family inside was sheltering in a bathroom, entirely unaware that a criminal had hidden himself in their home. As Sergeant Matt Walsh, a 19-year-old veteran and supervisor, led the entry team into that bathroom, Chavez opened fire. Sgt. Walsh was struck twice in areas not covered by his protective vest. Officers returned fire, killing Chavez at the scene.</p>
<p>Chief Joseph, District Attorney Jeff Rosen, and Mayor Matt Mahan later voiced deep frustration during a press conference regarding Chavez&apos;s prior criminal history and his release on supervised release (SORP) despite facing new charges while on parole. While Sgt. Walsh recovers in stable condition and the family remains physically unharmed, the community is left grappling with the chaotic realities of urban policing.</p>

<p>For the family hiding in the bathroom, they were experiencing a textbook case of disrupted reality. Normalcy bias is our brain&apos;s tendency to underestimate the possibility of disaster even when warning signs flash. Families do not expect an armed fleeing suspect to burst into their private sanctuary. When danger strikes so intimately, cognitive overload sets in, freezing normal decision-making pathways.</p>

<h3>Tunnel Vision and Hyper-Arousal Under Fire</h3>
<p>For officers like Sergeant Matt Walsh stepping through that threshold, the human brain undergoes drastic physiological changes. Under extreme life-or-death stress, the sympathetic nervous system floods the body with adrenaline. This causes auditory exclusion, tunnel vision, and a massive drain on fine motor skills. In fractions of a second, an officer must process ambiguous visual data, differentiate threats, and protect innocent lives. The psychological burden of wearing a badge in an environment where 15 officers have been shot or shot at in just five years creates a constant, grinding baseline of hyper-vigilance.</p>
