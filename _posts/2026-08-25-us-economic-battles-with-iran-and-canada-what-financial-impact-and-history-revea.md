<section>
  <h2 id="financial-impact">The Financial Mechanics: Who Really Pays for Tariffs and Sanctions?</h2>
  <p>To the average observer, a tariff on Canadian goods sounds like a tax paid by Canada. However, standard economic principles dictate otherwise. Tariffs are taxes levied on imported goods entering a country, typically paid by domestic importers—meaning American businesses—who often pass those increased costs down to American consumers.</p>

  <p>When the cost of Canadian lumber, steel, or consumer goods rises due to tariffs, domestic industries that rely on those raw materials face compressed profit margins. To stay afloat, they raise prices on finished goods. This contributes directly to inflationary pressures right at home. It is a classic economic chain reaction where protectionist policies designed to protect domestic industries can inadvertently increase the cost of doing business across multiple sectors.</p>

  <p>Sanctions against Iran operate differently, focusing primarily on financial exclusion and embargoes rather than direct border taxes. Yet, their financial impact is equally tangible. By restricting Iran's ability to export oil and participate in global banking systems, sanctions can tighten global energy supplies. Even if U.S. direct reliance on Iranian oil is low, global oil is priced on a unified market. Any tightening of supply can drive up crude oil prices globally, pushing up fuel costs at the pump for drivers everywhere.</p>
