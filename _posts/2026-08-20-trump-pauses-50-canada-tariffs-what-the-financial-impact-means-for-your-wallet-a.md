<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Three-Day Tariff Pause and Deal</h2>

<p>The trade drama reached a fever pitch just before midnight on Tuesday. President Trump took to Truth Social to announce that the looming 50% levies on select Canadian goods were being put on hold for three days, “based on the fact that Canada and the U.S.A., subject to the finalization of documents, have a DEAL!”</p>

<p>The announcement followed intense trade talks between Trump and Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney. The proposed 50% duties—invoked under the rarely used Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930—were originally designed to target approximately $20 billion in Canadian imports. Administration officials framed the move as a response to alleged trade discrimination against U.S. producers in the motor vehicle, alcohol, and dairy sectors. Alongside the tariff pause, Trump floated the possibility that the Keystone XL pipeline project, halted in 2021, could be revived.</p>

<h2 id="financial-mechanics">The Financial Mechanics: Who Pays for Tariffs?</h2>

<p>To understand the real financial impact of this near-miss, we have to look past the political rhetoric and examine how tariffs actually work in practice. A common misconception is that a tariff is a tax paid directly by the exporting country. In reality, tariffs are import taxes levied by the U.S. government and paid directly by the American importer of record—the domestic distributor, wholesaler, or retailer bringing the goods across the border.</p>
