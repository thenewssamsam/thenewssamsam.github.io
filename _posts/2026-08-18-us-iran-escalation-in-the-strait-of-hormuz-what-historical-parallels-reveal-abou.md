  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the U.S.-Iran Standoff?</h2>
    <p>Following weeks of heavy bombing and daily military exchanges, the United States has transitioned its strategy from direct aerial bombardment to a naval blockade aimed at forcing Iran to reopen the vital Strait of Hormuz. For global energy markets and international shipping, this narrow waterway is an economic jugular vein. The U.S. blockade is designed to exert maximum economic pressure on Tehran, preventing vital trade and compelling compliance with ceasefire terms.</p>
    
    <p>However, rather than capitulating under economic strain, Tehran has signaled a shift to a "fully offensive" posture. According to recent intelligence reports and statements from Iranian officials, the regime has reorganized its military strategy, deployed commanders to regional proxies, and prepared contingency measures—ranging from regional sabotage to challenging the U.S. naval presence directly. At the same time, logistical strains have emerged on the American side, including depleted interceptor stockpiles and fatigued naval carrier groups operating at historic lengths of deployment.</p>

  <section id="historical-parallel">
    <h2>A Historical Parallel: Blockades, Attrition, and Escalation</h2>
    <p>History shows that nations rarely accept a maritime blockade quietly, nor do economic blockades remain purely economic for long. When great powers attempt to throttle trade to force political compliance, the target nation almost invariably seeks asymmetric means to break the circle.</p>

    <div class="ncg-lens-box">
      <h3>The Historical Lens: The Anatomy of Maritime Standoffs</h3>
      <p>From the continental economic blockades of the 19th century to energy chokepoint disputes in the 20th century, history demonstrates three recurring patterns:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>The Asymmetric Response:</strong> A nation facing superior conventional naval power rarely meets the blockade head-on with equal tonnage. Instead, it relies on irregular warfare, proxy maneuvers, and missile developments designed to exploit the blockader's extended supply lines.</li>
        <li><strong>The Salami-Slicing Trap:</strong> Incremental escalation—small, calculated provocations rather than a single all-out assault—often defines the initial phase of pushback. Both sides test each other's resolve millimeter by millimeter.</li>
        <li><strong>The Economic Threshold:</strong> As business and trade leaders in the targeted nation note, an economic blockade can eventually inflict more systemic damage than outright combat, creating intense domestic pressure to break the deadlock by any means necessary.</li>
      </ul>
      <p><em>Historical Context Note:</em> While no two conflicts share identical geopolitical landscapes, the dynamic where an economic squeeze forces an adversary to gamble on escalation is a classic trap documented across numerous maritime conflicts.</p>
    </div>

    <p>The current standoff in the Persian Gulf mirrors these historical friction points. With Iran's conventional forces battered by earlier airstrikes, Tehran is leaning into asymmetric capabilities—such as advanced missile technology designed to evade air defenses and regional proxy networks—to test whether Washington's political and logistical endurance will crack first.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="economic-impact">
    <h2>The Pressure Cooker of Economic Warfare</h2>
    <p>Economic warfare is deceptively clean on paper, but grueling in practice. The underlying logic of a naval blockade is that financial suffocation will yield political capitulation faster and with fewer casualties than a full-scale ground war.</p>
    
    <p>Yet, as officials within Iran's commercial sector have pointed out, the severity of a blockade cuts both ways. When trade routes are severed and energy supplies are choked, the economic shockwaves ripple far beyond the immediate combat zone, threatening global markets, inflation stability, and consumer prices. At the same time, the blockading nation faces its own form of attrition: maintaining carrier strike groups at sea indefinitely strains maintenance schedules, supply chains, and military personnel readiness.</p>

    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>"History teaches us that economic blockades are rarely static solutions; they are pressure cookers that invariably force a desperate adversary to test the limits of great-power resolve."</p>
    </div>
