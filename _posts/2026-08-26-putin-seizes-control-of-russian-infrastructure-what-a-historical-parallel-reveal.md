<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Decree Behind the Drone Strikes</h2>
<p>When a government begins nationalizing or temporarily seizing private property during wartime, it is rarely just about logistics—it is about survival and absolute control. Russian President Vladimir Putin recently signed a sweeping decree granting the state the power to temporarily take administrative control of "critical infrastructure" across the country if private owners fail to adequately protect facilities from escalating Ukrainian attacks. This sudden move arrives as the war deepens inside Russian borders, with long-range drone strikes hitting vital fuel networks, e-commerce supply hubs like Ozon, and transport centers.</p>

<p>The decree, which came into force immediately, empowers federal authorities to step in whenever private companies fall short on "anti-drone security" or fail to restore damaged operations fast enough. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov explicitly pointed the finger at private owners for not paying proper attention to security measures in the face of mounting Western-backed Ukrainian air campaigns. As fuel shortages pinch ordinary Russian citizens and the economic cost of the conflict strikes closer to home, the boundary between private commerce and state power in Moscow is evaporating.</p>

Whenever the periphery of a warring nation's economy is successfully targeted by an adversary, central governments almost invariably reach for the levers of emergency mobilization and command control. We can observe distinct parallels in past conflicts where the sheer friction of total war forced decentralized markets into rigid state oversight.</p>

<h3>The 20th Century Blueprint of Total Mobilization</h3>
<p>Consider the economic shifts observed during major geopolitical struggles of the 20th century. During periods like World War I and World War II, belligerent nations—ranging from the British Empire to the Soviet Union and Weimar-era precursors—faced acute supply disruptions, factory bombings, and fuel bottlenecks. Private enterprises were routinely told that profitability was secondary to survival. When private industrialists failed to secure critical rail lines, energy grids, or manufacturing plants against enemy bombardment, governments did not negotiate; they implemented state administration.</p>

<h3>The Illusion of Absolute Security Through Control</h3>
<p>Another striking parallel across history is the justification used by leaders during these transitions. Blaming private actors for security shortcomings—as Dmitry Peskov did regarding anti-drone defenses—serves a dual political purpose. It provides an immediate scapegoat for military vulnerabilities while justifying an expansion of executive authority that outlives the immediate crisis. Autocracies under pressure frequently discover that external threats provide a convenient mechanism for domestic consolidation, turning military setbacks into opportunities to eliminate economic independence.</p>
