  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened at Walmart?</h2>
    <p>When the nation's largest retailer stumbles, the broader economy pays attention. Walmart recently announced that its U.S. comparable store sales grew by just 2.6% during its most recent quarter—a sharp decline from the 4.1% growth seen just a few months prior, and its slowest pace in six years. Wall Street reacted swiftly, sending company shares tumbling more than 8% and dragging the broader U.S. stock market down with it.</p>
    
    <p>While Walmart still topped overall revenue and profit expectations—boosted partly by a $2.9 billion tariff refund resulting from a Supreme Court ruling—the underlying details of the earnings report painted a cautious picture. Low-income shoppers are clearly tightening their purse strings, while rising energy costs and inflationary pressures continue to squeeze both corporate profit margins and family grocery budgets.</p>

  <section id="financial-impact">
    <h2>The Financial Impact: Breaking Down the Numbers</h2>
    <p>To understand the true financial impact of Walmart's earnings report, we have to look past the headline revenue figure of $187.94 billion and examine the mechanical pressures at play. Comparable sales, which measure performance at established stores and associated e-commerce operations, slowed significantly. Furthermore, pharmacy sales took a hit due to federal legislation requiring capped prices on certain high-cost Medicare drugs.</p>
    
    <p>At the same time, the macroeconomic environment is imposing heavy operational costs. Walmart expects to absorb an additional $2 billion in incremental fuel costs this year alone, driven largely by geopolitical tensions and conflicts in energy-producing regions like Iran. To combat this margin compression and help struggling consumers, the retailer is actively deploying its $2.9 billion tariff refund to temporarily lower prices on 11,000 items, particularly groceries and everyday general merchandise.</p>
