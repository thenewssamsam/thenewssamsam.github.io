  <p>After a grueling two-week blackout caused by a historic derecho packing winds in excess of 100 miles per hour, electricity has been fully restored to the vast majority of northwest Indiana. For the 375,000 customers who suddenly found themselves plunged into darkness on August 11, the announcement from Northern Indiana Public Service Company (NIPSCO) brings a profound sense of relief—yet it also leaves behind a complex aftermath of community exhaustion, political friction, and hard questions about modern infrastructure vulnerability.</p>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The 100 MPH Derecho and Its Aftermath</h2>
  <p>On August 11, a destructive weather system tore through northwest Indiana, knocking out power to roughly 80% of NIPSCO’s customer base. The sheer scale of the devastation forced a massive mobilization. More than 4,000 internal and contract workers deployed from 14 states, working around the clock alongside local first responders, law enforcement, and even the Indiana National Guard to clear debris and rebuild shattered electrical grids.</p>
  
  <p>By Tuesday, NIPSCO announced that power had been restored to 99% of affected customers. The remaining outages, according to the utility, involve properties where privately owned electrical equipment sustained severe damage that requires independent repair before service can be safely reconnected. NIPSCO Chief Operating Officer and President Vince Parisi acknowledged the immense hardship inflicted on the region, calling it the worst damage the company has ever experienced.</p>

  <h2 id="psychology-perspective">The Psychology of Extended Crises: Normalcy Bias and Collective Fatigue</h2>
  <p>When a natural disaster strikes, human beings immediately grapple with a cognitive hurdle known as <em>normalcy bias</em>—our innate tendency to underestimate the likelihood of a catastrophe or assume that normalcy will return almost immediately. In the first 48 hours of a blackout, people rely on familiar routines, expecting lights to flicker back on any minute. But as days stretch into a second week, that psychological buffer shatters.</p>

  <p>Extended power outages tax human emotional resources through chronic uncertainty. Unlike a sudden storm whose physical danger passes quickly, a prolonged blackout creates low-grade, persistent stress. Refrigerators fail, food spoils, remote work grinds to a halt, and vulnerable family members face health risks from extreme summer heat. Psychological research shows that prolonged loss of control over one's immediate environment triggers irritability, helplessness, and acute community friction.</p>

  <h2 id="institutional-friction">Accountability, Monopolies, and Public Frustration</h2>
  <p>The psychological toll of the blackout quickly translated into political action. Indiana Governor Mike Braun voiced sharp criticism of NIPSCO’s corporate management, despite praising the boots-on-the-ground line workers. Pointing out that NIPSCO operates as a regional monopoly utility, Braun argued that Hoosiers pay monthly utility bills with the reasonable expectation of robust system maintenance and swift disaster response.</p>
