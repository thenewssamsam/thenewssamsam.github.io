<section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
<h2>What Happened: The Tragic Reality in Minneapolis</h2>
<p>Seven months after 37-year-old American citizen and Department of Veterans Affairs ICU nurse Alex Pretti was shot and killed by federal immigration officers in Minneapolis, his grieving family has broken their silence. In an exclusive interview with ABC News "Good Morning America" co-anchor George Stephanopoulos, Michael and Susan Pretti, alongside their daughter Micayla, voiced a painful and enduring demand: true accountability. Pretti was killed on January 24 during the Trump administration's "Operation Metro Surge" immigration crackdown, roughly two weeks after fellow U.S. citizen Renee Good suffered a similar fatal encounter with ICE officers.</p>

<p>Eyewitness video from the scene shows Pretti appearing to record immigration enforcement activity when an officer shoves a woman into him. As he attempts to intervene or protect himself from pepper spray, he is wrestled to the ground. Footage reveals that his legally permitted firearm was removed by an officer while he was pinned down, moments before approximately ten shots were fired—many striking him in the back, according to his mother. In the aftermath, high-ranking administration officials swiftly labeled Pretti a "domestic terrorist" and an "assassin," creating a fierce public relations battle that overshadowed the foundational facts of the shooting itself.</p>

<p>While the Department of Justice has opened a federal civil rights investigation, the Pretti family's legal representation, led by attorney Steve Schleicher, notes that communication from federal prosecutors has been sparse and unsatisfactory. The family's public plea is not merely about political rhetoric; it is a desperate search to understand why their son lost his life and who pulled the trigger.</p>

<h3 class="ncg-h3">The Mechanism of Defensive Framing and Attribution Bias</h3>
<p>In moments of institutional crisis, organizations and leaders frequently exhibit self-serving attribution bias. When an operation goes fatally wrong, the immediate psychological reflex—particularly within hierarchical, high-stakes environments—is threat preservation. Labeling an American citizen and healthcare worker an "assassin" or "domestic terrorist" before a thorough investigation has taken place serves a powerful psychological function for institutions under fire: it shifts the narrative from institutional accountability to preemptive self-defense. By reframing the victim as an aggressor, leaders attempt to activate the public's fear circuits, relying on threat-priming to reduce empathy for the victim.</p>

<h3 class="ncg-h3">Trauma, Shock, and the Agony of Media Exposure</h3>
<p>For Susan and Michael Pretti, discovering their son's death through televised news footage introduced a uniquely modern and brutal form of secondary trauma. Watching repeated broadcasts of a loved one's fatal moments fractures the natural grieving process. Psychologists note that visual trauma loops can severely impede psychological recovery, trapping families in a state of acute shock. When public officials compound this trauma by hurling labels like "agitator" without evidence, it creates a profound sense of institutional gaslighting—leaving the family to fight not only for legal justice, but for the basic dignity and memory of their child.</p>
