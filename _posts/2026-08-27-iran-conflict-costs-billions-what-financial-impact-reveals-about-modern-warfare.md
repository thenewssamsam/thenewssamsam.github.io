    <p style="font-size: 1.15rem; font-weight: 500; color: #222;">A staggering new report reveals that Iranian strikes have caused billions of dollars in damage to U.S. intelligence infrastructure in the Middle East, compounding tens of billions in direct military expenditures and sending global fuel prices soaring. As the conflict drags on into its sixth month, the financial ledger of modern high-tech warfare is revealing costs that stretch far beyond the initial battlefield estimates.</p>
    
    <p>When policymakers deliberate the initiation of armed conflict, the conversation frequently centers on immediate strategic objectives and tactical superiority. Yet, as recent disclosures regarding destroyed surveillance hardware and depleted precision-guided munitions stockpiles demonstrate, the true bottom line of protracted conflict involves complex supply chain strains, infrastructure replacement bottlenecks, and macroeconomic shockwaves that ripple directly down to the consumer level.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="ncg-what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 30px;">
    <h2 id="what-happened" style="font-size: 1.5rem; color: #1a1a1a; border-bottom: 1px solid #ddd; padding-bottom: 8px; margin-top: 30px;">What Happened: The Escalating Price Tag</h2>
    <p>According to a recent report citing multiple U.S. officials, Iranian drones and missiles have severely damaged intelligence posts and surveillance hardware across the Middle East. The destruction includes a CIA station in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, struck by drones in March, alongside a targeted facility in eastern Iraq. The total bill to repair or replace these critical intelligence assets is expected to reach into the billions.</p>
    
    <p>This intelligence infrastructure damage runs parallel to massive direct military expenditures. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth informed Congress that the conflict had already cost $37.5 billion, while requesting an additional $67 billion. The opening six days alone consumed more than $11.3 billion. Furthermore, reports indicate the U.S. military has expended nearly all of its stockpiled, long-range precision missiles, prompting internal debates within the administration over supply replenishment timelines.</p>
