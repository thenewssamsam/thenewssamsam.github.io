<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Case of Gao Zhen</h2>
<p>In a closed-door one-day trial that has drawn international condemnation, a court in Beijing sentenced 70-year-old New York-based sculptor Gao Zhen to three years in prison. His crime? Creating satirical artworks featuring former Chinese revolutionary leader Mao Zedong back in 2005 and 2009. Arrested during a visit to Beijing in August 2024, Gao was convicted under China's "Law on the Protection of Heroes and Martyrs"—legislation that did not even exist when his controversial pieces, such as "Mao's Guilt" and the "Miss Mao" caricature series, were originally produced.</p>

<p>Gao's arrest and subsequent maximum-term sentence have left his family devastated. His wife, Zhao Yaliang, and their US-citizen son have been barred from leaving China since his initial detention. Human rights organizations, including Amnesty International, have pointed out the chilling message this sends to artists and independent thinkers worldwide: that creative expression challenging official histories can be retroactively criminalized at any moment.</p>

<section id="the-psychology-of-censorship">
<h2>The Psychology of Censorship and Historical Memory</h2>
<p>From a psychological standpoint, why does a government feel threatened by bronze statues and caricature busts created nearly twenty years ago? To understand this reaction, we must look at how collective memory functions under authoritarian systems. Human societies rely heavily on shared narratives to maintain cohesion. When those narratives are built on unyielding perfection or infallible authority, any form of satire acts as an existential threat.</p>
