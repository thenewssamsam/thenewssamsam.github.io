<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The White House Granite Upgrade</h2>
<p>On August 19, 2026, President Trump led an impromptu tour to showcase his latest architectural augmentation to the executive mansion: a heavy-duty white California granite helipad designed to withstand 35,000 pounds of pressure per square inch. Sponsored in part by Sikorsky Aircraft—which manufactures Marine One—at an estimated cost of $5 million, the project replaces the traditional steel pads on the South Lawn, whose powerful engine blasts previously tore up the grass.</p>

<h3>The Roman Imperative of Permanent Materials</h3>
<p>Roman emperors famously shifted their building campaigns from brick and timber to marble and concrete to communicate the eternity of the state—encapsulated in Augustus's famous boast that he found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble. By embedding gold coins and carving seals into California granite that boasts a million-year projected lifespan, modern architectural additions tap into the same psychological impulse: linking a contemporary administration to an immutable sense of history.</p>

<h3>The Limits of Architectural Permanence</h3>
<p>However, historical parallels also remind us of the limits of physical infrastructure. The Roman Forum, the pyramids of Giza, and medieval castles all outlasted their builders, yet the regimes and political philosophies that constructed them eventually transformed or collapsed. Architecture can project permanence, but the true test of any governance lies in the civic institutions and cultural values upheld by the people, not simply the PSI rating of the stone beneath their feet.</p>
