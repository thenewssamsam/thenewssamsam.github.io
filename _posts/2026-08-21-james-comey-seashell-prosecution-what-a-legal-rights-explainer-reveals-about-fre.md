    <h2>A Legal Rights Perspective: Decoding the "True Threat" Doctrine</h2>
    <p>From a legal standpoint, the Comey prosecution forces the courts to grapple with a foundational First Amendment question: Where is the line between aggressive political rhetoric and a criminal threat? Under established US Supreme Court precedent, the First Amendment protects a vast amount of provocative, offensive, and even hostile speech. However, "true threats" are explicitly excluded from constitutional protection.</p>

    <h3>Defining a "True Threat" in Federal Court</h3>
    <p>A true threat is not protected speech because it inflicts the special harm of terrorizing individuals or inciting imminent lawless action. Courts evaluate whether a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a serious expression of an intent to inflict bodily harm. Prosecutors argue that "8647" operates as a clear, coded directive. Comey's lawyers counter that dictionaries, common idioms, and overall context show the phrase simply means to remove or eject politically, bearing no violent connotation in standard usage.</p>

    <h3>Selective Prosecution and Executive Animosity</h3>
    <p>Beyond the speech argument, Comey's legal team has raised the serious defense of "vindictive and selective prosecution." They argue that the indictment is the product of a years-long campaign by the president to utilize the criminal justice system to punish a political rival. Proving selective prosecution is notoriously difficult in American jurisprudence, requiring defendants to show clear discriminatory intent and effect. This legal battle highlights the delicate tension between executive accountability and the absolute necessity of maintaining an independent, non-partisan judiciary.</p>
