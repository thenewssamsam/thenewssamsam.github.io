    <h1>Artist Gao Zhen Jailed Over Mao Sculptures: What Historical Parallels Reveal About State Power</h1>
    <p class="ncg-lead">When 70-year-old artist Gao Zhen was sentenced to three years in prison in August 2026 for provocative sculptures of Mao Zedong created decades earlier, it marked another stark chapter in the long, troubled history of state control over artistic expression.</p>
  </header>

  <div class="ncg-content">
    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Gao Zhen Case?</h2>
    <p>Prominent Chinese artist and US permanent resident Gao Zhen has been sentenced to three years in prison after a Sanhe city court found him guilty of slandering China’s "heroes and martyrs." The sentencing comes exactly two years after Gao was detained on August 26, 2024, while visiting China with his wife and young son. His studio near Beijing was subsequently raided and sealed by authorities.</p>
    
    <p>The core of the prosecution centers on satirical artworks created by Gao and his brother, Gao Qiang, collectively known as the Gao brothers. Among their most famous pieces are sculptures depicting former leader Mao Zedong kneeling, and another showing a firing squad of seven Mao statues pointing rifles at Jesus. Crucially, human rights groups and international observers have pointed out that these controversial pieces were created more than ten years before China enacted the "heroes and martyrs" protection law in March 2018—a statute increasingly utilized by courts to penalize challenges to official historical narratives.</p>

Furthermore, Gao’s wife and son have been barred from leaving China on national security grounds, compounding the family's ordeal.</p>

    <h2 id="historical-parallels">Historical Parallels: When States Rewrite the Past</h2>
    <p>Throughout history, authoritarian regimes and centralized states have consistently viewed independent art and historical critique not merely as dissent, but as an existential threat. The prosecution of Gao Zhen under a retroactive interpretation of a 2018 law echoes classic historical patterns where political power seeks to codify an untouchable, sanitized official memory.</p>

    <h3 id="retrospective-justice">The Danger of Retrospective Justice</h3>
    <p>One striking historical parallel to Gao's case is the frequent use of ex post facto laws—legislation applied retroactively to criminalize acts that were legal when committed. Throughout history, monarchs and dictators alike have rewritten legal codes to punish critics for past expressions. By applying the "heroes and martyrs" law to sculptures made over a decade prior, the legal system serves less as an instrument of justice and more as an enforcement mechanism for political orthodoxy.</p>

    <h3 id="the-persecution-of-satire">Satire as a Dangerous Mirror</h3>
    <p>Art that lampoons powerful figures has always faced severe backlashes from ruling authorities. In Renaissance Europe and Soviet-era Russia, satirical depictions of leadership were routinely treated as treason or blasphemy. The Gao brothers' artwork—placing Mao in a posture of penance or confronting theological figures—strikes at the heart of state mythology. Totalitarian systems demand absolute reverence; satire introduces doubt, and doubt is the primary solvent of absolute authority.</p>
