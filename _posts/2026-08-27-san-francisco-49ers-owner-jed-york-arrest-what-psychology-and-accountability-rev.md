    <h2 id="psychology-of-power" class="ncg-h2">The Psychology of Power and Exceptionalism</h2>

    <p class="ncg-p">From a psychological perspective, high-status individuals often fall prey to cognitive biases such as the illusion of invulnerability or the "license to deviate" effect. When someone accumulates significant wealth, social status, or institutional authority, their brain subtly rewires how it perceives risk and societal rules.</p>

    <p class="ncg-p">Psychologists studying organizational behavior note that individuals at the top of powerful hierarchies can develop a profound empathy gap. They begin to assume that standard social contracts apply strictly to ordinary citizens, while their own exceptional circumstances grant them immunity. When a headline-grabbing incident like the Jed York arrest occurs, it serves as a jarring reminder that unchecked hubris eventually collides with reality.</p>

    <h2 id="accountability-crisis" class="ncg-h2">The Accountability Dilemma in Modern Sports</h2>

    <p class="ncg-p">Commissioner Goodell’s statement that the situation "is being reviewed" highlights a delicate dance familiar to major sports leagues. Commissioner offices exist to protect the brand, maintain competitive integrity, and project an image of strict neutrality. Yet, penalizing a billionaire franchise owner requires navigating a complex labyrinth of ownership bylaws, legal constraints, and public relations optics.</p>

    <p class="ncg-p">When ordinary employees face legal trouble, swift termination or discipline is standard. When owners face scrutiny, the machinery slows down for thorough legal reviews. This disparity often breeds public cynicism, reinforcing the belief that modern institutions practice a two-tiered system of justice. The unfolding review of York's arrest will ultimately test whether institutional watchdogs can hold their own power brokers to the same standards they demand of everyone else.</p>
