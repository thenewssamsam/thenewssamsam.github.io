<section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
<h2 class="ncg-h2">What Happened: The 35th Anniversary Without Washington</h2>
<p class="ncg-p">As Ukraine marked 35 years of independence from the Soviet Union on Monday, the streets of Kyiv bore witness to a somber yet defiant celebration. Foreign leaders flew in from across Europe to demonstrate solidarity, yet a glaring vacancy hung over the proceedings: the notable absence of senior representation from the United States administration. While U.S. lawmakers and former officials made the journey, the lack of top-tier executive participation underscores a profound shift in Washington's foreign policy posture during one of the most critical junctures of the ongoing conflict.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">Monday also marked four and a half years since President Vladimir Putin launched his large-scale invasion. Despite initial promises of swift diplomatic resolutions, negotiations remain stalled, supplies of vital U.S. air defense missiles are dwindling, and European allies are scrambling to close the defense gap. Secretary of State Marco Rubio offered formal congratulations via statement, but the physical absence of senior U.S. officials sends a powerful message about shifting priorities in Washington.</p>

<h2 class="ncg-h2">Historical Parallels: Alliances in Transition</h2>
<p class="ncg-p">When we examine this diplomatic pivot through a historical lens, familiar patterns emerge. Throughout modern history, major powers experiencing domestic fatigue or strategic realignments frequently step back from overseas commitments, leaving regional partners to shoulder the burden of collective security.</p>

<h3 class="ncg-h3">The Interwar Disengagement Parallel</h3>
<p class="ncg-p">The gradual retreat of a primary global backer mirrors historical shifts seen during past protracted conflicts. When primary patrons reduce direct involvement, smaller allied nations are forced to rapidly adapt, either by forging new bilateral coalitions—such as the recent European 'Coalition of the Willing'—or by negotiating under duress. History shows that when superpowers signal withdrawal, regional deterrence dynamics change overnight.</p>
