  <section id="introduction">
    <p>President Donald Trump recently turned the South Lawn of the White House into an impromptu construction tour, showcasing a new granite helipad and detailing his vision for a $400 million ballroom. While the immediate news cycle focuses on the logistics and controversy of the project, these efforts reflect a timeless political tradition: the desire to physically cement one's legacy into the foundation of the nation's capital.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened?</h2>
    <p>On Wednesday, President Trump hosted members of the press to inspect the ongoing construction of a new White House helipad. He praised the durability of the granite being used, contrasting it with traditional asphalt, and confirmed that work on the accompanying ballroom continues despite ongoing legal challenges regarding its funding and approval. The president emphasized the structural integrity of the materials, suggesting these additions will endure for generations.</p>

    <h2>A Historical Parallel: Architecture as Power</h2>
    <p>Throughout history, leaders have used major construction projects not merely for utility, but as a symbolic extension of their administration. From the Roman emperors who famously "found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble" to the grand urban redesigns of Napoleon III in Paris, architecture is a primary tool for projecting permanence.</p>

    <h3>The Weight of Permanence</h3>
    <p>Trump’s focus on the "million-year life" of his granite choices echoes the pharaonic ambition to create monuments that defy the passage of time. Historically, this strategy is a double-edged sword. When a project succeeds, it becomes a celebrated landmark; when it faces technical or political failure—as seen in the recent issues with the Lincoln Memorial’s Reflecting Pool—it can become a symbol of hubris or mismanagement.</p>

    <h3>The Tension of Approval</h3>
    <p>The current dispute over the ballroom's funding, which bypasses traditional congressional approval, finds historical echoes in the "imperial presidency" debates of the 20th century. Leaders often struggle with the balance between executive vision and legislative constraints. History suggests that while a president may win the battle to build, the ultimate judgment of the project rests on whether it serves the public good or merely the ego of the builder.</p>

    <p>Watching the president focus so intently on the durability of stone makes me reflect on where we choose to place our own efforts. The Bible reminds us that human structures, no matter how grand, are temporary. As we see in <em>Matthew 6:19-20</em>: "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven."</p>

    <p>There is a profound humility in recognizing that even the strongest granite eventually wears down. This doesn't mean we shouldn't build or steward our resources well, but it invites us to consider the legacy we are truly creating. Are we building things that serve others, or are we seeking a name for ourselves? The true, lasting legacy of any leader—or any person—is found in the kindness, justice, and truth they plant in the lives of those around them, rather than in the mortar of a wall.</p>
