  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p class="ncg-lead">For millions of Americans with disabilities, the right to live, work, and thrive in local communities rather than behind closed institutional doors is the cornerstone of modern civil rights. But a high-stakes legal challenge and a recent Department of Justice shift have thrown that decades-old promise into sudden uncertainty, leaving families wondering what comes next.</p>
    <p>Growing up near the Jersey Shore, siblings Cody and Kaleigh Brendle knew what it meant to navigate the world side by side. Cody, who is blind and has autism, and Kaleigh, who is also blind and recently headed to law school, are part of what experts call the "ADA generation." They came of age under the protections of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 and the landmark 1999 Supreme Court ruling known as the <em>Olmstead</em> decision. Yet, those foundational safeguards are now facing their most significant legal stress test in decades.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2 class="ncg-h2">What Happened: The Legal Battle Over Community Integration</h2>
    <p>The current controversy stems from a coordinated lawsuit brought by several Republican-led states, titled <em>Texas v. Kennedy</em>, which is currently making its way through the federal court system. The plaintiff states argue that the federal government lacks the statutory authority to mandate that states provide disability support services in integrated community settings rather than in institutional environments.</p>
    <p>In June, the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ)—the federal agency historically tasked with enforcing these civil rights protections—issued a legal memorandum aligning itself with the challenging states. The memo asserts that Congress never explicitly imposed an integration mandate on states through the original legislation, and that the historic <em>Olmstead</em> ruling merely held that states cannot institutionalize patients without justification, leaving what counts as "adequate justification" an open question.</p>

    <p>By July, the DOJ clarified that it would no longer enforce the <em>Olmstead</em> decision in the manner it had during past administrations. Legal scholars and policy experts have pointed out how striking this pivot is. More than 8 million Americans rely on Medicaid home- and community-based support systems to live ordinary lives—grocery shopping, holding jobs, and participating in family life. For these individuals, the federal government stepping back from enforcement is not an abstract bureaucratic adjustment; it is an existential shift.</p>

  <section id="legal-explainer" class="ncg-section">
    <h2 class="ncg-h2">A Legal Rights Explainer: Understanding Olmstead and the ADA</h2>
    <p>To understand why this legal pivot has alarmed advocates, it helps to examine what the law actually establishes. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) prohibited discrimination against individuals with disabilities in all areas of public life, including jobs, schools, and transportation. Nearly a decade later, the Supreme Court's 1999 ruling in <em>Olmstead v. L.C.</em> applied the ADA's integration mandate specifically to state-funded services.</p>

    <h3 class="ncg-h3">What the Integration Mandate Actually Requires</h3>
    <p>In practice, the mandate dictates that states must provide public support services—such as personal care assistants, job coaches, and accessible housing assistance—in the most integrated setting appropriate to the individual's needs. Historically, about 99% of Medicaid-funded disability services were provided in restrictive institutional settings like hospitals or large residential facilities. Today, thanks to <em>Olmstead</em>, more than 80% of those individuals receive support while living at home and participating directly in their local communities.</p>
