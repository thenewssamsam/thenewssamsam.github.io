Yet, psychological research into major life transitions suggests that monumental shifts rarely happen overnight. Instead, they are the result of gradual cognitive processing, changing family needs, and the slow erosion of initial conviction.</p>

    <h3>1. The Sunk Cost Fallacy vs. Psychological Flexibility</h3>
    <p>When someone makes a bold public stance—like stepping down from royal duties and moving across the ocean—they often face immense pressure to maintain that course indefinitely to prove they were right. This is driven by the <strong>sunk cost fallacy</strong>, where individuals persist in a behavior simply because they have already invested heavily in it. True psychological health, however, requires <strong>cognitive flexibility</strong>: the ability to re-evaluate circumstances when reality shifts, children grow older, and priorities evolve.</p>

    <p>In the immediate aftermath of a crisis or major falling out, <em>normalcy bias</em> can cause people to underestimate how much long-term geography matters. Moving to California offered space, privacy, and a fresh start. Yet, as children reach school age, the deep-seated human desire for extended family support systems, cultural roots, and familiar landscapes often reasserts itself. What felt like an absolute finality in 2020 naturally gave way to nuanced reconsideration by 2026.</p>
