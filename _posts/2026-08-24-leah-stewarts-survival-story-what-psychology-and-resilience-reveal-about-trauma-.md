<p>When a sudden, life-threatening crisis strikes, it shatters the quiet assumption that our daily routines are entirely predictable. For 35-year-old teacher and mother Leah Stewart, a routine morning swim at Sydney's Coogee Beach turned into a brutal confrontation with nature. More than two months after a white shark attack left her in a medically induced coma with severe injuries and the amputation of an arm, Ms Stewart has broken her silence. Her message isn't one of despair, but of profound gratitude and a determination not to waste a "second chance at life."</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened at Coogee Beach</h2>

<p>On June 13, Ms Stewart was enjoying a morning swim between the flags close to the shore at Coogee Beach in Sydney's east when she was mauled by a white shark. The attack was swift and devastating, resulting in severe bites to her arms and legs, multiple fractures, and massive blood loss. First responders and medical teams at St Vincent's Hospital fought to save her life during days in an induced coma and subsequent complex surgeries.</p>

<p>Following an outpouring of local and global support that raised more than $500,000 to aid her recuperation, Ms Stewart recently released her first public statement. Expressing deep appreciation for the generosity of the community, she announced the halting of further donations, stating that the funds had secured her family's immediate stability and future medical options. Most striking, however, was her psychological turnaround: deciding to "get better, not bitter."</p>

<h2 id="psychology-perspective">The Psychology of Trauma and Post-Traumatic Growth</h2>

<p>From a psychological perspective, surviving a catastrophic event like a shark attack triggers immediate physiological and emotional shock. When the human brain experiences acute trauma, the nervous system floods with stress hormones designed for immediate survival. Yet, the long-term journey—navigating physical rehabilitation, adjusting to permanent physical changes, and confronting the memory of the event—demands an entirely different mental framework.</p>

<p>Psychologists often study the phenomenon of <em>post-traumatic growth</em>, a concept recognizing that individuals can experience profound positive psychological transformation after surviving severe adversity. While trauma certainly causes immense pain, it can simultaneously reorder priorities, deepen relationships, and foster an intense appreciation for ordinary moments.</p>

<p>When Ms Stewart remarked that she wanted to "get better, not bitter," she highlighted a critical psychological crossroads.
