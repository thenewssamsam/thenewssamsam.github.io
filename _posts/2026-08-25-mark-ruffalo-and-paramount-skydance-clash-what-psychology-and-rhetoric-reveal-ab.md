    <h1>Mark Ruffalo and Paramount Skydance Clash: What Psychology and Rhetoric Reveal About Corporate Fights</h1>
    <p class="ncg-subtitle">When a high-profile Hollywood merger collides with geopolitical activism, the boundaries between policy criticism, corporate self-defense, and identity blur into a volatile public battleground.</p>
  </header>

  <section class="ncg-section">
    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened?</h2>
    <p>Actor Mark Ruffalo recently found himself at the center of a fierce public dispute after pushing back against the proposed $111 billion Paramount-Warner Bros. merger backed by Skydance Media. Ruffalo amplified a social media post from the Boycott, Divestment, and Sanctions (BDS) movement criticizing Oracle Executive Vice Chair Safra Catz over the tech giant's support for the Israeli military following the October 7, 2023, Hamas attacks. Ruffalo targeted Oracle co-founder Larry Ellison—whose son, David Ellison, is acquiring Paramount Global—arguing that the merger would consolidate dangerous surveillance technologies and threaten industry labor and editorial freedom.</p>

Prominent pro-Israel organizations, including the Simon Wiesenthal Center and the Anti-Defamation League, echoed Paramount's condemnation, praising the media giant for confronting prejudice directly. Ruffalo fired back on X, calling the antisemitism accusation "appalling and fundamentally dishonest," insisting his scrutiny was aimed squarely at military contracts, surveillance technology, and billionaire media consolidation rather than Jewish people.</p>

    <h3 id="groupthink-and-tribal-framing">1. Groupthink and Tribal Framing</h3>
    <p>Psychologically, humans naturally sort complex issues into binary categories: us versus them, ally versus adversary. When an influential figure like Ruffalo criticizes a business deal tied to prominent families, observers tend to view the conflict through an existing ideological lens. Institutions and advocacy groups often utilize tribal framing to consolidate support, ensuring that a critique of corporate power is swiftly interpreted as an attack on an entire identity group, or vice versa.</p>

    <h3 id="moral-licensing-and-emotional-escalation">2. Moral Licensing and High-Stakes Vocabulary</h3>
    <p>Words like "genocide," "apartheid," and "antisemitism" carry profound historical and emotional weight. When deployed in everyday corporate or political arguments, they trigger intense emotional responses. Psychologists note that using heavy moral language can serve as a form of rhetorical escalation, designed to immediately delegitimize an opponent's motives. For critics, these words describe what they view as systemic humanitarian crises; for targets and advocacy groups, invoking such terms in the context of a business merger crosses a line that trivializes real-world suffering and invokes ancient prejudice.</p>
