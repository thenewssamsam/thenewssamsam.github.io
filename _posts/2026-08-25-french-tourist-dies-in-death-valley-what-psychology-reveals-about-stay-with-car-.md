<section id="what-happened">
  <h2>What Happened in Death Valley</h2>
  <p>On a Monday afternoon in one of the planet's most unforgiving landscapes, Pierre Michel Formosa and a travel partner were driving near the Queen of Sheba Mine in Death Valley National Park. According to National Park officials, their vehicle became unexpectedly trapped in mud. With temperatures hovering around 113 degrees—well below the searing 130-degree peaks common in the park, but still lethal to an unshaded human body—the two men made a fateful choice.</p>
  
  <p>Instead of remaining with their vehicle, the pair left their car and began walking across the open salt flats toward Badwater Road in an effort to secure help. After walking just over a mile, Formosa could no longer continue. His companion pressed onward, eventually reaching Badwater Road where he flagged down a passing motorist. Authorities were alerted, and a California Highway Patrol helicopter was dispatched to search for Formosa. Search crews located him approximately 1.5 miles from the road, but he was pronounced dead at the scene.</p>
  
  <p>"This is a heartbreaking reminder of how quickly conditions here can turn dangerous," Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds said in a statement. "Death Valley is an extraordinary place, but its heat, distances, and remoteness can be dangerous, regardless of experience."</p>

<section id="psychology-perspective">
  <h2>The Psychology of Crisis: Why Do People Leave Their Cars?</h2>
  <p>Park rangers and survival experts repeat a golden rule with unwavering consistency: if your car breaks down in the desert, stay with it. A vehicle provides shade, acts as a visible landmark for rescue crews, and is far easier to spot from the air than a lone human walking across vast salt flats. Yet, time and again, stranded motorists abandon their cars.</p>

  <h3>Action Bias and the Urge to 'Do Something'</h3>
  <p>In high-stress situations, human psychology is heavily influenced by "action bias"—the deep-seated impulse to take physical action rather than remain passive, even when inaction is the objectively safer choice. Sitting inside a disabled car while temperatures rise feels agonizingly unproductive. The brain screams that waiting equals danger, pushing travelers to move toward safety, regardless of the odds.</p>

  <h3>Normalcy Bias and Underestimating Scale</h3>
  <p>Coupled with action bias is normalcy bias, the cognitive tendency to underestimate the severity of a threat because it defies everyday experience. To someone unfamiliar with extreme desert environments, a walk of "just a mile or two" to the nearest road sounds entirely manageable. They fail to internalize how rapidly dehydration, radiant heat from salt flats, and physical exertion can incapacitate the human body when temperatures exceed 110 degrees.</p>

  <h3>The Illusion of Proximity</h3>
  <p>In vast, flat, and featureless landscapes like Death Valley, distance perception becomes severely distorted. A road or landmark that appears deceptively close in the clear desert air can actually be miles away across brutal terrain. Once a person steps away from their vehicle, they immediately lose their primary shelter and shade, entering an environment where mistakes are measured in minutes rather than hours.</p>
