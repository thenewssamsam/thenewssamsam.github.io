    <h1>US-Iran MoU Expiration: What the Collapse of the Islamabad Agreement Ending Fighting Means for the Gulf</h1>
    <p class="na-lead-paragraph">
      As the June 2026 Memorandum of Understanding aimed at ending military hostilities between the United States and Iran formally expires on Monday, August 16, neither side is signaling a willingness to extend the pact. With mutual accusations of violations, stalled economic commitments, and escalating rhetoric over the Strait of Hormuz, the collapse of the Islamabad agreement leaves global energy markets facing renewed uncertainty.
    </p>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Expiration of the Islamabad MoU</h2>
    <p>
      The Memorandum of Understanding (MoU), brokered through high-level talks hosted by Pakistan and signed on June 17, 2026, by U.S. President Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, has reached its maximum 60-day window without securing a permanent treaty. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi confirmed in a Telegram post on Saturday, August 15, that Tehran had not made a decision to restart negotiations with Washington. Meanwhile, mediators from Qatar and Pakistan continue exploratory diplomatic exchanges, and Iran is reportedly pursuing a separate shipping arrangement with Oman in the Strait of Hormuz—conditional upon a broader political resolution. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      On the American side, President Trump told supporters in New York on Friday, August 14, that the U.S. would soon formally claim the critically important waterway as U.S. territory. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong>
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="background">
    <h2>Background: How the June Agreement Unraveled</h2>
    <p>
      The June 17 agreement followed more than three months of intense warfare that resulted in thousands of deaths and crippled commercial trade in the Persian Gulf, particularly global oil and gas exports. The deal temporarily bypassed complex structural disputes—such as Iran's nuclear program—to achieve an immediate halt to fighting in both Iran and Lebanon. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      Under the terms of the Islamabad MoU:
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li>Both nations declared an immediate, permanent termination of military operations, pending a UN Security Council-backed final deal within 60 days. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong></li>
      <li>The United States agreed to end its naval blockade within 30 days, lift sanctions, issue oil export waivers, unfreeze Iranian assets, and work toward a $300 billion reconstruction package. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong></li>
      <li>Iran agreed to clear mines from the Strait of Hormuz, permit toll-free commercial passage for 60 days, and reaffirm its commitment against pursuing nuclear weapons. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong></li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      However, implementation faltered almost immediately. Disputes arose over whether the document constituted a binding permanent ceasefire or a temporary cessation of hostilities. By June 27, President Trump labeled Iranian military strikes a violation of the deal, while Tehran accused Washington of breaching Article 1 through continued military actions. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      By July 7, President Trump declared the agreement "over," and subsequent U.S. military strikes targeted infrastructure inside Iran, resulting in at least 50 reported deaths. In response, Iranian officials stated that Tehran had suspended all reciprocal commitments. <strong>[Confirmed — Al Jazeera]</strong>
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="analysis">
    <h2>The Analysis: Why the Deal Collapsed and Who Holds Leverage</h2>
    <p>
      The breakdown of the MoU stems from fundamentally incompatible expectations and a lack of trust regarding sequential concessions. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      From Tehran's perspective, Washington failed to deliver on its foundational economic and security obligations—specifically failing to unfreeze financial assets, lift sanctions, withdraw naval forces, or secure an Israeli withdrawal from southern Lebanon. Concurrently, recent leadership changes within Iran's national security apparatus—such as appointing former Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) head Mohsen Rezaei to lead the Supreme National Security Council—indicate a hardening domestic stance. Tehran calculates that it possesses the regional leverage to withstand economic pressure and disrupt maritime trade. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
    </p>
    <p>
      Conversely, the Trump administration faces distinct strategic constraints. According to regional analysts like Barbara Slavin of the Stimson Centre, U.S. military options are increasingly limited due to depleted interceptor missile stocks, drained strategic petroleum reserves, and extended naval hardware deployments. With few military or economic pressure points left that haven't already been deployed, Washington's escalation—including Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's promises of unprecedented financial measures and Trump's rhetoric regarding territorial claims over the Strait of Hormuz—largely reflects diplomatic posturing ahead of upcoming domestic political cycles. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
    </p>
  </section>

  <section id="what-happens-next">
    <h2>What Happens Next: Scenarios for the Persian Gulf</h2>
    <p>
      With the 60-day window closed, regional dynamics could follow one of several paths over the coming months:
    </p>
    <ol>
      <li>
        <strong>Prolonged Maritime Standoff:</strong> Low-level friction in the Strait of Hormuz may persist without erupting into full-scale war, as both sides manage operational risks while maintaining hostile rhetoric. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Post-Midterm Diplomatic Reset:</strong> As suggested by policy analysts, substantive negotiations may remain frozen until after domestic electoral milestones in the United States reduce immediate political pressures on Washington. <strong>[Analysis]</strong>
      </li>
      <li>
        <strong>Alternative Regional Corridors:</strong> Iran may successfully negotiate secondary bilateral shipping frameworks—such as its discussions with Oman—bypassing direct U.S. oversight in regional trade routes. <strong>[Confirmed / Unconfirmed]</strong>
