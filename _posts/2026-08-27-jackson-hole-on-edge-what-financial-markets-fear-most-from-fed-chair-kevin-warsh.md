  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p>Global economic leaders, Wall Street strategists, and anxious investors are descending upon Jackson Hole, Wyoming, this week for the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City’s annual economic policy symposium. But this year’s gathering is far from a standard academic retreat. Markets arrive at the resort town deeply unsettled, caught in a high-stakes standoff between the U.S. Treasury and the Federal Reserve.</p>
    
    <p>With long-term borrowing costs sitting near a two-decade high and the U.S. Treasury executing a surprise intervention in the bond market, all eyes are locked on Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh. His upcoming keynote speech on Friday is being treated by major institutions like Bank of America as a definitive risk event for global equities, the U.S. dollar, and fixed-income assets.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="ncg-section" id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: The Bond Market Pressure Cooker</h2>
    <p>The tension in Jackson Hole follows a volatile week in Washington and New York. Last week, the yield on the 30-year Treasury note surged to a 19-year high, driven by persistent worries over inflation, massive sovereign financing needs, and the federal deficit. Stubborn price pressures refuse to recede quietly, with the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index rising 3.7% in the year to July.</p>

    <h2>A Financial Impact Perspective: The Mechanics of the Fed-Treasury Tug-of-War</h2>
    <p>To understand why Wall Street is sweating ahead of Friday, we have to look past the political headlines and examine the stark financial mechanics at play. When the Treasury steps in to buy back long-term debt, it attempts to manipulate the supply-and-demand balance in the fixed-income market to force yields down. Lower yields mean cheaper long-term borrowing for mortgages, corporate expansions, and government debt servicing. But this comes at a steep structural cost.</p>
