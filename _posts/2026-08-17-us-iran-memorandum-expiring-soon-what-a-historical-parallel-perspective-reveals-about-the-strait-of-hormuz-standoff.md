  <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom:32px;">
    <h2 style="font-size:1.5rem; border-bottom:2px solid #e9ecef; padding-bottom:6px; margin-top:0; color:#1a1a1a;">What Happened: The Expiring MOU</h2>
    <p>A framework agreement between the United States and Iran is slated to expire, marking a critical juncture as their ongoing conflict approaches the six-month mark. Signed back in June, the preliminary deal established a permanent end to active military action while launching a 60-day negotiating window to forge a permanent treaty regarding Iran's nuclear program.</p>
    <p>Yet, as the deadline approaches, the White House has remained tight-lipped on whether an extension is possible. Over the past two months, both governments have traded accusations of violating the accord. At the center of the diplomatic breakdown is a bitter feud over the Strait of Hormuz—the narrow, indispensable oil shipping route that remained fully open prior to the outbreak of hostilities but has since seen persistent vessel disruptions and exchanges of fire.</p>
    <p>Rhetoric has correspondingly heated up. During a Friday evening address, former President Donald Trump escalated tensions by declaring intentions to designate the Hormuz Strait as United States territory following what he characterized as Iran's imminent defeat. Tehran quickly pushed back. Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi posted on social media that the waterway cannot be seized by executive order, aircraft carrier, or speech, asserting that Iran neither cowers before displays of force nor fears threats.</p>
    <p>Meanwhile, the operational chess pieces continue to move. The U.S. Central Command recently released imagery of the USS George Washington shifting into the Middle East from the western Pacific, where it had previously served as a counterweight to Chinese military maneuvers. The carrier is slated to relieve the USS Abraham Lincoln following a record-length deployment that has drawn congressional scrutiny from Democrats demanding accountability regarding onboard living conditions.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="historical-parallel" style="margin-bottom:32px;">
    <h2 style="font-size:1.5rem; border-bottom:2px solid #e9ecef; padding-bottom:6px; margin-top:0; color:#1a1a1a;">Historical Parallel: The Fragility of Maritime Chokepoints</h2>
    <p>When policymakers argue over control of narrow maritime passages like the Strait of Hormuz, they are stepping onto ground that has repeatedly tested great powers throughout modern history. Whenever a critical global trade corridor faces military friction, the situation inevitably evokes memories of past chokepoint crises—most notably the 1956 Suez Crisis.</p>
    <p>At that time, the nationalization of the Suez Canal by Egypt triggered an international confrontation involving Britain, France, and Israel. The geopolitical logic of the era dictated that controlling vital waterways was synonymous with maintaining imperial and economic supremacy. However, the crisis ultimately demonstrated the severe limits of military force in resolving economic and territorial disputes over international transit lanes, proving that physical occupation of a chokepoint often generates far more instability, economic fallout, and diplomatic isolation than it solves.</p>
    
    <div style="background-color:#fdf6e2; border-left:4px solid #d97706; padding:16px; margin:24px 0; border-radius:0 8px 8px 0;">
      <p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:600; color:#92400e;">Historical Parallel Insight</p>
      <p style="margin:0; font-style:italic; color:#78350f;">History teaches us that declaring sovereignty over a global maritime artery is far easier in a speech than it is to enforce sustainably without triggering cascading economic and military consequences.</p>
    </div>

    <p>Another instructive parallel can be found in the Tanker War phase of the 1980s Iran-Iraq War, during which both belligerents targeted commercial oil tankers in the Persian Gulf, drawing direct U.S. naval intervention to protect shipping lanes. Back then, as today, naval deployments and aircraft carrier movements served as powerful signals of deterrence. Yet history shows that naval escorts and show-of-force operations rarely settle the underlying political hostility; instead, they manage symptoms while keeping the global economy on edge.</p>
  </section>

    <p>For observers watching headlines unfold from thousands of miles away, the expiration of a diplomatic memorandum can feel distant and abstract. Yet energy markets, supply chains, and international stability are inextricably linked to these narrow strips of water.</p>
    <p>When evaluating these developments, readers can cultivate grounded perspective through a few practical approaches:</p>

      <li style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Appreciate diplomatic complexity:</strong> Understand that framework agreements are fragile instruments; their expiration does not automatically mean total war, but it does mark a return to high-stakes uncertainty.</li>
