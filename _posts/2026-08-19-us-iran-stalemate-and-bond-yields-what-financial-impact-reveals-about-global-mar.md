<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened in the Markets?</h2>
<p>A sudden and synchronized sell-off of government bonds swept across global markets on Tuesday morning, pushing borrowing costs to multi-decade highs. The dramatic market shift arrived as hopes for a negotiated end to hostilities between the United States and Iran vanished.</p>
<p>On Monday, U.S. President Donald Trump firmly ruled out extending a ceasefire between Washington and Tehran, while Iranian officials issued warnings of renewed military escalation. Both nations have effectively slammed the door on further peace talks for the moment.</p>
<p>The situation took an even more alarming turn overnight when a cargo vessel was struck by a projectile while transiting the Strait of Hormuz. This vital maritime chokepoint has experienced an effective closure throughout the nearly six-month conflict, continuing to choke global trade and drive up the cost of energy and vital commodities. Global benchmark Brent crude oil futures responded swiftly, hovering firmly above the -a-barrel mark.</p>

<p>In fixed-income markets, the fallout was immediate and severe. Yields on U.S. 30-year Treasurys climbed nearly 3 basis points to 5.335%, marking their highest level since 2002. The 20-year Treasury note hit a post-2006 high, while the benchmark 10-year Treasury yield touched 4.748%, its highest mark since 2007. Selling pressure wasn't limited to the United States; Germany's 10-year bund reached a 15-year high, France saw its highest yields since 2008, and Japanese 10-year yields hit a 30-year high, mirroring spikes across British, Italian, Swiss, and Canadian sovereign debt.</p>

<p>When investors lose confidence in economic stability, they sell fixed-income assets, driving bond prices down and yields up (since yields and prices move inversely). Analysts point to a combination of persistent inflation fears and demanding risk premiums. Dan Coatsworth, head of markets at AJ Bell, noted that rising long-dated bond yields reflect not just expectations of higher interest rates, but also mounting anxiety over massive government borrowing levels and the higher compensation investors demand for holding long-term sovereign risk.</p>

<h2>A New Financial Giant: The AI Infrastructure Boom</h2>
<p>While geopolitical conflict provides the immediate spark, financial experts point to an unprecedented domestic and global structural shift compounding the bond market's pain: the massive capital requirements of artificial intelligence.</p>
<p>Speaking to CNBC, Carl Weinberg, founder of High Frequency Economics, highlighted that vast capital injections into AI infrastructure are competing directly with sovereign debt for the world's limited pool of savings. With estimates suggesting up to  billion borrowed over the past year—and another  billion in funding, debt issuance, and IPOs waiting in the wings—technology enterprises have joined governments as hyper-borrowers.</p>
