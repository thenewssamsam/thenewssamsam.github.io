    <h2>The Builder’s Impulse: A Historical Parallel</h2>
    <p>Throughout American history, the "builder-in-chief" is a persona many presidents have adopted. From Thomas Jefferson’s constant renovations at Monticello to the grand public works projects of the New Deal, leaders often feel a psychological pull to leave a physical mark on the landscape. This impulse is rarely just about utility; it is an attempt to create a tangible bridge to the future.</p>

    <h3>The Tension of Permanence</h3>
    <p>Historically, building projects during times of political instability often carry a dual message. When a leader faces low approval ratings or external conflict, large-scale construction can serve as a display of confidence and control. However, history also warns that physical structures are subject to the same decay as political policies. Just as the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool’s recent maintenance struggles highlight the fragility of "permanent" structures, the history of Washington is littered with ambitious projects that failed to outlive their creators' political influence.</p>
