  <section class="ncg-intro">
    <p>The art world has lost one of its most eccentric, beloved, and commercially successful visionaries. Yayoi Kusama, the legendary Japanese artist whose immersive "Infinity Mirror Rooms" and whimsical polka-dot installations captivated millions across the globe, has died in Tokyo at the age of 97.</p>
    <p>While her later years were defined by massive auction records, blockbuster museum retrospectives, and ubiquitous Instagram selfies, her path to global acclaim was anything but straightforward. Beneath the bright colors and playful pumpkins lay a lifelong battle with severe trauma, mental illness, and decades of marginalization in a male-dominated avant-garde art scene.</p>
  </section>

News of her death instantly reverberated through international cultural circles, triggering an outpouring of tributes from museums, fashion houses, and fans worldwide.</p>

    <h2 class="ncg-h2">A Psychology & Behavior Perspective: The Art of Survival</h2>
    <p>To truly understand Kusama’s art, one must look at the psychological crucible that forged it. Born in Matsumoto, Japan, in 1929, Kusama endured a deeply dysfunctional and abusive childhood. During World War II, she found herself working in a military factory, sewing parachutes for the imperial army. It was during these formative, traumatic years that she began experiencing severe visual and auditory hallucinations.</p>

Kusama once described her hallucinations as a sensation where her body, and the world around her, was being dissolved by repetitive patterns. The dots were a way to map, contain, and neutralize overwhelming psychological distress.</p>

    <p>Moving to New York in the late 1950s, Kusama plunged into the avant-garde movement, staging provocative "happenings" and challenging the male-dominated abstract expressionist establishment. Despite friendships with icons like Andy Warhol, she remained an outsider—a foreign woman of color navigating a Western-centric art world.</p>
