  <p class="ncg-lead">When Instagram head Adam Mosseri took the witness stand in a federal courtroom in Oakland, California, it brought a familiar face to an escalating legal battle over how social media apps capture and hold our attention. As state attorneys general press forward with a sweeping lawsuit accusing Meta of designing platforms to engage children and teens while allegedly concealing safety risks, the trial opens a wider window into the hidden behavioral mechanics of modern technology.</p>

  <div class="ncg-quick-take">
    <h3>Quick Take: The Core Story</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>What happened:</strong> Instagram chief Adam Mosseri testified in a high-profile federal trial in Oakland where a coalition of state attorneys general is suing Meta over youth social media addiction.</li>
      <li><strong>The defense:</strong> Mosseri denied hiding safety information from the public and disputed the idea that he encouraged teams to shield data from his view. Meta called the legal claims "unsubstantiated."</li>
      <li><strong>The broader stakes:</strong> The lawsuit follows earlier court battles regarding app design, highlighting growing scrutiny over how digital platforms shape human behavior, habit formation, and public health.</li>

  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Courtroom</h2>
  
  <p>The federal courtroom in Oakland hummed with tension as Instagram head Adam Mosseri stood before a bipartisan coalition of state attorneys general. The lawsuit, initially filed in October 2023 by nearly thirty states, targets Meta—parent company of Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp—with allegations that the tech giant intentionally engineered its platforms to foster compulsive use among children and teens.</p>

  <p>During roughly an hour of questioning on Tuesday, Colorado Assistant Attorney General Jason Slothouber pressed Mosseri on his public-facing role and internal communications. Slothouber inquired whether part of Mosseri's job was to "humanize" Instagram and whether company lawyers had ever recommended shielding internal research about app safety from executive oversight. Mosseri maintained that he encourages transparency from his teams.</p>

  <p>"I do think it's important and valuable for us to explain what we do to the public," Mosseri stated, adding that it would "surprise" him if legal teams routinely stripped sensitive data from his presentations. He emphasized that he consistently guides his teams to provide full context so they can make informed decisions.</p>

  <h2 id="psychology-perspective">A Psychology Perspective: The Architecture of Habit</h2>

  <p>To understand why trials like this capture public attention—and why they spark such fierce debate—we have to look beyond corporate boardrooms and into the realm of behavioral psychology. Human beings are fundamentally wired to seek connection, social validation, and immediate feedback. Social media platforms did not invent these desires; rather, they built extraordinarily sophisticated digital environments that tap directly into ancient motivational loops.</p>

  <p>When Mosseri previously testified in Los Angeles regarding teen platform use, he made a notable distinction between "clinical addiction" and "problematic use," describing the latter simply as spending too much time on an app. From a psychological standpoint, this distinction touches on a core feature of modern user experience design: variable reward schedules. When a user pulls to refresh a feed or checks for notifications, the unpredictable nature of the reward—sometimes a message from a close friend, sometimes mundane content—triggers dopamine pathways that reinforce habitual checking behaviors.</p>

Because smartphones and social media became ubiquitous almost overnight, humanity lacked the generational runway to develop instinctive social filters for constant connectivity. What feels like a routine evening scroll to an adult can easily become an overwhelming emotional anchor for a developing adolescent whose identity formation is deeply intertwined with peer approval.</p>
