    <h2>What Happened in Reno?</h2>
    <p>On Sunday, August 23, 2026, a human-caused blaze dubbed the Hawk fire transformed from a minor weekend concern into a raging inferno. Fueled by strong, gusty winds, low humidity, and bone-dry vegetation, the fire swept from the Sierra foothills directly toward the center of Reno.</p>
    <p>According to Truckee Meadows Fire and Rescue, nearly 90,000 residents found themselves within mandatory "GO NOW" evacuation zones or warning areas. The flames pushed right to the edge of the University of Nevada campus, threatening neighborhoods, cutting power to roughly 10,000 homes and businesses in Washoe County, and forcing the closure of major thoroughfares like US Route 395.</p>

    <h3>1. Normalcy Bias</h3>
    <p>Human brains are wired to expect tomorrow to look a lot like today. Normalcy bias causes people to underestimate the likelihood of disaster or its effects. When a small fire starts over the ridge, our cognitive baseline tells us, "That's someone else's problem" or "The fire department will handle it quickly, like always." This mental lag is precisely why evacuation orders often meet initial resistance.</p>

    <h3>2. Loss Aversion and Decision Paralysis</h3>
    <p>When told to evacuate immediately, people face an instant psychological tug-of-war. The pain of leaving behind a lifetime of possessions—as David Barb experienced upon returning to find his taxidermy shop leveled—triggers intense loss aversion. People freeze, trying to weigh what to pack, where to take pets, and whether the threat is truly as severe as authorities claim.</p>
