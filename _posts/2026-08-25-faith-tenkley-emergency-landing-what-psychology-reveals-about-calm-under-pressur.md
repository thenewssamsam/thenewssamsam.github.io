    <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened Off the Boca Raton Coast</h2>
    <p>According to local reports, Faith Tenkley had departed Page Field in Fort Myers on Saturday morning, bound for Bimini in the Bahamas to pick up her parents. Roughly 15 miles west of Boca Raton, her aircraft began experiencing severe mechanical issues. Initially planning to divert to the local airport, Tenkley quickly realized the landing gear was malfunctioning. Fearing that a hard runway touchdown might ignite the recently refueled aircraft, and compounded by the sudden failure of her right engine, she made the split-second decision to ditch the plane in the ocean.</p>
    
    <p>Video captured by beachgoers showed the aircraft making a controlled, slow descent over a populated beach before touching down delicately on the water. Within a minute, a local lifeguard reached Tenkley, followed closely by Boca Raton Ocean Rescue. Tenkley was later evaluated at a hospital and reported zero injuries. Remarkably, amidst viral social media speculation misidentifying her as a male pilot rescued by a surfer, Tenkley quickly stepped forward to clarify the details, noting she had simply removed her long skirt over her bikini to swim ashore.</p>

    <h2 id="psychology-perspective">The Psychology of Crisis: Why Some Stay Calm</h2>
    <p>From a psychological standpoint, Tenkley's ability to navigate a failing aircraft away from crowded land zones while processing multiple simultaneous failures is a textbook example of cognitive control under extreme stress. When human beings encounter sudden, catastrophic danger, the sympathetic nervous system triggers an immediate fight-or-flight response. Heart rates skyrocket, blood rushes away from the prefrontal cortex—the seat of complex problem-solving—and adrenaline floods the body.</p>
