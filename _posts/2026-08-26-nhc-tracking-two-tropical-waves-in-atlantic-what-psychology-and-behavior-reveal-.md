  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in the Atlantic</h2>
  <p>According to the latest updates from the National Hurricane Center (NHC), meteorologists are tracking two distinct systems moving across the Atlantic basin. The first disturbance, designated Invest 95L, is currently positioned several hundred miles northeast of Bermuda. Moving northeast at roughly 20 to 25 miles per hour, it has a high chance of organizing into a short-lived tropical depression or storm before encountering hostile environmental conditions by Wednesday.</p>
  
  <p>Further out in the eastern Atlantic, a second disturbance—Invest 96L—is located southeast of the Cabo Verde Islands off the coast of Africa. The NHC gives this system a strong 70 percent chance of developing into a tropical depression over the coming days as it tracks slowly across the tropical Atlantic. Neither system poses an immediate threat to Florida or the broader U.S. mainland at this time, and computer forecast models still show high uncertainty regarding the eventual path of Invest 96L.</p>

  <h3 id="normalcy-bias-and-optimism">Normalcy Bias and Optimism</h3>
  <p>One of the strongest psychological hurdles during hurricane season is known as normalcy bias. This is our tendency to believe that because things have functioned normally in the past, they will continue to do so, and that disaster will always happen to someone else. Even though forecasts from NOAA and Colorado State University predict a below-average hurricane season overall due to strong El Niño conditions bringing hostile wind shear, meteorologists continually warn that averages matter very little when a single storm impacts your specific neighborhood.</p>
