  <section id="introduction">
    <p>The recent dismantling of a major cocaine distribution ring at Penn State, involving 14 individuals and two fraternities, has sent shockwaves through the university community. With allegations that drug packaging served as a form of "pledge indoctrination," the story highlights a dark intersection between student life and organized crime. While modern headlines focus on the specific arrests of Agostino Abbatiello and his associates, history suggests that the allure of "secret" fraternal subcultures often creates environments where accountability dissolves.</p>

  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>The Anatomy of the Penn State Ring</h2>
    <p>Pennsylvania Attorney General Dave Sunday detailed a coordinated operation where students allegedly made regular trips to New York and Philadelphia to procure large quantities of cocaine. The drugs were then processed inside off-campus fraternity houses. The involvement of Delta Upsilon and the now-unrecognized Sigma Chi chapter highlights a deep-seated issue of institutional control.</p>

    <h2>Echoes of the Past: Secret Societies and Outsider Norms</h2>
    <p>Historically, the "fraternal" model—defined by exclusivity, secret rituals, and intense loyalty—has often struggled with the tension between legitimate social bonding and the formation of insular, law-breaking subcultures. In the 19th-century American university system, secret societies were frequently viewed with suspicion by faculty, not just for their social cliques, but for their tendency to create "a state within a state."</p>
