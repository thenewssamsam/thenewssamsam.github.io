<h1 id="top">Tropical Storm Lala Batters Hawaii: What a Parenting Lens Reveals About Preparing Kids for Disaster</h1>

<p>When rare severe weather strikes an island community, the immediate headlines focus on wind speeds, power grids, and flooded roads. But behind every statistic of 165,000 darkened homes and isolated, bridge-cut communities in Hawaii lies a quieter, equally urgent challenge: how families and children process the sudden disruption of their safe spaces.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened in Hawaii</h2>
<p>Tropical Storm Lala brought intense flooding, strong winds, and widespread destruction across the Hawaiian Islands over the weekend, packing hurricane-force wind gusts in Hawaii, Honolulu, and Maui counties. According to Hawaii Gov. Josh Green, at least 100 homes were damaged or destroyed, and approximately 165,000 customers remained without power as the storm began pulling away from Kauai on Sunday night.</p>

In response, state and county offices, schools, and courts were closed for Monday, while emergency crews scrambled to rescue residents from rising waters—including an elderly woman pulled from a tree after her house washed away.</p>

<h2 id="parenting-perspective">A Parenting Perspective: Helping Children Through Climate Crises</h2>
<p>For parents across the Hawaiian islands, surviving a storm like Lala is only the first hurdle. The second, more delicate task is helping children make sense of the chaos. When a child watches a familiar street turn into a rushing river or hears the terrifying howl of wind against the windows, their foundational sense of safety is momentarily shaken.</p>
<p>Child development experts emphasize that children take their emotional cues from the adults around them. In the wake of natural disasters, the goal is not to pretend bad things do not happen, but to provide a grounded, reassuring presence that balances honesty with stability.</p>

<div class="ncg-quote-box">
<p>&ldquo;Children do not need false guarantees that storms will never happen; they need steady adults who show them how communities pull together when they do.&rdquo;</p>
</div>

<p>Here are a few practical, age-banded ways parents can navigate severe weather recovery and post-disaster anxiety with their children:</p>

<h3>For Younger Children (Ages 3–7)</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Keep explanations simple and concrete:</strong> Focus on what helpers are doing right now. Point out firefighters, utility crews, and neighbors sharing supplies.</li>
  <li><strong>Limit media exposure:</strong> Repeatedly watching looping footage of flooded homes and collapsed bridges can trick a young brain into believing the danger is happening over and over again.</li>
  <li><strong>Maintain comforting routines:</strong> When power is out and schedules are scrambled, simple rituals like a flashlight story hour or a favorite board game help restore a sense of normal predictability.</li>
</ul>

<h3>For Older Children and Teens (Ages 8–17)</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Invite open questions:</strong> Ask them what they saw, heard, or worry about. Listen to their concerns without immediately dismissing them with a platitude.</li>
  <li><strong>Involve them in recovery:</strong> Give older kids age-appropriate tasks, such as helping sort emergency supplies, writing thank-you notes to first responders, or checking on elderly neighbors. Action is a powerful antidote to anxiety.</li>
  <li><strong>Discuss the science calmly:</strong> Talk about why storms form and how modern forecasting and emergency response protect communities, shifting the focus from fear to resilience.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="how-families-respond">How Families Can Build Everyday Resilience</h2>
<p>Disasters like Tropical Storm Lala remind us how quickly everyday conveniences can vanish. Turning an emergency into a shared family learning experience builds long-term confidence rather than lingering panic.</p>
<p>Review your family disaster plan together once power is restored. Let children help pack an emergency kit with a few comfort items of their choice. When kids feel they have a role to play in the family&apos;s safety plan, fear transforms into preparedness.</p>

<h2 id="closing-thought">One Last Thought</h2>
<p>Storms will always test our infrastructure, but they also test our internal fortitude. As Hawaii begins the massive cleanup effort ahead, the resilience shown by neighbors rescuing one another speaks louder than any weather report. By walking alongside our children with patience and honest reassurance, we teach them that even when the power goes out, human care and community resolve remain fully lit. Preparedness is not about eliminating every risk; it is about trusting our capacity to face them together.</p>
