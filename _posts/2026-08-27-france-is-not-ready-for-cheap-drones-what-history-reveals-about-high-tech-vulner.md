<section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: France Confronts the Drone Reality</h2>
    <p>When France's chief of defense staff, General Fabien Mandon, stood before the country's business lobby Medef this week, he delivered a sobering assessment of modern military readiness. France, he warned, is not yet prepared to face the relentless, low-cost threat of mass-produced drones. In a conflict landscape reshaped by the war in Ukraine, Western militaries are grappling with a brutal economic mismatch: firing million-dollar interceptor missiles at unmanned aerial vehicles that cost barely tens of thousands of dollars to build.</p>
    
    <p>"We have enemies who have developed a large number of low-cost solutions that will wear down our models," General Mandon told the audience. While emphasizing that there is no immediate catastrophe and that the nation remains at peace, he stressed that the "cost per kill" equation must change urgently. Legacy defense contractors and agile startups are scrambling to design cheaper interceptors, but as the general noted, current defensive arsenals simply lack the production capacity to withstand waves of saturation attacks.</p>

    <h2>A Historical Perspective: The High Cost of Sophistication</h2>
    <p>From a historical standpoint, General Mandon’s warning is far from unprecedented. Throughout military history, dominant powers have repeatedly fallen into the trap of over-engineering expensive solutions, only to be outmaneuvered by adversaries wielding cheaper, highly adaptable mass-produced alternatives.</p>

Yet, empires frequently struggled when confronted by mobile, decentralized tribal forces whose weapons were cheap to manufacture and easily replaced. The lesson was simple: complexity creates brittleness.</p>

    <h3>The Armor-Piercing Evolution of the Middle Ages</h3>
    <p>A more striking parallel exists in the 14th century, when heavily armored knights represented the ultimate high-tech weapon system of feudal Europe—costly, elite, and seemingly invincible. The introduction of the longbow and early crude gunpowder weapons shattered that monopoly. A peasant archer could be trained in weeks and equipped at minimal cost, yet could neutralize a knight whose armor and horse represented a small fortune. Western defense ministries today find themselves cast in the role of the medieval knight: heavily armored, incredibly sophisticated, and economically vulnerable to a swarm of low-cost equivalents.</p>

<section id="how-readers-respond">
    <h2>How Should Readers Respond?</h2>
    <p>While an ordinary citizen cannot redesign national defense procurement policies, General Mandon’s warning carries broader lessons for how we navigate rapid technological and economic disruption in our own lives:</p>
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Audit Your Dependencies:</strong> Just as militaries are overly dependent on high-cost solutions, examine whether your personal or professional life relies too heavily on fragile, expensive systems that could be disrupted by leaner alternatives.</li>
        <li><strong>Value Agility Over Scale:</strong> Appreciate the value of adaptability. In business and personal planning, the ability to pivot quickly often outweighs massive upfront investments in rigid structures.</li>
        <li><strong>Support Realistic Discussions:</strong> Encourage honest, transparent dialogue about risks rather than assuming institutional perfection. Acknowledging vulnerability is the first step toward genuine resilience.</li>
    </ul>
