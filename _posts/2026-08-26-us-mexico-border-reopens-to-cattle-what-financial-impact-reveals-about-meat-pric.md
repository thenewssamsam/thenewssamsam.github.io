  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened at the Border</h2>
    <p>On Monday, a milestone crossing unfolded at the port of Douglas, Arizona, as nearly 700 head of cattle streamed through the 30-foot border wall from Sonora, Mexico. This movement marked the first legal cattle import across the southern border in more than a year. The closure had been enacted to prevent the northward migration of the New World screwworm, a destructive, flesh-eating parasite that burrows into warm-blooded animals and poses a severe threat to livestock populations.</p>

    <p>According to Alan Huddleston, chief veterinary officer at the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service, the reopening serves as a vital "proof of concept." Animals underwent rigorous screening, including examinations by specially trained screwworm-sniffing dogs, to ensure a negligible risk of parasite transmission. Gerardo Guerrero Gonzalez of Mexico's agriculture ministry emphasized that binational cooperation remains fundamental to controlling the outbreak while safely resuming trade.</p>

    <h2>The Financial Mechanics of Cattle Imports</h2>
    <p>To understand why this border reopening matters beyond veterinary circles, we must examine the basic economic mechanics of the North American beef supply chain. For the past year and a half, the absence of Mexican feeder cattle created a severe supply constraint in the U.S. market. At the same time, domestic U.S. cattle herds have hovered near multi-decade historical lows due to persistent drought conditions, high feed costs, and herd liquidation in prior years.</p>
