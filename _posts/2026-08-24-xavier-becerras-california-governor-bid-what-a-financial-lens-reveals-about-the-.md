  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Campaign Meets the Balance Sheet</h2>
  
  <p>Democrat Xavier Becerra enters the race for California governor with a formidable resume, pointing directly to his decades-long work helping pass, defend, and expand the Affordable Care Act (ACA). During his tenure under the Biden administration, national health insurance coverage reached a historic high of 92%, insuring roughly 310 million Americans. Polling currently suggests Becerra is well-positioned in his matchup against Republican opponent Steve Hilton, a former Fox News commentator.</p>

  <p>However, the political winds in Washington have shifted dramatically. Following the passage of the federal One Big Beautiful Bill Act and the expiration of enhanced Obamacare premium tax credits, California faces the rollback of billions in federal healthcare support. According to a May analysis by the University of California, Berkeley Labor Center, the number of uninsured Californians under 65 is projected to nearly double from 2.4 million to 4.6 million by 2030.</p>

  <p>While Becerra has pledged to issue an executive order to keep affected residents insured—suggesting the state can extract waste from administrative overhead like "attorneys, accountants, pencil pushers"—he has yet to detail how California will backfill as much as $30 billion in annual federal funding.</p>

  <h3>The Mechanics of Premium Spirals</h3>
  <p>Insurance markets rely fundamentally on risk pools—the balancing of healthy enrollees against sicker, higher-cost patients. When rising premiums price out younger, healthier consumers—such as 28-year-old Eric Maciel, who found an $800 monthly plan too steep and now avoids pickup sports to prevent injury—the average risk pool grows sicker and more expensive to cover. To compensate, health plans inevitably raise premiums further, creating a vicious cycle of adverse selection.</p>

  <h3>Uncompensated Care and Job Losses</h3>
  <p>When millions lose coverage, they do not stop getting sick; they simply stop paying upfront for preventive care. They delay treatment until conditions become acute, forcing them into emergency rooms. Hospitals absorb these mounting unpaid medical bills as uncompensated care, which ultimately drives up costs for everyone else.</p>
