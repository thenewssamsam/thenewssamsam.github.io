  <section id="what-happened" class="ncg-section">
    <h2>What Happened: The $20 Billion Retaliation</h2>
    <p>The economic relationship between the United States and Canada has entered turbulent waters. Following failed negotiations and the Trump administration's announcement of 50% tariffs on Canadian products—including planned duties on automotive and steel imports set for early 2027—Canadian officials have struck back. Canada announced retaliatory import duties of up to 50% on roughly $20 billion worth of American goods.</p>
    
    <p>According to Canadian industry minister Melanie Joly, the measures are explicitly designed to exert political and economic pressure on the United States by targeting key regions. The tariffs will apply to more than 800 types of U.S. products, spanning dairy, seafood, appliances, steel, aluminum, and manufacturing tools, with implementation scheduled ahead of the U.S. general election.</p>

    <div class="ncg-quick-take">
      <h3>Quick Take</h3>
      <p>Canada buys approximately one-tenth of all U.S. exports. By focusing import duties on products from politically sensitive swing states and heavy manufacturing hubs like Michigan, Indiana, and Wisconsin, Ottawa aims to maximize economic leverage.</p>

    <h2>The Financial Impact: Who Pays the Bill?</h2>
    <p>When governments impose tariffs, the common misconception is that the exporting country pays the tax. In financial reality, tariffs act as import taxes collected at the border, meaning the cost is ultimately absorbed by domestic importers, retailers, and consumers. As these new duties take effect, the ripple effects will be felt across household budgets, manufacturing supply chains, and broader market indices.</p>

    <h3>Targeting Manufacturing and Regional Supply Chains</h3>
    <p>States with robust manufacturing and automotive sectors—particularly Michigan and Indiana—are positioned to absorb a significant blow. Because Canada is the top export destination for 27 U.S. states, the application of 15%, 25%, and 50% tariffs on goods ranging from tractor-trailers to auto parts threatens tightly integrated cross-border supply chains. For example, the auto industry relies on parts moving fluidly across the Ontario-U.S. border; adding friction and tax burdens to this pipeline increases production costs for vehicles.</p>

    <h3>The Inflationary Feedback Loop</h3>
    <p>Economic analysis from firms like Oxford Economics highlights that trade friction is a two-way street. Because these tariffs function essentially as sales taxes, they carry domestic consequences for Canada as well. Economists project that the import duties could push up Canadian inflation by 0.3 percentage points while creating an equal drag on economic growth. When goods become artificially more expensive, consumers alter purchasing habits, companies face compressed profit margins, and overall market efficiency declines.</p>
