<section id="what-happened">
<h2>What Happened: The Myth of the Completely Closed Strait</h2>
<p>Before the current conflict involving the U.S., Israel, and Iran escalated, roughly 20 million barrels of oil traversed the narrow Strait of Hormuz daily. Tehran has consistently maintained that the waterway is completely closed and under its military control. However, data and statements from U.S. officials and energy analytics firms tell a different story.</p>
<p>Following weeks of targeted U.S. bombing campaigns that degraded Iranian radar and maritime surveillance infrastructure, a clandestine corridor opened up along Oman's coastline. Energy intelligence firms like Vortexa estimate that recent daily averages hover between 6 million and 7 million barrels, with peak moving averages nearing 10 million barrels and single-day spikes hitting 14 million. Tankers are slipping through at night with their transponders turned off, making shuttle runs to transfer crude to waiting vessels. While a severe supply deficit remains and consuming nations continue to deplete strategic reserves, enough oil is leaking out to prevent an immediate global price explosion.</p>

<h2>Financial Realities and the "Managed Disruption" Equilibrium</h2>
<p>In global commodities, absolute blockades are extraordinarily difficult to maintain when the economic incentive to smuggle or bypass restrictions runs into billions of dollars. Dan Alamariu, chief geopolitical strategist at Alpine Macro, recently highlighted that these leaking barrels are changing the fundamental timeline of the war. When oil keeps moving, neither side feels an immediate, crushing economic urgency.</p>

<h2>Price Triggers and Political Thresholds</h2>
<p>The financial math governing this conflict has clear, measurable thresholds. Analysts point out that as long as Brent crude stays below $90 to $100 per barrel, the U.S. administration may maintain its current economic pressure strategy without launching a massive new military campaign. However, if prices break past $105 to $110, domestic inflation and soaring gas prices will dramatically shift the political calculus.</p>
