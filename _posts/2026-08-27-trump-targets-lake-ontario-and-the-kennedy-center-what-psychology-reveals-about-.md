  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened: Maps, Monuments, and Trade Wars</h2>
    <p>President Donald Trump recently made headlines with a pair of unexpected proposals: threatening to demolish the historic John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts if ongoing renovation disputes persist, and posting maps proposing to rename Lake Ontario as "Lake America." These declarations emerged against a backdrop of stubborn economic pressures, upcoming midterm elections, and stalled negotiations in ongoing trade and foreign policy conflicts.</p>
    
    <p>To critics and foreign allies alike, the announcements appeared outlandish, even bizarre. Yet, far from being isolated flukes, these maneuvers fit neatly into a broader, recurring pattern of communication that has defined Trump’s political career. When legal hurdles block his construction plans or trade partners refuse to bow to sweeping tariff demands, the administration shifts the battlefield from traditional policy negotiation to symbolic spectacle.</p>
  </section>

    <h3>2. The Illusion of Control Under Stress</h3>
    <p>Psychologists note that when individuals or leaders face institutional resistance—such as federal judges ruling against executive renovations or foreign nations rejecting trade demands—they often experience a subconscious sense of impotence. Escalating the rhetoric with grandiose counter-threats is a classic ego-defense mechanism. It projects absolute strength and control precisely where vulnerability is highest.</p>

    <h3>3. Us Versus Them and Group Polarization</h3>
    <p>Political spectacle is inherently tribal. Outraging institutional establishments while delighting a populist base creates a powerful in-group cohesion. Supporters interpret the institutional backlash as proof that the leader is actively fighting entrenched elites. Meanwhile, opponents grow even more entrenched in their opposition. The result is deep societal polarization driven by emotional engagement rather than policy debate.</p>
