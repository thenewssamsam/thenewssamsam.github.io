  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The 2026 Colorado River Cuts</h2>
  
  <p>The Arizona Reconsultation Committee met on August 24, 2026, to review the federal framework designed to stave off a catastrophic crash at Lake Mead and Lake Powell. While Arizona water officials accepted the two-year reduction of 760,000 acre-feet per year to maintain stability, they strongly pushed back against a broader 10-year framework that could double those cuts to 3 million acre-feet across the Lower Basin states of Arizona, California, and Nevada.</p>

  <p>The cuts fall disproportionately on the Central Arizona Project (CAP), which supplies over a third of the tap water for metro Phoenix and vital irrigation for agriculture and tribal lands. State officials argue that imposing these sweeping reductions without adequate federal backing or equitable contributions from Upper Basin states threatens to destabilize regional economies. Meanwhile, Nevada has already filed a lawsuit against the federal government, arguing that Washington failed to analyze the severe economic fallout such reductions would inflict on municipal ratepayers and local businesses.</p>

  <h2 id="financial-impact">The Financial Mechanics: Who Pays for the Pipelines?</h2>

  <p>From a purely financial perspective, municipal water management is an expensive capital expenditure game. When surface water supplies shrink, cities cannot simply turn on a faucet; they must pivot to pumping stored groundwater, constructing new water treatment plants, and laying miles of high-capacity transmission pipes. Max Wilson, Phoenix's water policy adviser, warned that adapting to these lower river allocations will require billions of dollars in public infrastructure.</p>
