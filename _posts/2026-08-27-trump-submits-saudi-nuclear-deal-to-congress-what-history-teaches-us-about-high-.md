<p>When high-stakes geopolitics collides with domestic legislative oversight, the echoes of past diplomatic gambits inevitably ring out. President Donald Trump has officially submitted a classified civil nuclear agreement with Saudi Arabia to Congress for review, setting the clock ticking on a 90-day window under the Atomic Energy Act. Yet, even as the document sits on Capitol Hill, a major caveat remains: the administration insists the pact will not move forward unless Riyadh normalizes relations with Israel.</p>

<p>For casual observers, it reads like classic modern horse-trading. But history suggests that tying massive technological and energy pacts to regional political alignment is a delicate tightrope walk—one that has repeatedly tested the patience of empires, allies, and lawmakers alike.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Nuclear Agreement Lands on Capitol Hill</h2>

<p>According to sources speaking to the Associated Press, the classified civil nuclear agreement was delivered to Capitol Hill on Monday. The pact was initially signed in Washington by U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright and his Saudi counterpart. However, the diplomatic landscape shifted almost immediately when President Trump announced a strict prerequisite: Saudi Arabia must join the Abraham Accords and normalize diplomatic ties with Israel for the deal to cross the finish line.</p>

<p>This unusual move places both the White House and Congress in a complex procedural maze. Under the statutory rules governing what are commonly known as "123 agreements," Congress has 90 days in session to review the text. Unless lawmakers pass a joint resolution of disapproval, the agreement can take effect without a formal floor vote by either chamber. Adding to the intrigue, the entire contents of this specific agreement have been classified at an unusually high level—diverging from previous civilian nuclear pacts that were largely made public with only minor classified annexes.</p>
