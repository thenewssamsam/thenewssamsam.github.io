  <section id="what-happened">
    <h2>What Happened in the Florida Primary</h2>
    <p>Florida voters are casting ballots Tuesday to select party nominees for governor to replace term-eligible Republican Ron DeSantis, as well as choosing contenders to complete the remainder of Marco Rubio’s unexpired U.S. Senate term. Alongside high-profile executive and senatorial contests, the state is holding its first congressional primaries under a redrawn district map designed to alter the state's legislative footprint.</p>
    <p>The gubernatorial race highlights striking intraparty dynamics on both sides. On the Republican ballot, 11 candidates are vying for the nomination, featuring prominent figures such as U.S. Rep. Byron Donalds, backed by Donald Trump, and Lt. Gov. Jay Collins, a close ally of DeSantis. Meanwhile, the Democratic primary features six contenders, including former U.S. Rep. David Jolly, navigating a complex path to rebuild the party’s statewide electoral standing after decades without a gubernatorial victory.</p>

  <section id="historical-perspective">
    <h2>A Historical Parallel: Eras of Political Realignment</h2>
    <p>When an American state undergoes simultaneous executive turnover, legislative redistricting, and major ideological shifts within both major parties, historians immediately look for structural echoes in the past. Florida's 2026 primary cycle is not an isolated flash in the pan; it mirrors periods of transition where rapidly growing states reorganize their political coalitions.</p>
    <p>Historically, dramatic shifts in legislative maps—such as those seen following post-census redistricting cycles in the mid-20th century—frequently trigger intense intraparty friction. When boundaries are redrawn, veteran lawmakers are forced into new configurations, opening the door for insurgent factions to challenge establishment favorites. We saw similar factional struggles during the party realignments of the late 1960s and 1970s, when southern states transitioned from one-party dominance into competitive two-party arenas.</p>
    
    <div class="ncg-quote-box">
      <p>"History shows that map-driven redistricting rarely settles a political landscape instantly; instead, it acts as a pressure valve, releasing years of demographic and ideological tension all at once."</p>
    </div>

    <p>Every historical comparison, however, carries distinct limits. Unlike past decades marked by slow demographic shifts, today's electorate moves at the speed of digital media and mail-in voting. With nearly three-quarters of primary votes cast early or via absentee ballots in recent cycles, the operational reality of modern elections means that the outcome is often effectively decided before Election Day even begins.</p>
  </section>

  <section id="how-voters-should-respond">
    <h2>How Voters Should Respond</h2>
    <p>Elections defined by massive structural changes and crowded fields can easily induce voter fatigue or apathy. To navigate these high-stakes primaries thoughtfully, citizens can take a few concrete steps:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Look beyond the headlines:</strong> Focus on down-ballot races, including state legislative and local offices, where individual votes carry direct, measurable weight in community governance.</li>
      <li><strong>Understand the rules:</strong> Remember Florida’s closed primary system—only voters registered with a specific party may participate in that party's nominating contests.</li>
      <li><strong>Track the mechanics:</strong> Familiarize yourself with how your county reports early and mail-in ballots so you can interpret election night results with realistic expectations.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section id="faq">
    <h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>When do polls close in the Florida primary?</summary>
      <p>Polls close at 7 p.m. local time across the majority of the state in the Eastern time zone, and at 8 p.m. ET in counties located in the Central time zone.</p>
    </details>
    <details class="ncg-faq-item">
      <summary>Can unaffiliated or independent voters participate in the primary?</summary>
      <p>No. Florida operates closed primaries, meaning only voters registered with a specific political party can vote in that party's primary election.</p>
    </details>
  </section>
