    <section id="what-happened" style="margin-bottom: 32px;">
        <h2 style="font-size: 1.5rem; font-weight: 700; color: #1f2937; margin-bottom: 12px; border-left: 4px solid #7c3aed; padding-left: 12px;">What Happened: The Approach of Tropical Storm Moke</h2>
        <p>The National Hurricane Center issued its first advisory on Tropical Depression Two-C on Thursday morning, placing the system roughly 1,000 miles east-southeast of the Big Island, moving west-northwest. Forecasters expect the system to intensify into Tropical Storm Moke as it enters a more favorable environment.</p>
        
        <p>While wind shear may keep its intensity capped as a tropical storm or a weak Category 1 hurricane, the primary hazard remains the sheer volume of water. According to the National Hurricane Center, Moke could dump 5 to 15 inches of rain on the Big Island—particularly over mountains and eastern slopes—with other islands seeing 1 to 5 inches. This comes dangerously close on the heels of Hurricane Lala, which left over 100 Big Island homes damaged and saturated soils that are ill-equipped to absorb another deluge.</p>

        <h3 style="font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; color: #374151; margin-top: 16px; margin-bottom: 8px;">Normalcy Bias Meets Exhaustion</h3>
        <p>After Hurricane Lala, many residents were just beginning the arduous cleanup process. The arrival of Moke triggers what psychologists call normalcy bias running on empty—the psychological defensive mechanism where people underestimate the likelihood of a disaster, or its implications, because they simply want life to return to normal. When a second threat hits before recovery finishes, it shatters the illusion of safety, often leading to acute anxiety or complete emotional numbness.</p>

        <h3 style="font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; color: #374151; margin-top: 16px; margin-bottom: 8px;">Learned Helplessness vs. Actionable Agency</h3>
        <p>Repeated stress can sometimes foster a sense of learned helplessness, where individuals feel powerless to protect their homes and livelihoods against relentless weather patterns. However, behavioral science shows that reclaiming small points of control—such as organizing emergency documents, checking on vulnerable neighbors, or preparing go-bags—actively combats this helplessness and restores cognitive stability.</p>
