<h1 class="ncg-title">Indiana Gov. Mike Braun Blasts NIPSCO: What the Outage Crisis Reveals About Infrastructure and Monopoly Power</h1>
</header>

<p class="ncg-intro">Nearly two weeks after a destructive derecho battered northwestern Indiana with 110-mph winds, more than 20,000 customers remain without power. Governor Mike Braun has publicly condemned utility provider NIPSCO for unacceptable delays, highlighting a critical tension between guaranteed regional monopolies and their fundamental obligations to local ratepayers.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">When catastrophic weather strikes, communities expect their utilities to respond with urgency. But when the lights stay off for two weeks, economic losses mount, perishable goods spoil, and the structural vulnerabilities of regional utility monopolies are laid bare for everyone to see.</p>

<h2 id="what-happened" class="ncg-section-heading">What Happened: A Historic Storm and a Slow Recovery</h2>

<p class="ncg-p">The crisis began with a severe derecho on August 11, packing winds up to 110 miles per hour that shattered utility poles, tore down transmission lines, and knocked out power to over 60% of Northern Indiana Public Service Company's (NIPSCO) customers. In the immediate aftermath, more than 350,000 customers lost electricity.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">While NIPSCO crews have worked around the clock on what the company describes as the worst infrastructure damage in its history, progress has plateaued for thousands of households. As of Sunday, over 21,000 customers were still dark. Communities like Munster, Gary, and Portage face restoration estimates stretching deep into a second week.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">Frustration boiled over when Indiana Governor Mike Braun took to social media to blast the utility's handling of the crisis. Pointing out that NIPSCO possesses ample resources, Braun argued that the company has failed the Hoosiers who rely on its services for safety and comfort.</p>

<h2 id="financial-perspective" class="ncg-section-heading">The Financial Impact of Utility Monopolies</h2>

<p class="ncg-p">From an economic perspective, a utility outage of this magnitude is not merely an operational headache; it is a major financial shock to a captive consumer base. NIPSCO operates as a regional monopoly. Ratepayers cannot simply switch providers if they are unhappy with customer service or storm response times. They are legally and geographically bound to the utility.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">This monopoly model creates a distinct economic imbalance. In competitive markets, poor service leads directly to lost revenue as customers take their business elsewhere. In regulated utilities, however, the financial risk is largely absorbed by the consumer. Ratepayers fund the infrastructure through monthly bills, yet they bear the direct financial brunt—spoiled groceries, missed wages, hotel costs, and damaged property—when that infrastructure fails under stress.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">Situations involving massive power imbalances naturally invite moral reflection on stewardship and the responsibilities of leadership. When large institutions hold unchecked authority over basic human needs like light, heat, and safety, they carry a profound ethical duty to those they serve.</p>

<p class="ncg-p">Proverbs 29:4 tells us, <em>"By justice a king gives stability, but the person who exacts gifts ruins it."</em> Similarly, Luke 12:48 reminds us that, <em>"Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required."</em> A monopoly granted by the public trust comes with heavy accountability. When systems fail ordinary people, integrity demands transparent communication, relentless effort, and an honest reckoning with how resources are managed before disaster strikes.</p>
