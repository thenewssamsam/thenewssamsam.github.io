  <h2 id="what-happened">What Happened: The Evidence in Court</h2>
  
  <p>The trial centers on events from January 2023, when Patrick Clancy returned home from picking up dinner to find his three children—Cora, five, Dawson, three, and eight-month-old Callan—dead, and his wife Lindsay severely injured from an apparent suicide attempt. Lindsay Clancy has pleaded not guilty to murder charges, with her defence attorneys arguing that she was suffering from an extreme episode of postpartum psychosis at the time.</p>

  <p>Testimony from family members and medical experts has painted a heartbreaking picture of a mother whose mind was rapidly deteriorating. Just a month prior to the tragedy, Clancy had gone to her mother and then-husband, telling them she was having "thoughts of harming the children." Her mother, Paula Musgrove, testified that Lindsay would plead, "'This isn't me, I've never been like this before.'" Forensic psychologist Paul Zeizel testified that Clancy was "unable to conform her behaviour to the rule of law and she had no appreciation for the wrongfulness of her act."</p>

  <p>Conversely, prosecutors argue that the killings were calculated and intentional. Yet, the testimony from her husband, Patrick, highlighted the terrifying unpredictability of the condition: on the very day of the tragedy, he noted she had "one of her best days," playing outside with the children and building a snowman. This abrupt, light-switch shift from seeming stability to absolute delusion is a hallmark of the rare condition experts are now desperate to bring into public view.</p>

  <h2 id="understanding-illness">Understanding Postpartum Psychosis: A Medical Blind Spot</h2>

  <p>Postpartum psychosis is among the most severe psychiatric conditions a woman can experience after childbirth, characterized by hallucinations, delusions, insomnia, and a delirium-like state. Survivors like Meghan Cliffel, who experienced terrifying delusions in 2015 following the birth of her second daughter, describe the illness as a terrifying descent into unreality that requires immediate emergency care.</p>

  <p>Despite its severity, medical experts point out a startling institutional barrier: postpartum psychosis remains unclassified in the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual, the DSM-5. Without a formal listing in this psychiatric handbook, the condition receives limited attention in medical school textbooks, struggles to secure adequate research funding, and faces persistent insurance coverage hurdles. Advocates and survivors argue that official recognition is the first vital step toward building a comprehensive care system that protects mothers before tragedy strikes.</p>

  <h2 id="practical-response">How Should Christians Respond?</h2>

  <p>Recognizing the reality of postpartum mental health crises calls faith communities to active, compassionate engagement rather than silent judgment:</p>
  
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Support New Mothers Proactively:</strong> Check in deeply on postpartum mothers. Ask specific questions about their sleep, emotional well-being, and mental health rather than just offering polite pleasantries.</li>
    <li><strong>Remove the Stigma of Mental Health:</strong> Create church environments where struggling parents feel safe admitting they are overwhelmed, experiencing intrusive thoughts, or needing professional medical intervention.</li>
    <li><strong>Advocate for Better Care Systems:</strong> Support initiatives that expand education, research, and insurance coverage for maternal mental health care, ensuring no mother slips through the cracks of the medical system.</li>
  </ul>
